This question bank follows the structure of the combined **RES 1B** and **RES 2B** study guides. It is designed for active recall and exam preparation rather than as a substitute for the current legislation, MAS notices, SGX rules or firm procedures. The source guide states that each module is assessed by 60 multiple-choice questions in 1 hour 30 minutes, with a 75% pass mark; the amount of penalties is not tested.

# Part I — RES 1B

## Chapter 1 — Capital markets industry and participants

### 1. [RES 1B §1.1] What is the difference between a primary market and a secondary market?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** In a primary market, an issuer raises new capital by issuing securities to investors. In a secondary market, investors trade securities already issued, usually with other investors rather than with the issuer.

**Rationale:** The distinction matters for understanding the issuer’s financing process, investor liquidity and the roles of intermediaries and trading venues.

</details>

### 2. [RES 1B §1.1] Distinguish exchange trading from over-the-counter trading.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Exchange trading takes place through a regulated venue with standardised products, published rules and organised matching. OTC trading is negotiated bilaterally or through a dealer network, allowing more flexible terms but creating greater counterparty, documentation, valuation and liquidity considerations.

</details>

### 3. [RES 1B §1.2] What are the principal functions of banks, securities dealers, fund managers and other institutional participants?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Banks provide financing, deposit-taking and payment services; securities dealers arrange or execute trades and may act as agent or principal; fund managers manage pooled or discretionary portfolios; and other participants such as insurers, pension funds, corporations and government-linked entities supply or demand capital and investment products.

</details>

### 4. [RES 1B §1.2] What is the role of a CMS licence holder in the capital markets?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A CMS licence holder conducts regulated activities such as dealing in capital markets products, fund management or advising, subject to the scope of its licence, financial resources, representatives, systems and continuing obligations. It must protect customers, maintain proper records and comply with applicable conduct and prudential requirements.

</details>

### 5. [RES 1B §1.3] Differentiate MAS, SGX, SGX RegCo, SGX-ST and CDP.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** MAS is Singapore’s central bank and financial regulator. SGX operates the exchange group; SGX RegCo performs frontline regulatory functions; SGX-ST operates the securities trading market; and CDP provides central depository and clearing-related services for securities. Their exact responsibilities should be read with the applicable legislation and rules.

</details>

### 6. [RES 1B §1.3] Why must a representative understand both legislation and exchange rules?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Legislation and MAS regulations establish statutory obligations and offences, while exchange and clearing rules govern trading, membership, settlement and market operations. A transaction can comply with one layer and still breach another, so representatives must apply the full rule framework.

</details>

### 7. [RES 1B §1.4] What are the main quantitative entry routes for a Mainboard listing described in the guide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide describes three routes: a profit test involving at least S$30 million of aggregate pre-tax profit over the latest three years with minimum profit in the latest year; a profit-and-market-capitalisation route involving profit over three years and at least S$150 million market capitalisation; and a revenue-and-market-capitalisation route involving revenue in the latest completed financial year and at least S$300 million market capitalisation. Exact eligibility must be checked against the current listing rules.

</details>

### 8. [RES 1B §1.4] How does Catalist differ from Mainboard in admission and continuing obligations?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Catalist is a sponsor-supervised market and does not use the same quantitative entry tests as Mainboard. A full sponsor assesses and supervises the issuer; the guide highlights public-shareholding and shareholder-spread requirements, an offer document and a stated period for listing. Mainboard issuers have direct SGX admission and continuing-listing requirements, while Catalist issuers rely more heavily on sponsor supervision.

</details>

### 9. [RES 1B §1.4] What is the significance of continuing listing obligations and SGXNet disclosure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Admission is not the end of the issuer’s obligations. The issuer must continue to meet requirements relating to disclosure, financial reporting, interested-person transactions, shareholder meetings and other corporate actions. Material information must be released through the prescribed channel, including SGXNet, so that investors receive information on an equal and timely basis.

</details>

### 10. [RES 1B §1.4] What is a SPAC and what investor-protection issues should a representative recognise?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A SPAC is a listed shell formed to raise funds and later acquire an operating business. Representatives should understand the conditions for listing, time limits and de-SPAC process, disclosure and shareholder-approval requirements, redemption or exit features, dilution and conflicts, and the risk that no suitable acquisition is completed.

</details>

## Chapter 2 — Licensing and business operations

### 11. [RES 1B §2.1–2.2] What is the purpose of a CMS licence and why is the activity scope important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A CMS licence authorises a legal entity to conduct specified regulated activities. The holder may conduct only the activities and deal in the products permitted by its licence, and must satisfy requirements on management, representatives, financial resources, systems, controls and customer protection.

</details>

### 12. [RES 1B §2.2] State the base-capital amounts highlighted for a securities dealer under the guide’s BCR table.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide’s table highlights S$5 million for a clearing member, S$1 million for a trading member or non-member, S$500,000 for an introducing broker and S$50,000 for other relevant categories. These are study-guide figures and must be checked against the current regulations before operational use.

</details>

### 13. [RES 1B §2.2] What management and staffing features are relevant to a CMS licence application?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide describes a board with at least two directors, including a Singapore-resident director, a Singapore-resident chief executive officer, and at least two full-time representatives for each regulated activity, subject to the applicable requirements. MAS also assesses fitness, propriety, competence, financial resources, compliance arrangements and internal controls.

</details>

### 14. [RES 1B §2.2] What kinds of changes must a CMS licence holder notify to MAS, and why?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Changes to key persons, business activities, ownership, representatives, offices, financial condition or other prescribed particulars may require notification or approval. The purpose is to let MAS assess whether the licence holder continues to meet the conditions under which it was authorised; the guide highlights a 14-day period for specified changes.

</details>

### 15. [RES 1B §2.3] What does registration of a representative achieve?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Registration identifies an individual authorised to act for a principal in specified regulated activities. The representative must meet competence and fit-and-proper requirements, act for only one principal at a time, remain properly supervised and comply with continuing professional-development and notification obligations.

</details>

### 16. [RES 1B §2.3] What are the representative’s obligations when changing particulars or ceasing to act for a principal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The representative and principal must make the prescribed notifications, including cessation and changes in particulars, within the applicable time periods; the guide highlights seven- and 14-day requirements for different changes. The representative must not continue holding out as authorised after cessation.

</details>

### 17. [RES 1B §2.4] What must a compliant advertisement for a capital-markets product or service avoid?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It must be clear, fair, balanced and not misleading. It should identify material risks and limitations, avoid false or unsubstantiated claims, avoid presenting past performance as a promise, state relevant fees or conditions, and comply with the firm’s approval and record-keeping procedures.

</details>

### 18. [RES 1B §2.5] When are a Customer Account Review and Customer Knowledge Assessment relevant?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** For retail customers trading SIPs, the firm uses a CAR for listed SIPs and a CKA for unlisted SIPs, unless an applicable exemption or alternative rule applies. The purpose is to assess knowledge and experience, not to guarantee suitability or eliminate investment risk.

</details>

### 19. [RES 1B §2.5] Compare the validity periods of CAR and CKA in the guide.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide states that CAR is valid for three years and CKA for one year, subject to the relevant transaction, change-of-circumstance and reassessment rules. Firms must keep evidence of the assessment, result, explanations and customer communications.

</details>

### 20. [RES 1B §2.5] What should happen when a customer fails a knowledge or experience assessment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** For a listed SIP, the guide allows a negative CAR outcome to be followed only by the prescribed senior-management approval, written outcome and warnings, customer confirmation and responsibility acknowledgement. A negative CKA generally prevents the customer from dealing in an unlisted SIP unless a stated exemption applies. The customer must not be pressured into proceeding.

</details>

### 21. [RES 1B §2.5] Why is the EIP/SIP classification important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The classification determines whether additional knowledge or experience safeguards apply. If a product is not an EIP or if the firm cannot properly classify it, it may have to treat it as a SIP and perform the relevant assessment. Outsourcing classification does not remove the CMS holder’s responsibility.

</details>

### 22. [RES 1B §2.6] What confidentiality principles apply to customer information?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Customer information must be collected, used, disclosed and retained only for proper purposes and in accordance with the Banking Act secrecy framework, PDPA obligations, applicable Do-Not-Call rules and permitted exceptions. Access should be limited to those with a legitimate need, with controls against unauthorised disclosure.

</details>

### 23. [RES 1B §2.7] What documents and controls should exist for a customer trading account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The firm should establish and verify customer identity, authority, risk profile, account terms, trading permissions, beneficial ownership and relevant declarations. It should record instructions, confirmations, statements, communications, limits, approvals and changes, and monitor the account for unauthorised activity, conflicts and unusual conduct.

</details>

### 24. [RES 1B §2.7] When should a securities contract note and customer statement be provided?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide highlights contract notes by the next business day and periodic statements, including monthly or quarterly statements where prescribed. The documents should accurately identify the transaction, price, quantity, charges, capacity and settlement information.

</details>

### 25. [RES 1B §2.8] Why are books and audit trails important, and what retention period is highlighted?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Books and audit trails let the firm reconstruct orders, decisions, communications, approvals, allocations, money movements and compliance reviews. The guide highlights retaining relevant records for five years, together with annual accounts within five months and auditor reporting requirements.

</details>

### 26. [RES 1B §2.9] What is the difference between customer money and customer assets?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Customer money is cash received or held for the customer, while customer assets include securities and other property held for or belonging to the customer. Both require segregation, proper records, permitted account or custody arrangements, reconciliations and controls against misuse.

</details>

### 27. [RES 1B §2.9] What are the key controls for handling customer money and assets?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Deposit or hold them only with permitted institutions or custodians, maintain trust or custody arrangements and accurate ledgers, complete reconciliations, make permitted withdrawals or investments, give required disclosures, and ensure lending, pledging or use of assets is authorised and documented. The guide highlights next-business-day handling requirements for relevant receipts.

</details>

## Chapter 3 — Market conduct

### 28. [RES 1B §3.1–3.2] Why can market-conduct provisions have extraterritorial reach?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Conduct outside Singapore can affect Singapore markets, investors or listed products. The SFA provisions may therefore apply where the conduct has the required Singapore connection or market impact; a representative cannot assume that offshore execution removes Singapore obligations.

</details>

### 29. [RES 1B §3.3] What is false trading or market rigging?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is conduct that creates or maintains a false or misleading appearance of active trading or market activity, or affects the price of a capital-markets product through artificial transactions. Examples include wash trades, matched orders or trades lacking genuine change of beneficial ownership.

</details>

### 30. [RES 1B §3.3] How can “hunting stops” become market misconduct?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Deliberately pushing a market price toward known stop orders to trigger them, without legitimate trading purpose and with an intention to create an artificial price or profit from the triggered orders, may amount to manipulation or false trading. The focus is on purpose, conduct, effect and the surrounding facts.

</details>

### 31. [RES 1B §3.4] What is securities-market manipulation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is conduct intended or likely to create an artificial price, maintain a price at an artificial level, or otherwise manipulate the market for securities. The analysis considers the transaction pattern, orders, information, intent and whether the activity distorts genuine supply and demand.

</details>

### 32. [RES 1B §3.5] Why are false or misleading statements and information prohibited?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Investors may trade on the information and the market may be distorted. The prohibition covers publishing or communicating materially false or misleading information, including information likely to affect price, where the relevant mental element and market connection are present.

</details>

### 33. [RES 1B §3.6] What is fraudulent inducement to deal in capital-markets products?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It involves using deception, concealment or other fraudulent conduct to induce another person to enter into or refrain from entering into a transaction. A representative must not make misleading claims, omit material facts or exploit a customer’s reliance to obtain a trade.

</details>

### 34. [RES 1B §3.7] What is the risk of using manipulative or deceptive devices even if no single false statement is made?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A scheme can be deceptive because of the overall method, timing, order pattern or concealment, even when no individual statement is obviously false. The rule captures conduct designed to defraud, deceive or manipulate and requires representatives to assess the substance and purpose of a strategy.

</details>

### 35. [RES 1B §3.8] Why is disseminating information about illegal transactions prohibited?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Repeating or distributing information about an illegal transaction can encourage further trading, amplify the artificial price or help the wrongdoer profit. A person should not communicate or publish information that promotes or supports prohibited conduct.

</details>

### 36. [RES 1B §3.9] What are the core elements of insider trading?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The person possesses information that is not generally available and that would be likely to materially affect the price or value of the product, and deals or communicates the information in circumstances prohibited by the SFA. The guide distinguishes connected and non-connected persons and explains statutory exceptions or defences.

</details>

### 37. [RES 1B §3.9] What is “inside information” in practical terms?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is specific information that is not generally available and would be material to an investor’s decision or likely to affect price or value. Rumour, incomplete information or information held inside a firm is not automatically safe to use; the representative must consider whether it is public, precise and material.

</details>

### 38. [RES 1B §3.10] What is securities hawking?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Securities hawking is the prohibited solicitation or offering of securities in circumstances covered by the SFA’s hawking provisions. Representatives should use approved channels and materials, respect customer contact rules and avoid unsolicited pressure or misleading sales tactics.

</details>

### 39. [RES 1B §3.11] What are short-selling controls highlighted in the guide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A seller must identify and mark a sale correctly, comply with short-position reporting requirements where applicable, and not misrepresent ownership or availability. Borrowing or lending arrangements should meet applicable requirements, including the guide’s reference to full collateralisation for relevant securities-lending arrangements.

</details>

### 40. [RES 1B §3.12] What is the exam relevance of penalties when the amount is not tested?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Candidates should understand that market misconduct can lead to criminal, civil, regulatory, disciplinary, reputational and financial consequences, and that different offences have different consequences. The guide says the amount of penalties is not tested, so focus on the prohibited conduct and consequence categories rather than memorising figures.

</details>

## Chapter 4 — Ethics and professional standards

### 41. [RES 1B §4.1–4.2] How are legal compliance and ethical conduct related?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Law sets minimum enforceable standards; ethics also asks what is fair, honest, prudent and appropriate when a rule does not give a complete answer. A decision can be technically legal yet inconsistent with professional integrity, customer trust or the firm’s code.

</details>

### 42. [RES 1B §4.3] What makes an ethical dilemma difficult?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It involves competing duties, incentives, interests or values, such as customer loyalty versus revenue pressure, confidentiality versus reporting, or speed versus adequate diligence. The representative should identify the facts, stakeholders, duties, conflicts and consequences rather than choosing based only on convenience.

</details>

### 43. [RES 1B §4.4] What do the professional standards require regarding competence and diligence?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A representative must maintain the knowledge and skills needed for the service, understand the product and risks, conduct reasonable investigation, use reliable information, keep appropriate records and act promptly and carefully. Lack of knowledge is a reason to escalate or decline, not to improvise.

</details>

### 44. [RES 1B §4.4] What is the duty concerning misrepresentation and performance information?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Do not misrepresent qualifications, services, products, risks, returns, guarantees or historical performance. Performance information should be fair, balanced, supported by records and accompanied by meaningful assumptions, comparisons, fees, risks and limitations.

</details>

### 45. [RES 1B §4.4] How should a representative handle material non-public information?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Do not trade, recommend or communicate it improperly. Preserve confidentiality, restrict access, follow watch-list or restricted-list procedures, escalate to compliance and wait until the information is properly public or compliance authorises an action under an applicable exception.

</details>

### 46. [RES 1B §4.4] What does fair dealing require?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Treat customers fairly and consistently, allocate opportunities and orders using legitimate criteria, do not favour one customer improperly, and communicate material information honestly. Fair dealing does not mean identical outcomes; it means a fair process consistent with disclosed terms and duties.

</details>

### 47. [RES 1B §4.4] What are the standards for suitability and customer interest?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Understand the customer’s objectives, finances, knowledge, experience, risk tolerance and constraints before making a recommendation. The recommendation should have a reasonable basis, fit the customer’s circumstances, explain material risks and not be driven by the representative’s compensation.

</details>

### 48. [RES 1B §4.4] How should conflicts, gifts, referral fees and outside interests be managed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Identify conflicts early, disclose material conflicts where appropriate, obtain approvals, segregate duties or decline to act when controls are insufficient. Benefits or referral fees must not distort advice, order priority, product selection or customer treatment.

</details>

### 49. [RES 1B §4.4] What is the representative’s duty to the employer and to the profession?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Follow lawful firm procedures, protect confidential information and assets, supervise or escalate appropriately, cooperate with compliance and avoid conduct that damages market integrity. The duty to the employer does not justify breaking the law or harming customers.

</details>

### 50. [RES 1B §4.5] State a practical six-step framework for resolving an ethical dilemma.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Identify the facts and dilemma; identify stakeholders and duties; generate realistic options; test the options against law, policy, professional standards and consequences; seek advice or escalate; then decide, document, act and review the outcome. The precise labels may differ, but the framework should be reasoned and auditable.

</details>

## Chapter 5 — Securities dealing practices and skills

### 51. [RES 1B §5.1–5.2] What should a representative accomplish during client onboarding?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Establish and verify identity, authority, beneficial ownership, objectives, financial circumstances, experience, knowledge, risk appetite, constraints and expected activity; explain the service, products, fees, risks and conflicts; obtain required documents and approvals; and set suitable account permissions and monitoring.

</details>

### 52. [RES 1B §5.2] Distinguish AI, II, EI and retail customers.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Accredited, institutional and expert investors meet different regulatory criteria and may receive different protections or be eligible for different products and processes. Retail customers generally receive the fullest set of conduct and knowledge safeguards. A label is not enough: the firm must verify status, obtain required consent or declarations and apply the correct rules.

</details>

### 53. [RES 1B §5.2] Why is risk profiling not the same as asking a customer whether the customer is willing to lose money?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Risk profiling considers capacity for loss, objectives, time horizon, liquidity needs, knowledge, experience, investment constraints and behavioural tolerance. A customer’s informal answer does not replace a documented, evidence-based assessment or the representative’s duty to explain product-specific risks.

</details>

### 54. [RES 1B §5.3] What is meant by presenting a coherent investment or trading strategy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The representative should connect the customer’s objectives and constraints to an understandable thesis, asset or product selection, position size, time horizon, risks, costs, exit conditions and monitoring plan. The recommendation should be internally consistent rather than a collection of disconnected trades.

</details>

### 55. [RES 1B §5.3] What disclosures should accompany a recommendation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Explain the product and strategy, material risks, possible loss, liquidity and valuation issues, fees and charges, conflicts, assumptions, relevant tax or currency considerations and the limits of any forecast or disclaimer. Communications and the customer’s decision should be recorded.

</details>

### 56. [RES 1B §5.4] What are the principal elements of best execution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Consider price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size, nature of the order, liquidity, venue and the customer’s instructions. The firm needs an execution policy, monitoring and review process, and evidence that the outcome was reasonably designed to achieve the best result for the customer.

</details>

### 57. [RES 1B §5.4] Why is payment for order flow a concern?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A payment or benefit for routing orders can create a conflict between the customer’s best interests and the firm’s revenue. The guide highlights prohibition or strict treatment of payment for order flow; routing must not be selected because it produces an inducement at the expense of execution quality.

</details>

### 58. [RES 1B §5.4] What are the risks in cross trades and late trading?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A cross trade can disadvantage one customer or conceal a conflict if price, allocation, consent and priority are not fair and documented. Late trading can give a customer an improper advantage by accepting orders after a valuation or market cut-off; both require strict controls and supervision.

</details>

### 59. [RES 1B §5.4] How should an execution policy treat large or illiquid orders?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It should recognise that the best result may require staged execution, different venues, limit orders, careful information handling and a balance between price, market impact, speed and certainty. A policy designed only for small liquid orders is not adequate for large, bespoke or illiquid orders.

</details>

### 60. [RES 1B §5.5] What are internal controls intended to prevent?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** They prevent and detect unauthorised trading, errors, fraud, conflicts, unsuitable recommendations, misuse of customer money or assets, data leakage and failures in settlement or reporting. Controls include segregation of duties, approvals, limits, reconciliations, surveillance, exception reporting, access controls and escalation.

</details>

### 61. [RES 1B §5.6] What account types should a representative be able to distinguish?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide discusses account structures such as cash, margin, discretionary or managed arrangements, joint, trust, corporate and custody-related accounts. Each has different authority, documentation, liability, suitability, money-handling and conflict implications; the representative must not treat all accounts as ordinary individual cash accounts.

</details>

### 62. [RES 1B §5.7] How is a strong client relationship built on trust?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Through accurate and timely communication, reliability, confidentiality, fair dealing, transparency about fees and conflicts, consistent follow-up, prompt correction of errors and conduct aligned with the customer’s objectives. Trust is a control outcome as well as a commercial relationship: it reduces pressure to conceal problems or oversell products.

</details>

### 63. [RES 1B §5.8] What is credit risk in securities dealing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is the risk that a customer, counterparty, issuer, broker or other obligor fails to perform a payment or delivery obligation. Manage it through due diligence, limits, margin or collateral, settlement controls, concentration monitoring, escalation and timely action when exposures deteriorate.

</details>

## Chapter 6 — CPF Investment Scheme

### 64. [RES 1B §6.1–6.2] What are CPFIS-OA and CPFIS-SA, and why is the distinction important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** CPFIS-OA permits specified investments from the Ordinary Account, while CPFIS-SA relates to the Special Account and has more restricted investment possibilities and retirement-purpose safeguards. The available products, limits, service providers and withdrawal treatment differ, so a representative must identify the source account before discussing an investment.

</details>

### 65. [RES 1B §6.3] What is the role of a CPF investment account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A CPF investment account is the account through which eligible CPF monies are used for permitted CPFIS investments. The member must open it with an approved agent bank, meet eligibility and assessment requirements, and ensure that transactions, dividends, proceeds and charges are handled according to CPF rules.

</details>

### 66. [RES 1B §6.4–6.5] What eligibility checks and learning requirements apply before a member invests?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The member must meet the applicable age, CPF balance, account and product requirements, complete the prescribed self-awareness or risk-assessment process where required, and open the relevant investment account. Eligibility is not a guarantee that a particular product is suitable or available.

</details>

### 67. [RES 1B §6.6] Summarise the CPFIS purchase and sale process.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Confirm eligibility and product inclusion, ensure the CPF investment account and agent-bank process are in place, submit an authorised instruction, execute through the permitted provider, settle using the correct CPF channel and record the transaction. On sale, proceeds and dividends must be credited or applied as the CPF rules require rather than paid out as ordinary cash.

</details>

### 68. [RES 1B §6.7–6.8] What are service providers and product-inclusion criteria intended to achieve?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Approved agent banks, investment administrators, brokers and other service providers provide the infrastructure and controls for CPF transactions. Product-inclusion criteria limit investments to products and markets that meet prescribed eligibility, disclosure, liquidity, rating, listing or other safeguards.

</details>

### 69. [RES 1B §6.9–6.11] How are transfers, dividends and charges treated under CPFIS?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Transfers into the CPF Ordinary Account and transfers between CPF and investment channels are subject to prescribed procedures and limits. Dividends, interest and profits generally return through the required CPF route, while agent-bank, brokerage, platform, transaction and other permitted charges reduce the investment amount or proceeds as disclosed.

</details>

### 70. [RES 1B §6.12–6.14] What happens to CPFIS holdings on release, bankruptcy or death?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Release is governed by CPF rules, including the relevant age and retirement-sum framework, and is not simply an ordinary brokerage withdrawal. Bankruptcy and death trigger special statutory, estate, nomination and administrative treatment; the representative should not promise immediate access and should refer the member to CPF, the agent bank and the proper estate or insolvency process.

</details>

## Chapter 7 — Prevention of financial crimes

### 71. [RES 1B §7.1] Distinguish money laundering, terrorism financing, sanctions evasion and fraud.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Money laundering disguises the origin or ownership of criminal proceeds; terrorism financing provides or moves funds for terrorist purposes and may involve legitimate funds; sanctions evasion circumvents restrictions imposed on designated persons, countries or activities; and fraud obtains value through deception. One transaction can involve more than one risk.

</details>

### 72. [RES 1B §7.2–7.4] What is the purpose of Singapore’s AML/CFT regime and MAS notices?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The regime requires financial institutions to identify and understand customers and beneficial owners, assess risk, monitor relationships and transactions, maintain controls, file reports and avoid tipping off. MAS notices and guidelines translate statutory duties into sector-specific control expectations.

</details>

### 73. [RES 1B §7.3] Name the main laws that the guide associates with financial-crime controls.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide refers to the SFA and related regulations, the Corruption, Drug Trafficking and Other Serious Crimes (Confiscation of Benefits) Act, the Terrorism (Suppression of Financing) Act, the MAS Act and applicable tax legislation, together with MAS notices and regulations. The exact scope must be read with current law.

</details>

### 74. [RES 1B §7.5] What are targeted financial sanctions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** They restrict dealings with designated persons, entities, countries, assets or activities, including terrorist financing and proliferation-related targets. Firms need screening, escalation, asset-freezing or non-dealing procedures as applicable, and must not help a customer evade a sanction or alert the customer improperly.

</details>

### 75. [RES 1B §7.6] Why are tax crimes relevant to AML controls?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Certain tax offences can be predicate offences that generate criminal proceeds. A tax-related transaction may therefore require the same source-of-wealth, source-of-funds, transaction-monitoring, escalation and reporting analysis as other suspected criminal proceeds.

</details>

### 76. [RES 1B §7.7–7.8] Explain the three lines of defence.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The first line is the business and front office, which owns customer and transaction risk; the second line is compliance or risk management, which sets standards, advises, monitors and challenges; and the third line is internal audit, which independently tests governance and controls. The board and senior management provide governance and accountability across the lines.

</details>

### 77. [RES 1B §7.9] What information should be obtained during AML/CFT onboarding?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Obtain and verify identity, address, occupation or business, purpose and intended nature of the relationship, source of funds and wealth where relevant, beneficial ownership, ownership and control structure, expected activity and connected parties. For a legal person, determine who ultimately owns or controls it, including the guide’s 25% beneficial-ownership threshold where applicable.

</details>

### 78. [RES 1B §7.9] What are PEP, simplified CDD and enhanced CDD controls?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** PEP relationships require risk-sensitive senior approval, source-of-wealth or source-of-funds measures and enhanced ongoing monitoring. Simplified CDD may be used only where the risk and applicable rules permit; enhanced CDD applies to higher-risk cases, such as complex ownership, high-risk jurisdictions, unusual activity or sanctions and PEP concerns.

</details>

### 79. [RES 1B §7.10–7.11] What is an enterprise-wide risk assessment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It identifies and assesses the firm’s ML, TF and sanctions risks across customers, countries, products, services, delivery channels, technologies and transactions, then links the risk rating to controls and resources. The guide highlights reviewing it at least every two years and reassessing new products, practices and technologies before launch.

</details>

### 80. [RES 1B §7.12] What operational controls support an effective AML/CFT programme?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Controls include customer and transaction screening, ongoing monitoring, unusual-activity alerts, risk-based review, approval and escalation, staff access controls, reliable records, independent testing, vendor oversight and management information. Controls should be calibrated to risk rather than treated as a purely mechanical checklist.

</details>

### 81. [RES 1B §7.13] What reporting timelines and thresholds are highlighted in the guide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide highlights transaction-monitoring attention around the S$20,000 threshold, a 30-day review period for relevant circumstances, a 120-day period for specified follow-up or review situations, STR filing within 15 business days after forming the required suspicion, and retention of records for five years. These are exam-study references and should be verified against current notices.

</details>

### 82. [RES 1B §7.13–7.14] What is tipping off and why is it dangerous?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Tipping off is improperly alerting a customer or another person that a suspicious transaction report, investigation or law-enforcement action is being considered or made. It can frustrate investigations, enable asset movement and expose the firm and staff to legal and regulatory consequences.

</details>

### 83. [RES 1B §7.14–7.16] How does a firm assess AML/CFT effectiveness?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It tests whether governance, risk assessment, CDD, screening, monitoring, investigation, reporting, record keeping and training actually identify and mitigate risk. Use independent audit, thematic reviews, sample testing, quality indicators, root-cause analysis, remediation tracking and senior-management reporting; effectiveness is more than having written policies.

</details>

## RES 1B appendices

### 84. [RES 1B Appendix A] What are the three broad fit-and-proper dimensions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Honesty, integrity and reputation; competence and capability; and financial soundness. The assessment considers seriousness, relevance, recency, repetition, remediation and the person’s role, not just whether there has been a formal conviction.

</details>

### 85. [RES 1B Appendix B] What routes can satisfy the CAR knowledge and experience criteria?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide highlights an appropriate diploma or professional qualification, six transactions in listed SIPs during the preceding three years, or at least three consecutive years of relevant experience in the preceding ten years, subject to the prescribed evidence and assessment rules.

</details>

### 86. [RES 1B Appendix C] How does CKA differ from CAR?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** CKA is directed to unlisted SIPs and uses product-relevant knowledge and experience, including unlisted products, CIS or ILP treatment as applicable. CAR is directed to listed SIPs. Both require evidence, a recorded outcome and the firm’s prescribed controls.

</details>

### 87. [RES 1B Appendix D] What categories of risk appear in the overseas-listed product warning?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Regulatory and legal risk, disclosure and information risk, tax and currency risk, transaction and custody costs, counterparty and settlement risk, liquidity and valuation risk, and political, economic and social risk. The customer should receive and acknowledge the warning where required.

</details>

### 88. [RES 1B Appendix E] What is the practical meaning of an excluded investment product?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is a product excluded from the relevant simplified treatment or classified outside the EIP category under the applicable regulations. The firm must classify it accurately; if it is a SIP, apply the relevant CAR or CKA process and customer safeguards.

</details>

### 89. [RES 1B Appendix F] Give four categories of suspicious transaction examples.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Examples include transactions with no apparent economic purpose, unexplained cash or third-party payments, rapid or complex transfers between jurisdictions, accounts using false or inconsistent identity information, unexplained changes in tax or wealth profile, and activity linked to terrorism, sanctions or high-risk persons. A red flag is not proof; it requires risk-based investigation and escalation.

</details>

### 90. [RES 1B Appendix G–H] How should the review questions and essential readings be used?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The review questions should be attempted after studying each chapter and then used to identify gaps; the essential readings identify the SFA, regulations, PDPA, CDSA, MACMA, TSOFA, tax legislation, MAS notices and SGX rules that underpin the guide. They are a navigation and revision tool, not permission to rely on outdated text.

</details>

# Part II — RES 2B

## Chapter 1 — Capital markets industry and derivatives participants

### 91. [RES 2B §1.1] What are the main categories of derivatives described in the guide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Derivatives include futures, options, swaps, forwards and other contracts whose value derives from an underlying such as securities, rates, currencies, commodities or indices. They may be exchange-traded and standardised or OTC and negotiated.

</details>

### 92. [RES 2B §1.1–1.2] Why do exchange-traded and OTC derivatives require different control emphases?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Exchange-traded contracts use standard terms, organised trading, margining and clearing, while OTC contracts allow bespoke terms and may create greater bilateral counterparty, valuation, documentation and collateral risks. The firm must understand the product, venue, settlement and counterparty structure.

</details>

### 93. [RES 2B §1.2] What roles do a derivatives dealer, fund manager, bank and remote member play?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A dealer executes or makes markets and may act as agent or principal; a fund manager uses derivatives for investment or hedging; a bank may provide financing, custody, clearing or dealing; and a remote member accesses a venue from another jurisdiction subject to membership and regulatory arrangements. Each role has distinct risks and permissions.

</details>

### 94. [RES 2B §1.3] Identify the significance of SGX-DT, SGX-DC, CDP, ICE Clear Singapore and APEX Clear.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** They represent trading, clearing, depository or clearing infrastructure for different products and markets. A representative must know whether a contract is traded, cleared, margined and settled through SGX or another venue, because the applicable rulebook, default-management process and customer-asset arrangements differ.

</details>

### 95. [RES 2B §1.3] Where do the rules for derivatives dealing come from?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** They come from the SFA and subsidiary legislation, MAS notices and guidelines, SGX and clearing-house rules, the relevant exchange or OTC documentation, and firm policies and procedures. Contractual documents such as an ISDA Master Agreement and collateral annex may also govern the relationship.

</details>

## Chapter 2 — Licensing and business operations

### 96. [RES 2B §2.1–2.2] What base-capital categories does the derivatives BCR table distinguish?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** For exchange-traded derivatives, the guide highlights S$5 million for a clearing member, S$1 million for a trading member or non-member, S$500,000 for an introducing broker and S$50,000 for other categories. For OTC derivatives, it highlights S$5 million for a clearing business, S$5 million for a non-clearing retail business, S$1 million for a non-clearing non-retail business and S$500,000 for others; specified commodity futures and leveraged FX have their own categories and amounts.

</details>

### 97. [RES 2B §2.2] What are the highlighted BCR figures for specified commodity futures and leveraged FX?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide lists S$1 million for a specified-commodity-futures clearing member, S$500,000 for a non-clearing or non-member category, and S$250,000 for an introducing broker or restricted category. For leveraged FX it lists S$5 million for retail business and S$1 million for AI, II or EI business. These study figures should be checked against current rules.

</details>

### 98. [RES 2B §2.3] What controls apply to registration of a derivatives representative?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The representative must be registered for the correct activity, meet competency and fit-and-proper standards, act for the proper principal, complete required training and CPD, and be supervised. The public register and notifications help customers and regulators verify the person’s status.

</details>

### 99. [RES 2B §2.4] How should derivatives advertising differ from ordinary product marketing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It must not understate leverage, margin calls, losses, complexity, liquidity, valuation or counterparty risks. Claims about returns, success rates or ease of trading must be supportable and balanced by material risks, charges, assumptions and limitations, with proper firm approval.

</details>

### 100. [RES 2B §2.5] What confidentiality rules apply to derivatives customers?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Protect identity, positions, orders, collateral, financial information, counterparties and proprietary trading information. Disclosure requires a proper legal, regulatory, contractual or customer-authorised basis, and access should be limited and monitored.

</details>

### 101. [RES 2B §2.6] What should be established before opening and managing a derivatives account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Establish identity, legal capacity, beneficial ownership, authority, objectives, knowledge, experience, financial resources, risk tolerance, margin capacity, product permissions, trading limits and liquidation terms. Explain the account agreement, risk disclosure, margin calls, close-out, fees and the firm’s right to act when obligations are not met.

</details>

### 102. [RES 2B §2.6] Why are margin and liquidation terms central to customer protection?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Derivatives create leveraged exposure, so a small underlying-price movement can produce a large gain or loss and a customer may have to post additional funds quickly. Clear margin, top-up, close-out, valuation and deficit provisions reduce disputes and prevent a customer from trading without understanding the financial consequences.

</details>

### 103. [RES 2B §2.7] What must a derivatives audit trail allow the firm to reconstruct?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It should reconstruct customer instructions, quotes, orders, amendments, cancellations, execution, allocation, confirmations, margin calls, collateral, valuations, communications, approvals and settlement. The guide highlights five-year record retention and appropriate books and auditor controls.

</details>

### 104. [RES 2B §2.8] What is the purpose of daily computation and customer-money controls?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Daily computation, including the guide’s reference to completion before noon, helps determine whether the firm holds enough resources and has correctly accounted for customer obligations. Customer money and assets must be segregated, reconciled, safeguarded and used only for permitted purposes.

</details>

### 105. [RES 2B §2.9] What is a register of interests in listed specified products?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It records prescribed interests held by relevant officers, employees or connected persons in listed specified products so conflicts and personal dealing can be monitored. The guide highlights updating the register within seven days and retaining it for five years.

</details>

### 106. [RES 2B §2.6–2.8] What is the purpose of the derivatives risk-disclosure Form 13?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It provides a standard warning about the risks of trading futures, options and other derivatives, including leverage, margin, rapid losses, market volatility, liquidity and possible additional obligations. It should be properly provided, signed and dated before the relevant activity as required.

</details>

## Chapter 3 — Market conduct for derivatives

### 107. [RES 2B §3.1–3.2] Why do derivatives market-conduct rules address both the derivative and its underlying?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Trading a derivative can affect the price or supply of the underlying, and manipulation of the underlying can distort the derivative. The rules therefore cover conduct across related instruments and venues where the activity has the prohibited purpose or effect.

</details>

### 108. [RES 2B §3.3] Give two examples of false trading or market rigging in derivatives.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Wash trades and matched orders that create artificial volume or price are examples. Entering transactions without genuine trading intent to make the market appear active or to trigger other participants’ orders can also be prohibited.

</details>

### 109. [RES 2B §3.4] What is cornering or manipulation of a derivatives contract?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is acquiring or controlling a dominant position in the underlying, deliverable supply or relevant contracts so as to distort prices, squeeze counterparties or force artificial settlement outcomes. The assessment includes position size, intent, market conditions, delivery mechanics and the effect on others.

</details>

### 110. [RES 2B §3.5] How can securities-based derivatives be manipulated?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A person can trade or place orders in the derivative, the underlying security or both to create an artificial price, volume or appearance of demand or supply. The prohibition is not avoided merely because the trade occurred in a different product from the instrument whose price was affected.

</details>

### 111. [RES 2B §3.6] What makes information false or misleading for market-conduct purposes?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A statement, forecast, signal or omission can be misleading if it gives a materially inaccurate impression to the market or is likely to affect the price of a derivative or underlying. The representative must verify sources, distinguish opinion from fact and avoid communicating information intended to move the market artificially.

</details>

### 112. [RES 2B §3.7] What is bucketing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Bucketing occurs when an intermediary accepts a customer’s order but does not execute or transmit it as represented, instead taking the opposite position or otherwise dealing with the order in a way that deprives the customer of the promised execution. It is a serious conflict and market-integrity risk.

</details>

### 113. [RES 2B §3.8–3.9] Distinguish fraudulent inducement from a manipulative or deceptive device.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Fraudulent inducement focuses on deception used to cause another person to deal or refrain from dealing. A manipulative or deceptive device covers a broader scheme, method or course of conduct intended to defraud, deceive or distort the market. The same facts may engage both provisions.

</details>

### 114. [RES 2B §3.10] What are the key insider-trading questions for a derivatives representative?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Ask whether the information is specific, not generally available and material to the derivative or underlying; whether the person is connected or otherwise possesses it; whether a trade, recommendation or communication occurred; and whether a statutory exception or defence applies. When uncertain, stop and escalate to compliance.

</details>

### 115. [RES 2B §3.11] Why is dissemination of information about an illegal transaction prohibited?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Communication can facilitate the original offence, attract additional participants, support the artificial price and allow the scheme to continue. A representative must not circulate tips, signals or promotional material that encourages illegal trading.

</details>

### 116. [RES 2B §3.12–3.14] What is prohibited conduct concerning customer orders?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Do not conceal or fail to disclose an order when disclosure is required, withdraw or withhold an order to manipulate the market, or trade against a customer’s order improperly. Order handling must follow priority, consent, best-execution, conflict and record-keeping controls.

</details>

### 117. [RES 2B §3.15] What is front running and why is it unfair?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Front running is trading for oneself or another account before executing a known customer order, using the expected price impact to gain an advantage. It misuses customer information and harms the customer’s execution, so personal trading and order-priority controls are essential.

</details>

### 118. [RES 2B §3.16] What controls are required for cross trades?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Ensure the trade is permitted, both sides are treated fairly, the price is reasonable, conflicts are disclosed or controlled, required consent or approvals are obtained, and the allocation and execution are documented. A cross must not be used to favour the firm or a preferred customer.

</details>

### 119. [RES 2B §3.17–3.18] Distinguish churning from overtrading.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Churning generally means excessive trading in a customer account by the representative or firm to generate commissions or other benefits without regard to the customer’s interests. Overtrading is excessive or unsuitable trading more broadly, including activity encouraged by leverage or poor controls; both are assessed against objectives, resources, strategy, frequency and costs.

</details>

### 120. [RES 2B §3.19] What is a pre-arranged trade?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is a trade arranged in advance between parties in a way that bypasses genuine market competition or creates an artificial price, volume or appearance of trading. Legitimate block or negotiated transactions must still comply with the applicable venue rules, pricing, disclosure and approval requirements.

</details>

### 121. [RES 2B §3.20] How should a candidate study penalties for derivatives misconduct?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Learn the categories of regulatory, criminal, civil, disciplinary, financial and reputational consequences, and understand which conduct triggers them. The source says the amount of penalties is not tested, so do not spend revision time memorising figures.

</details>

## Chapter 4 — OTC derivatives

### 122. [RES 2B §4.1] What is an OTC derivative and what are the principal risks?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is a privately negotiated derivative whose terms are not necessarily standardised or exchange-matched. Risks include counterparty default, collateral and margin disputes, valuation uncertainty, liquidity, legal enforceability, settlement, model, operational, market and documentation risk.

</details>

### 123. [RES 2B §4.1] What are IRS, NDIRS, CCS, FRA and commodity swaps?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** An IRS exchanges interest-payment streams; an NDIRS exchanges interest amounts without physical delivery of the underlying currency or notional; a CCS exchanges cash flows in different currencies; an FRA fixes or settles an interest-rate exposure for a future period; and a commodity swap exchanges commodity-linked cash flows. The contract terms determine the exact economics.

</details>

### 124. [RES 2B §4.2] What is the role of a central counterparty in OTC derivatives?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A CCP interposes itself between counterparties, manages margin and default resources, and supports multilateral netting and risk management. It reduces bilateral counterparty exposure but creates clearing, margin, liquidity, concentration and default-fund obligations.

</details>

### 125. [RES 2B §4.2–4.3] What regulatory requirements can apply to OTC derivatives?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Depending on product and counterparty, requirements may include reporting, central clearing, margin, trade documentation, record keeping, risk disclosure, business-conduct controls and eligible-counterparty or customer protections. SGX-DC clearing rules apply where contracts are cleared through SGX-DC.

</details>

### 126. [RES 2B §4.3] What is the significance of SGX Titan OTC and margin rules?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The clearing platform and margin methodology determine how trades are accepted, valued, margined, netted and settled. Representatives must understand initial and variation margin, eligible collateral, calls, deadlines and default procedures rather than treating an OTC derivative as an unsecured promise with no liquidity consequence.

</details>

### 127. [RES 2B §4.4] What should Form 13 and Form 14 risk disclosures achieve?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** They provide prescribed information about risks and product features so the customer can make an informed decision. The relevant form, delivery, signature, date and supporting records must be completed before or in connection with the transaction as required; a disclosure cannot cure misleading advice.

</details>

### 128. [RES 2B §4.5] Why is an ISDA Master Agreement important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It standardises the legal framework for OTC transactions, including representations, events of default, termination, netting, payment terms and dispute provisions. A Credit Support Annex may govern collateral. Representatives should ensure the transaction is within the authorised documentation and escalate legal or documentation gaps.

</details>

### 129. [RES 2B §4.5] How does OTC settlement differ from exchange settlement?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Exchange and CCP processes generally use standard contracts, daily marking-to-market and centralised margin and settlement, while OTC contracts may settle bilaterally on dates and methods negotiated by the parties. Bilateral valuation, payment, confirmation, collateral and dispute controls are therefore critical.

</details>

### 130. [RES 2B §4.5] What should a representative check before recommending a bespoke OTC derivative?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Check the customer’s objective, sophistication, authority, financial resources, suitability, legal capacity, documentation, collateral and liquidity capacity, valuation method, termination rights, counterparty, costs, model assumptions, scenarios, worst-case loss and regulatory status. If the product cannot be explained clearly, it should not be presented as routine.

</details>

## Chapter 5 — Ethics and standards for derivatives dealing

### 131. [RES 2B §5.1–5.3] Why do leverage and complexity make ethics especially important in derivatives?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Leverage can magnify losses, products may be difficult to value, and customers can be exposed to margin calls or obligations exceeding the initial amount. Ethical conduct requires clear explanations, reasonable-basis analysis, fair dealing and resistance to sales incentives that encourage unsuitable risk.

</details>

### 132. [RES 2B §5.4] What professional standards apply to market information and market manipulation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Use reliable sources, distinguish fact from opinion, disclose material assumptions and do not create false signals, spread rumours or use customer information for personal benefit. The same professional duty applies whether the information concerns an exchange-traded contract, OTC instrument or underlying market.

</details>

### 133. [RES 2B §5.4] How should complaints and performance claims be handled?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Acknowledge and escalate complaints promptly through the firm’s independent process, preserve records and avoid retaliation or defensive concealment. Performance claims must be fair, consistent, supported by methodology and balanced for leverage, fees, losses, survivorship, time period and liquidity.

</details>

### 134. [RES 2B §5.4] Why can disclosing a customer’s principal identity be a conflict?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Revealing that a particular customer is behind a trade can expose confidential strategy, create information advantages for others or affect price and negotiation. Protect customer identity and order information unless disclosure is authorised or required.

</details>

### 135. [RES 2B §5.4] What do diligence, reasonable basis and communication standards require?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Investigate the product, counterparty, market, assumptions and customer circumstances sufficiently to support the recommendation; communicate material risks and uncertainties in a comprehensible way; and retain records that show the basis, approvals, instructions and outcome.

</details>

### 136. [RES 2B §5.4] What is the BIS FX Global Code and what themes does the guide associate with it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is a global set of principles for the wholesale FX market. The guide associates it with ethics, governance, execution, information sharing, risk management and confirmation and settlement, and highlights six broad principles requiring transparent, fair, robust and well-controlled conduct.

</details>

### 137. [RES 2B §5.5] How is best execution applied to a derivatives transaction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Consider price or rate, spread, commission, financing and collateral cost, size, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, liquidity, counterparty, market impact, product terms and the customer’s instructions. For OTC trades, the firm should have a reasonable process for comparing quotes or otherwise demonstrating a fair outcome.

</details>

### 138. [RES 2B §5.6] Apply the ethical framework to a salesperson told to hide a large possible margin call.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Identify the facts and affected customer, recognise duties of honesty, suitability and fair dealing, reject the concealment, explain the margin risk, document the issue and escalate to compliance or management. If the firm will not correct the communication, do not proceed and use the applicable whistleblowing or escalation channel.

</details>

### 139. [RES 2B §5.4–5.6] What should a representative do when compensation conflicts with best execution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Disclose and manage the conflict, apply the firm’s execution policy, compare the customer outcome across venues or counterparties, obtain required approval and preserve evidence. Compensation cannot justify a venue, counterparty or product choice that produces an inferior customer result.

</details>

### 140. [RES 2B §5.4–5.6] Why are records an ethical control rather than mere administration?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Records make the representative accountable for advice, pricing, conflicts, consent, execution and escalation. They allow the firm and regulator to test whether conduct matched the customer’s interest and the stated process, and discourage post hoc rationalisation.

</details>

## Chapter 6 — Derivatives dealing practices and skills

### 141. [RES 2B §6.1–6.2] Compare fundamental, technical and global-macro analysis.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Fundamental analysis evaluates economic, financial and issuer or commodity drivers; technical analysis studies price, volume, trends and patterns; global-macro analysis considers rates, currencies, inflation, policy, growth, geopolitical events and cross-market relationships. A sound process states assumptions and limitations rather than treating any method as certain.

</details>

### 142. [RES 2B §6.3] What should a derivatives trading strategy specify?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Define the objective, expected return, acceptable risk, time horizon, products, underlying exposure, entry and exit rules, leverage, margin and collateral, liquidity, stop-loss or hedge rules, concentration limits, monitoring and contingency actions. Constraints should be consistent with the customer’s resources and mandate.

</details>

### 143. [RES 2B §6.4] What controls are important during strategy implementation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Confirm authority and limits, use approved venues and counterparties, check orders and margin, apply best execution and allocation controls, record rationale and customer communications, monitor execution, collateral, P&L and exposure, and escalate exceptions, breaches or market disruptions.

</details>

### 144. [RES 2B §6.4] Why are stop-losses and daily loss limits not complete risk controls?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Gaps, illiquidity, limit moves, system outages and counterparty failure may prevent execution at the chosen stop. Limits reduce intended exposure but do not guarantee a maximum loss; they must be supported by stress testing, liquidity planning, margin monitoring and escalation.

</details>

### 145. [RES 2B §6.5] What KYC information is especially important for derivatives customers?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** In addition to identity and objectives, assess experience with leverage and the specific derivative, financial resources, margin capacity, liquidity, loss capacity, authority, hedging purpose, knowledge of close-out and collateral, and whether the customer can understand the product’s payoff and worst-case scenarios.

</details>

### 146. [RES 2B §6.6] What safeguards apply to a retail customer’s SIP trading account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Apply the relevant CAR or CKA, obtain the required account approval, assess the product and customer, provide disclosures and warnings, document the outcome and monitor the account. Knowledge assessment does not remove suitability, best-execution, conduct or risk-disclosure duties.

</details>

### 147. [RES 2B §6.7] Name and explain five risks in derivatives dealing.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Market risk arises from price, rate, FX or volatility changes; credit risk from counterparty default; liquidity risk from inability to exit or fund margin; gap risk from discontinuous price moves; and operational or technology risk from failed processes or systems. Other guide categories include correlation, political/legal, capital, regulatory and interconnected risks.

</details>

### 148. [RES 2B §6.7] What is correlation risk?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Positions that appear diversified may move together during stress, increasing rather than reducing exposure. Correlation can change abruptly, so risk management should test concentrations, common factors, basis relationships and stressed correlations.

</details>

### 149. [RES 2B §6.7] What is gap risk?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The market moves sharply from one price to another without executable prices between them, so a stop-loss may fill far away from the stop level or not at all. It is particularly important for leveraged, event-sensitive or illiquid positions.

</details>

### 150. [RES 2B §6.7] What does the guide mean by MAS CRAFT and impact-and-risk supervision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The framework represents supervisory attention to the firm’s risk profile, governance, controls, resilience and remediation. The practical lesson is to use diagnostic information and impact assessment to identify material weaknesses, correct them and demonstrate that controls operate effectively.

</details>

## Chapter 7 — Prevention of financial crimes

### 151. [RES 2B §7.1–7.4] Why must AML/CFT controls cover derivatives customers and counterparties?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Derivatives can move value quickly, create complex payment and collateral flows, obscure beneficial ownership and involve cross-border counterparties. The firm must understand the customer, purpose, expected activity, counterparties, jurisdictions and transaction flows and apply the same risk-based AML/CFT framework.

</details>

### 152. [RES 2B §7.5] How should sanctions screening interact with derivatives execution and settlement?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Screen customers, beneficial owners, counterparties, payment parties and relevant transactions before and during the relationship. A potential match or prohibited connection should be held, escalated and handled under the applicable asset-freezing, reporting and non-dealing requirements; staff must not help the parties evade screening.

</details>

### 153. [RES 2B §7.6] Give an example of a tax-crime red flag in a derivatives relationship.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A customer may seek complex cross-border structures or rapid transfers that have no commercial rationale and appear designed to conceal beneficial ownership, taxable income or the source of wealth. The firm should investigate purpose and source of funds and escalate if suspicion is formed.

</details>

### 154. [RES 2B §7.7–7.8] What is senior management’s responsibility for AML/CFT?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Senior management and the board set risk appetite, approve the framework, provide resources, appoint accountable officers, receive meaningful reporting, challenge weaknesses and ensure remediation. They cannot delegate away accountability by relying only on compliance or an automated system.

</details>

### 155. [RES 2B §7.9] What is the beneficial-owner question for a corporate derivatives customer?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Determine the natural person who ultimately owns or controls the entity, including ownership or control through chains, voting rights, agreements or other means. The guide highlights the relevant 25% threshold where applicable, but control can exist even without a simple 25% holding.

</details>

### 156. [RES 2B §7.10–7.11] How should a derivatives firm assess new-product AML/CFT risk?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Before launch, assess product features, settlement and collateral flows, counterparties, jurisdictions, anonymity, speed, technology, delivery channels, expected customer base and monitoring capability. Do not launch until controls, screening, alerts, approval, training and escalation are adequate for the identified risk.

</details>

### 157. [RES 2B §7.12] What is a risk-based transaction-monitoring approach?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Set scenarios and thresholds according to customer, product, geography, transaction and delivery-channel risk; investigate alerts in context; document decisions; tune false positives; and escalate patterns involving rapid transfers, unusual collateral, third parties, complex layering or activity inconsistent with the customer profile.

</details>

### 158. [RES 2B §7.13–7.16] What evidence shows that an AML/CFT framework is effective?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Evidence includes accurate risk assessments, timely CDD refreshes, effective screening, meaningful alerts, well-reasoned investigations, prompt STR decisions, reliable records, tested systems, trained staff, independent audit findings, remediation closure and management information showing outcomes rather than only activity volumes.

</details>

## Appendix review-question alignment drills

The following drills are paraphrased from the source guide’s review-question themes. They are additional recall checks, not a reproduction of the source question wording.

### 166. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q1] Which changes to a CMS licence holder’s records typically require notification to MAS?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Changes such as the firm’s name and principal place of business for the licensed activity are prescribed notification matters. Client-pool contact numbers and back-office arrangements are not a substitute for the prescribed record changes.

</details>

### 167. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q2] Which fit-and-proper concern is directly relevant to a representative’s eligibility?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Inability to meet financial obligations is relevant to financial soundness. Fit and propriety also cover honesty, integrity, reputation, competence and capability, including serious or relevant past conduct.

</details>

### 168. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q3] What acknowledgement is required before accepting relevant overseas-listed investment-product transactions from a retail investor?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Obtain the customer’s signed acknowledgement that the prescribed Risk Warning Statement highlighting the key risks has been received. A verbal explanation alone or an undocumented mailing is not the required substitute.

</details>

### 169. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q4] What is the purpose of periodic customer statements, and what is not enough to waive them?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Statements give customers a regular record of holdings, transactions, balances and charges. A verbal request, no change in the account or information sent separately by CDP does not by itself satisfy the prescribed conditions for not sending a statement; use the applicable written-consent and regulatory process.

</details>

### 170. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q5] In what general circumstance may a CMS holder advance its own money to a customer trust account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Only for a permitted purpose connected with facilitating positions or transactions in capital-markets products, or another purpose expressly allowed by the rules. It must not be an unauthorised way to conceal an under-funded trust account.

</details>

### 171. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q6] Does executing a trade outside Singapore necessarily avoid SFA insider-trading rules?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** No. The SFA can apply extraterritorially where the required Singapore connection or market effect exists. Use of a foreign broker, foreign venue or foreign currency does not by itself remove the market-conduct obligation.

</details>

### 172. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q7] What are typical channels for circulating false or misleading information?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Electronic media, rumours, word-of-mouth “hot tips” and unverified claims can all be used. Circulating an accurate audited account is not automatically a false-information technique, though a person can still misrepresent or selectively present it.

</details>

### 173. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q8] Why can a transaction between connected persons raise a market-conduct concern?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The connection may create access to generally unavailable material information, a conflict or an artificial appearance of trading. The representative must assess beneficial ownership, information barriers, approvals, disclosure and whether the transaction has a genuine economic purpose.

</details>

### 174. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q9] What securities-lending conditions should a dealer remember?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Record whether the dealer acts as agent or principal, follow procedures for returning borrowed products, and maintain collateral at the required level—highlighted in the source as 100% of market value for the relevant arrangement. The arrangement must also be properly documented and monitored.

</details>

### 175. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q10] What consequence categories can follow SFA market misconduct?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Civil penalties or sanctions and criminal penalties may apply, together with regulatory, disciplinary, financial and reputational consequences. “Restraint sanctions” is not a substitute category to memorise.

</details>

### 176. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q11] Which is an external influence on ethical judgement: authority figures or moral values?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Authority figures are an external influence. Moral values are internal; overconfidence and rationalisation are internal psychological or cognitive influences.

</details>

### 177. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q12] What ethical threat arises when a representative changes a portfolio mainly to protect personal commission income?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is a self-interest threat. The representative puts personal financial benefit ahead of the customer’s mandate and must identify, disclose, control or remove the conflict.

</details>

### 178. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q13] Why is promising a guaranteed 20% return usually a professional-standards breach?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It misrepresents the certainty of performance and may mislead the prospective customer about risk, methodology and track record. The conduct is misrepresentation even if the representative has a long history or a popular model.

</details>

### 179. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q14] Give examples of conflicts that should be disclosed or controlled.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Trading personally ahead of a customer order, receiving undisclosed extra compensation from a company being analysed, using a former employer’s research as one’s own, or engaging in outside activities that impair duties may all require conflict management. The response depends on the facts and firm policy, but concealment is not acceptable.

</details>

### 180. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q15] What two actions are central to applying an ethical framework?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Consider the ethical issues and relevant duties without allowing bias to control the decision, then execute the chosen course and track its progress. Identifying “enemies” is not a substitute for stakeholder and consequence analysis.

</details>

### 181. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q16] What is the four-eyes principle in client risk profiling?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A second appropriately authorised person independently reviews or approves the risk profile or related decision. It improves objectivity and reduces the chance that sales pressure, bias or a single person’s error determines the customer’s risk classification.

</details>

### 182. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q17] A designated market maker with S$50 million net assets owned by three corporations may meet which investor category?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide’s fact pattern is directed to the **accredited investor** category, subject to the exact statutory criteria and required verification. Investor status should never be inferred from a label alone.

</details>

### 183. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q18] What information must be obtained when establishing a client relationship?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Obtain relevant customer information, including objectives, financial situation, knowledge, experience, constraints and risk profile, before making or executing recommendations that require it. The customer cannot be left with the entire responsibility for suitability.

</details>

### 184. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q19] Which best-execution practices should a securities dealer apply?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Use sound due-diligence and execution policies, execute with appropriate speed, and apply considerations specific to the order type. Best execution is not limited to a customer’s written consent or to one factor such as price.

</details>

### 185. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q20] Name three practices that strengthen the client–representative relationship.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Communicate risk disclosures, give customer orders appropriate priority, distinguish whether the firm acts as agent or principal, and maintain professional product and process knowledge. These practices support informed decisions and trust.

</details>

### 186. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q21] What type of CPFIS-OA investment is subject to the highlighted 35% limit?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The source’s 35% limit applies to specified higher-risk CPFIS-OA investment categories such as stocks or shares and corporate bonds, subject to the CPF rules. It is not a blanket 35% limit on every CPFIS product.

</details>

### 187. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q22] Name an agent bank included under CPFIS in the source.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** OCBC Bank is one of the agent banks identified in the source; the guide also discusses DBS and UOB. Current approved-provider lists should be checked before use.

</details>

### 188. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q23] Would a member with S$18,000 in OA and S$20,000 in SA meet the source’s basic CPFIS investment thresholds?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** No. The source highlights minimum balances of S$20,000 in OA and S$40,000 in SA for the relevant eligibility tests; employment status alone is not the decisive issue.

</details>

### 189. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q24] What are two important CPFIS ETF inclusion conditions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The ETF should not be on the SGX-ST watch-list and must comply with CPF Investment Guidelines. Listing on SGX-ST and Singapore-dollar trading alone do not replace all inclusion criteria.

</details>

### 190. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q25] Which document generally supports a claim for residual CPFIS investments after a member’s death?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** A Grant of Probate is the relevant document where there is a will and probate is required; letters of administration or other estate documents may apply in other circumstances. The representative should refer the claimant to the agent bank and proper estate process.

</details>

### 191. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q26] Reselling luxury goods and depositing the proceeds into multiple accounts illustrates which laundering stage?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It most closely illustrates layering because the proceeds are moved through transactions or accounts to make tracing more difficult. Placement is the initial entry of proceeds into the financial system; integration is the appearance that laundered proceeds are legitimate wealth.

</details>

### 192. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q27] Which Singapore statute is the principal money-laundering statute highlighted in the review question?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The Corruption, Drug Trafficking and Other Serious Crimes (Confiscation of Benefits) Act is the principal statute highlighted. The SFA, TSOFA and other laws may also be relevant to particular conduct and controls.

</details>

### 193. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q28] What minimum retention period is highlighted for AML documents?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The guide highlights at least five years, subject to the applicable notice, investigation, litigation or firm policy requirements.

</details>

### 194. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q29] Which information is central to KYC for a new brokerage account?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Identify and verify the customer, understand the source of funds and determine the customer’s business activity or purpose. Marital status is not a general substitute for these AML/KYC requirements.

</details>

### 195. [RES 1B Appendix G, source Q30] What should a representative do after learning that a customer is on a relevant sanctions list?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Follow the sanctions process, including immediately preventing or freezing prohibited dealings where required and reporting or escalating to the authorities and compliance. Do not delete records, quietly transfer the customer or continue ordinary monitoring as if nothing happened.

</details>

### 196. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q1] Which changes must a derivatives CMS licence holder report to MAS?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The source highlights changes such as the licence holder’s name and the principal place of business for the licensed activity. The firm should use the prescribed notification channel and time period.

</details>

### 197. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q2] When must a derivatives CMS licence holder notify MAS that it has ceased the regulated activity?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The source highlights notification within 14 days after cessation of the activity, subject to the applicable current rule and prescribed form.

</details>

### 198. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q3] How long must customer transaction and trading records be kept?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The source highlights five years. A firm may need to retain records longer where another law, investigation, dispute, notice or policy requires it.

</details>

### 199. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q4] What does “hunting for stops” mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It means deliberately pushing prices up or down with the intention of triggering known stop orders. It is not merely accepting simultaneous orders, entering a wash trade or withholding an order.

</details>

### 200. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q5] Which of insider trading, false trading and pre-arranged trading are market-misconduct categories?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** All three are market-misconduct categories under the relevant SFA framework. “Under trading” is not the source’s substitute category.

</details>

### 201. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q6] A trader buys personal Brent Crude futures before executing a customer’s large order expected to move price. What is the conduct?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is front running: the trader misuses advance knowledge of the customer’s order to obtain a personal advantage before execution.

</details>

### 202. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q7] Which products are OTC derivatives in the source’s example?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Commodity swaps, forward-rate agreements and cross-currency swaps are OTC derivatives. Fixed-income securities are not themselves derivatives merely because they may be used as an underlying or hedging instrument.

</details>

### 203. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q8] What is the central objective of the ethical framework?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** To serve the best interests of clients while maintaining integrity, fairness, competence and compliance. Speed, product expertise and protecting the employer’s finances are not substitutes for the client-interest objective.

</details>

### 204. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q9] What was unethical about marketing five years of back-tested performance without saying it was back-tested?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The presentation failed to uphold performance-presentation standards because it could make simulated historical results appear to be actual performance. The methodology, assumptions and limitations had to be disclosed clearly.

</details>

### 205. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q10] Which factors belong in derivatives best execution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Speed, opportunities for price improvement and client-specific instructions are relevant. Confidential handling of price information is an important conduct control, but it is not itself the customer-outcome factor being tested in that combination.

</details>

### 206. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q11] When might a CKA not be required for a retail customer trading an unlisted SIP?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The source highlights the relevant experience route of at least six transactions in unlisted SIPs during the preceding three years, subject to the applicable rules and evidence. Experience in blue-chip listed shares does not automatically satisfy an unlisted-SIP CKA requirement.

</details>

### 207. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q12] What is regulatory risk in derivatives trading?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** It is the risk that changes in laws, regulations, regulatory interpretation or supervisory requirements affect products, markets, costs, permissions or the customer’s ability to trade. It is different from ordinary commodity-price, liquidity or employee-financial-crime risk.

</details>

### 208. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q13] Which statute is highlighted as governing money laundering?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** The Corruption, Drug Trafficking and Other Serious Crimes (Confiscation of Benefits) Act is the highlighted statute, while other laws may apply to terrorism financing, market conduct and related offences.

</details>

### 209. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q14] What should happen when a customer appears on a money-laundering sanctions list?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** Freeze or prevent prohibited dealings immediately where required and report or escalate to the authorities and compliance. Never delete records or port the customer to another representative or firm to avoid the controls.

</details>

### 210. [RES 2B Appendix F, source Q15] Are terrorism-financing funds always illegitimate or always large?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:** No. Terrorism financing may use funds from legitimate or illegitimate sources, and the amount may be small or large. Controls therefore focus on purpose, persons, networks, transactions and risk indicators rather than amount alone.

</details>

## Suggested revision method

1. Answer a chapter’s questions without opening the disclosures.
2. Mark each answer as correct, partly correct or incorrect and return to the corresponding guide subsection.
3. Re-attempt incorrect questions after explaining the rule aloud and creating a short fact pattern.
4. Finish with mixed practice: licensing, conduct, ethics, skills and AML/CFT should be interleaved because exam questions often test the interaction between them.
