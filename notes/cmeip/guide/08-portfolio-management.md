# Chapter 8 — Portfolio Management

Portfolio management is where the earlier chapters converge: risk and return measurement, correlation, the pricing models that link risk to expected return, the process that turns a client's objectives into an asset mix, and the measures that judge whether the manager added value. This chapter covers all four parts.

## 8.1 Introduction

The chapter has four parts.

- The **Efficient Market Hypothesis** — its variants and its relevance to portfolio management, plus portfolio management strategies in light of it.
- **Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory** — the foundation of portfolio management: diversification and correlation, systematic and non-systematic risk, and **William F. Sharpe's CAPM**, which describes the risk-return relationship by evaluating investments in a portfolio context.
- The **portfolio management process** — constructing a portfolio: the determinants of investment policy (objectives and constraints), and strategic and tactical asset allocation.
- **Performance** — how returns are measured, risk-adjusted measures (Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen's alpha), and performance attribution.

## 8.2 The Efficient Market Hypothesis

The EMH states that an efficient market is one where **security prices always fully reflect available information**. Market participants act collectively, with prices adjusting quickly to all known information about the firm. The direct implication is that stocks always trade at fair value and it is **impossible to outperform the overall market consistently on a risk-adjusted basis** through expert stock selection or market timing, because all available information is already in the price.

After more than half a century of research the validity of the EMH remains **inconclusive**. Possibly this is because there are **varying degrees** of market efficiency, allowing a minority with an advantage to exploit pockets of informational inefficiency for abnormal gains over certain periods.

There are three forms: weak, semi-strong and strong.

### 8.2.1 Weak Form of the EMH

Under the weak form, investors **cannot consistently make abnormal profits using historical price and volume information**. All past price information is fully reflected in the current price. Future prices cannot be predicted by analysing past prices and therefore follow a **random walk** without serial dependency. This implies investors cannot consistently achieve excess returns using **technical analysis**.

Some participants do not behave as if markets are weak-form efficient. Technical analysis remains used to detect unusual volume increases and price trends signalling buying or selling interest, and to trade with momentum and direction. Extensive **high-frequency trading (HFT)** seeking to exploit price trends and momentum is also inconsistent with weak-form efficiency. Institutions operating **dark pools** obtain confidential client trading information that could provide an unfair advantage — as in the **SEC enforcement action against two major banks** that supplied dark pool trading information to external high-frequency traders.

Despite this behaviour, the empirical evidence indicates technical analysis **generally does not yield abnormal returns**, supporting the weak form.

### 8.2.2 Semi-Strong Form of the EMH

Under the semi-strong form, **all publicly available information** is fully reflected in the current price, and prices react rapidly to new public information such as earnings announcements and management changes. This implies **neither fundamental nor technical analysis** allows investors to consistently beat the market.

As with weak-form efficiency, many participants do not behave as if markets are semi-strong efficient. There are many anecdotes of value-oriented investors with consistently successful track records, though it has been argued this can occur by **pure chance** given the sheer number of investors, and that observed excess returns may simply be compensation for an **unidentified risk**.

### 8.2.3 Strong Form of the EMH

Under the strong form, **all information, public or private**, is fully reflected in the current price. Investors cannot make consistent abnormal profits on any type of information, including insider or private information — even insider trading would give no advantage.

The evidence suggests real-world markets are **not** strong-form efficient. The history of insider trading in the US and numerous high-profile cases continue to suggest inside information does provide an advantage. Hence the need for regulatory oversight by the SEC and equivalent bodies worldwide. In less developed markets with fewer enforcement and monitoring capabilities, the advantage conferred by inside information could be even higher.

### 8.2.4 Interpreting and Applying the EMH

Supporters of market efficiency highlight that the **average fund manager does not outperform the market**. Despite claimed expertise, the majority underperform their benchmarks **even before fees**. Even for managers who outperform, the outperformance **does not generally persist**.

Markets are therefore probably quite efficient, making it difficult to beat the benchmark consistently. But there may be **periods of inefficiency** in which irrational behaviour and uneven distribution of price-sensitive information let more astute professionals outperform.

For those who believe in efficient markets, a **passive** strategy makes sense: find an efficient low-cost fund that replicates a benchmark index. For those who believe markets can be inefficient, they can:

- Try to outperform by trading actively.
- Adopt a longer-term approach, selecting and holding investments with strong fundamentals.
- Identify and choose an active portfolio manager.

## 8.3 Passive versus Active Management Strategies

Institutional and corporate investors choose between passive and active management. For individuals, the choice depends on additional factors beyond belief in market efficiency:

- Funds available for investing (size of investment).
- Desire for personal involvement in trading or investment.
- Amount of time available to manage investments.
- Investment expertise.
- Market structure.

### 8.3.1 Passive Strategy

The EMH implies it is not possible to outperform through active share selection and analysis. For investors who believe in efficient markets, frequent trading generates large brokerage commissions without enhancing expected performance. **Passive management**, such as a **buy-and-hold** strategy, is therefore recommended. Passive investors believe such a strategy will, over the long term, produce results just as good as active management because of market efficiency.

Alternatively they can invest in an **equity index fund** or portfolio. Index funds track a selected market index, and the manager is judged on **how well they track the target index**. Expenses — research costs, management fees, brokerage commissions — are kept to a minimum.

Investors who:

- Can only invest small amounts,
- Lack the time or expertise to monitor and manage investments, and
- Do not wish to pay fees,

should probably adopt a passive approach and rely on index funds for diversification.

### 8.3.2 Active Strategy

The existence of numerous asset management firms suggests most investors do not subscribe to the EMH and favour an active approach. Valuation models and analysis of the economy, industries and individual companies are all elements of active management, whose objective is to **outperform the market on a risk-adjusted basis**.

Active investors believe they have advantages over other participants — superior analytical skills, or access to information not available to others. Three active strategies:

**a. Security selection.** Value-oriented specialists believe they can outperform by selecting securities they believe are **undervalued**, typically using fundamental analysis and valuation models such as the dividend discount or P/E ratio model. They tend to look for **low P/E shares** or shares trading below intrinsic value.

**b. Sector rotation.** Shifting sector weights to take advantage of sectors expected to outperform while avoiding or underweighting those expected to underperform. Under the Industry Classification Benchmarks the market is classified into **10 industries**, further subdivided into super sectors and sectors; a **sector is the smallest subset of an industry**. It is common to categorise ordinary shares into broad sectors — interest-sensitive, cyclical and defensive — each expected to perform differently through the business cycle:

- **Interest-sensitive** shares are expected to **underperform during periods of high interest rates**.
- **Cyclical** shares do well in an upturn and badly in a downturn.
- **Defensive** shares are not hurt as badly during a downturn.

Effective sector rotation depends heavily on **accurate assessment of current economic conditions**.

**c. Market timing.** When equities are expected to do well, timers shift holdings from cash equivalents to equities; when equities are expected to do poorly, they liquidate equity holdings. Market timing strategies therefore involve **active asset allocation**.

## 8.4 Portfolio Theory

### 8.4.1 Introduction to Portfolio Theory

Portfolio theory discusses how investors create **optimal portfolios** that maximise expected return while minimising risk. A simple optimal portfolio can be built from two asset classes — S&P 500 equities and US Treasury bonds.

Over the last 90 years, portfolio returns for different equity-bond weights were plotted against portfolio standard deviation. Although stock-bond correlation over the last decade was mostly negative (averaging about **ρ = −0.20**), since **August 2017** correlation has turned positive again, so a positive correlation of **ρ = 0.1** is applied in the illustration.

The **efficient frontier** represents all combinations of the two asset classes generating the **highest return at a given level of risk**. Every combination on the frontier is an optimal portfolio, but for clients with different risk profiles: portfolios with higher equity weights suit higher risk appetites, with the highest-risk portfolio being **100% equity**; portfolios with higher bond weights suit lower risk appetites.

Investors should **not** pick portfolios that lie **below** the efficient frontier — for example a portfolio at **5.2% return and 8.1% volatility**, or one at **4.7% return and 8.5% volatility** — because at that level of risk better choices offer higher returns.

Most banks present potential optimal allocations diagrammatically on the efficient frontier, with lower risk and return portfolios at one end and higher at the other. The frontier is a **curve, not a straight line**, because the two assets' returns are not perfectly correlated.

If adding alternative investments — hedge funds, derivatives, private equity, commodity funds, real estate funds — can either **add return per unit of risk** or **lower risk per unit of return**, they are beneficial and move the efficient frontier toward the **upper left** of the graph.

But simply adding new assets does not necessarily increase return per unit of risk: some assets increase portfolio risk without adequately improving returns. Review the impact on expected volatility and returns before accepting any proposed addition.

### 8.4.2 Portfolio Diversification

Diversification is the financial equivalent of "don't put all your eggs in one basket". Studies show that increasing the number of securities from different industries reduces portfolio risk. Further diversification comes from different asset classes and selective international investment.

Diversification across asset classes is recommended because it produces a better risk-adjusted return, but it should not be done in an ad hoc or blanket fashion. Buying assets with a **lower return per unit of risk** and **high correlation** with the existing portfolio is unlikely to improve diversification. Identifying assets with the potential to generate higher return per unit of risk requires a well-thought-out process of reviewing markets, sectors and companies to overweight or underweight, either as long-term allocation or tactically.

**Worked illustration — diversification within an asset class.** A portfolio holding only one security (Apple) faces three broad levels of risk:

- **General economic environment** — business cycles, interest rates, currency movements.
- **Industry-specific risk** — the IT/consumer electronics industry: changes in consumer preferences, technological obsolescence.
- **Firm-specific risk** — new product launches, research and marketing success, Apple's management.

Adding a second stock (Monsanto, a producer of genetically modified grains) reduces portfolio risk **to the extent that firm-specific influences differ**. It does not matter whether the factors affecting Monsanto are diametrically opposed to those affecting Apple; all that matters is that correlation is **less than perfectly positive**. As long as correlation is not 1, adding Monsanto has the potential to diversify risk.

As more securities are added, the effect of firm-specific factors on volatility is further limited, so portfolio volatility continues to decline but **at a slower rate**. Studies show that around **half of portfolio standard deviation is eliminated by the time there are 10 securities**. The benefits then diminish, with marginal risk reduction small.

Theoretically, a portfolio containing **all** shares in a market (a stock market index) has completely diversified away all non-systematic risk, leaving only **systematic risk** — risks common to and affecting all securities. Because all shares are affected by the business cycle to varying degrees, business cycle risk cannot be avoided however diversified the portfolio.

The risk remaining after extensive diversification is **market risk** or **systematic risk**, attributed to market-wide sources. The risk eliminated by diversification is **firm-specific risk** or **non-systematic risk**.

### 8.4.3 Systematic and Non-systematic Risks

$$\text{Total risk} = \text{Systematic risk} + \text{Non-systematic risk}$$

**Systematic risk** (undiversifiable risk) results from factors affecting all firms, such as interest rate risk and inflation risk. It is caused by changes in the economic, political and sociological environment that affect the prices of all marketable securities, and **cannot be eliminated by diversification**.

**Non-systematic risk** (firm-specific risk) can be mitigated through diversification. It is the portion of total risk peculiar to a firm or industry, arising from factors affecting a company's earnings potential — company strategies, management errors, new product developments and innovations. Because it is caused by factors affecting specific firms or industries, it can be minimised by investing in a broad number of different firms or industries. It can theoretically be **reduced to zero** with a well-diversified portfolio of uncorrelated shares.

Four further points:

- **Systematic risk cannot be reduced through diversification**, but depending on exposure and asset class, investors can partly or almost fully **offset** it through **hedging** with options and futures.
- **Non-systematic risk can be reduced by diversification.** The larger the number of assets, the lower the overall asset-specific risk.
- **Systematic risk is reflected in beta (β)** — the sensitivity of the portfolio to market movement.
- **Realised total risk** is captured by the realised standard deviation of the portfolio, which includes systematic risk, non-systematic risk, and an **error term** capturing the deviation between predicted and actual standard deviation.

### 8.4.4 Correlation Coefficient

The correlation coefficient (ρ, "rho") measures the extent to which the returns of two securities move together. **Correlation denotes association, not causation.**

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **+1.0** | **Perfect positive correlation** — returns have an exactly direct linear relation; identical return patterns |
| **0** | Returns have **no linear relation** |
| **−1.0** | **Perfect negative correlation** — exactly inverse linear relation; when one return is high the other is negative |

For efficient diversification, include securities whose returns are **not highly correlated**. If returns are highly correlated, risk cannot be reduced significantly.

- Combining securities with **ρ = 1** provides **no reduction** in portfolio risk; the portfolio risk is simply the weighted average of the individual risks.
- Combining two securities with **ρ = 0** brings **some reduction**; adding more securities with lower correlation to the portfolio achieves significant risk reduction.
- Combining two securities with **ρ = −1** can **eliminate portfolio risk altogether** — the principle behind hedging strategies.

As long as correlation is less than perfectly positive, risk can be reduced. Typically returns have some correlation but are not perfectly positive, so portfolio risk can almost always be reduced but usually **not completely eliminated**.

### 8.4.5 Correlation is Not Stable

Assumptions about correlation must be adjusted from time to time because correlations between asset groups **change over time**. Correlations between S&P 500 returns and US 10-year Treasury yields fluctuated between negative and positive territory from **1997 to 2016**.

### 8.4.6 Correlation Rises During Crisis

One concern about diversification is the potential for asset correlations to **move toward 1 during major financial crises**. Though rare, extremely high correlation occurred in the **2008 Global Financial Crisis**.

Systematic risk was higher because financial institutions and the entire banking sector were threatened. As credit dried up and redemptions became rampant, fund managers had to sell assets to meet redemptions. Even companies less related to the crisis had to sell strong-performing assets to cover margin positions on directly affected assets. The **contagion effect** rippled through global markets because counterparty risk was at an all-time high as institutions had to be bailed out. Almost all assets corrected downwards, and correlations gravitated toward 1. The silver lining was that correlation remained highly positive during the **2009 rebound**.

There was nowhere for funds to hide in 2008 except **cash and government bonds**. The reverse happened in 2009, with recovery across most asset classes except listed real estate, which took longer.

### 8.4.7 Calculating Portfolio Return and Risk

The expected return of a portfolio is a **weighted average** of the expected returns of its individual securities:

$$R_p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i$$

where $$R_p$$ is the expected portfolio return, $$w_i$$ the portfolio weight for security $$i$$, $$r_i$$ the expected return on security $$i$$, $$n$$ the number of securities, and $$\sum w_i = 1.0$$.

**Portfolio risk is NOT a weighted average** of the individual securities' risk. For two assets:

$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho_{1,2} \sigma_1 \sigma_2}$$

where $$w_1$$ and $$w_2$$ are the portfolio weights, $$\sigma_1$$ and $$\sigma_2$$ the standard deviations, and $$\rho_{1,2}$$ the correlation coefficient.

**Worked example.**

| Security | Expected return | Standard deviation | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 18% | 20% | 40% |
| B | 9% | 10% | 60% |

Correlation coefficient between A and B is **0.8**.

$$\text{Expected return} = (0.4)(0.18) + (0.6)(0.09) = 0.072 + 0.054 = 12.6\%$$

$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.4^2 \times 0.2^2 + 0.6^2 \times 0.1^2 + 2(0.4)(0.6)(0.8)(0.2)(0.1)} = \sqrt{0.0064 + 0.0036 + 0.00768} = \sqrt{0.01768} = 0.1330 = 13.30\%$$

**The effect of correlation:**

| Correlation | Portfolio risk |
|---|---|
| ρ = 1 | **14%** |
| ρ = 0 | **10%** |
| ρ = −1 | **2%** |

Portfolio risk falls steadily from **14% to 2%** as correlation declines from +1.0 to −1.0.

### 8.4.8 International Diversification

Portfolio risk can also be reduced through international diversification, because share price movements in different countries are fairly uncorrelated.

Inherent in international diversification is **foreign exchange risk**. One way to remove it is to **hedge using forward exchange contracts**.

International diversification does **not always** lead to higher risk-adjusted returns. It makes sense to add securities from new markets only if the additions have the potential to raise the portfolio's risk-adjusted return; adding the wrong securities and markets can **lower** it.

Top-down fundamental research on the economy, industry and company is important, as are reviews of political and currency risk. **Back-testing simulations** can show how securities or markets performed in different periods, including under stress.

### 8.4.9 The Capital Asset Pricing Model

Derived by **Sharpe, Lintner and Mossin** in the mid-1960s, CAPM describes the equilibrium relation between **systematic risk** and **expected return**.

**Assumptions:**

- Investors evaluate portfolios on the basis of risk and return.
- Investors are rational and maximise return for a given level of risk, and minimise risk for a given level of return.
- Individual assets are **infinitely divisible** — fractional shares can be bought or sold.
- Investors can **borrow or lend at the risk-free rate**.
- There are **no taxes or transaction costs**.
- There is **no inflation**, or any change in interest rates, or inflation is fully anticipated.
- All investors have the same **one-period time horizon**.
- Information is **freely and instantly available** to all investors.
- Investors have **homogeneous expectations** of risk, return and covariances.
- Capital markets are in **equilibrium**.

Because all investors have the same expectations, they arrive at the same optimal portfolio of risky assets — the **market portfolio (M)**. Being completely diversified, it comprises only systematic risk, which cannot be eliminated because it results from macroeconomic factors affecting all securities. All assets are included in portfolio M **in proportion to their market value**.

In theory the market portfolio should include all risky assets worldwide, financial and real. In practice the portfolio of all ordinary shares on the S&P 500 is used to represent the universe, with the **S&P 500 Composite Index** as the proxy.

CAPM introduces the **risk-free asset**, which has no risk and provides the risk-free rate ($$R_f$$). Its return variance is **zero** and its covariance with any risky asset is **zero**.

Two important relations follow:

- The **capital market line**, specifying the equilibrium relation between expected return and **total risk** for efficiently diversified portfolios.
- The **security market line**, specifying the equilibrium relation between expected return and **systematic risk**. It applies to individual securities as well as portfolios.

**a. Uses of the CAPM.** CAPM evaluates investments against the market benchmark risk-return relation to see whether an investment provides a fair return given its systematic risk. Despite its shortcomings it is still used to offer insight into the market risk-return relation and to determine the cost of capital for securities.

**b. The Capital Market Line.** The CML shows the risk-return trade-off for **efficient portfolios**. It is the efficient frontier formed from combinations of the market portfolio with the risk-free asset. In equilibrium all investors should hold a portfolio somewhere on the CML.

$$E(R_p) = R_f + \frac{E(R_m) - R_f}{\sigma_m} \sigma_p$$

where $$E(R_p)$$ is the expected return on any efficient portfolio P on the CML, $$R_f$$ the risk-free rate, $$E(R_m)$$ the expected return on the market portfolio, $$\sigma_m$$ the standard deviation of market returns, and $$\sigma_p$$ the standard deviation of portfolio P's returns. $$E(R_m) - R_f$$ is the **market risk premium**.

The **slope** of the CML, $$[E(R_m) - R_f]/\sigma_m$$, is the **market price of risk** for efficient portfolios: the additional return the market requires for a **1% increase** in the portfolio's standard deviation. The expected return for any portfolio on the CML equals the risk-free rate plus the product of the market price of risk and the quantity of portfolio risk.

**c. The Security Market Line.** While the CML applies only to efficient portfolios, the SML depicts the risk-return trade-off for **all assets**, including individual securities and inefficient portfolios.

The relative systematic risk of a security or portfolio is the volatility of its return relative to the market portfolio's volatility, multiplied by the correlation coefficient. This is **beta (β)**. The market portfolio's beta is **1**.

- **β > 1** — proportionately higher systematic risk than the market, requiring a higher expected return.
- **β < 1** — lower systematic risk than the market, with a lower expected return.

A security with beta **1.5** has, on average, **1.5 times** as much systematic risk as the market. A risk-free asset has a beta of **zero**, but an asset with beta zero need not be risk-free — it can have unsystematic risk; either way both should yield the risk-free rate.

$$E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i (R_m - R_f)$$

where $$E(R_i)$$ is the expected return on security or portfolio $$i$$, $$R_f$$ the risk-free rate, $$R_m$$ the expected market return, $$\beta_i$$ the systematic risk relative to the market, and $$R_m - R_f$$ the market risk premium.

Securities with betas greater than 1 should have larger risk premiums than the market and therefore require higher rates of return, because beta measures undiversifiable risk and greater systematic risk should be accompanied by greater expected return. Securities with betas below the market's are less risky and should have lower required returns.

**Worked example.** Growth Portfolio A has a beta of **1.20**, the risk-free rate is **2.5%** and the expected market return is **14%**:

$$E(r) = 2.5 + 1.2(14 - 2.5) = 16.3\%$$

**d. Generating positive alpha.** **Alpha** is the actual outperformance of the portfolio above what is predicted. If the actual return exceeds the predicted CAPM return, the manager is generating positive alpha — the ability to outperform the predicted CAPM return (or whatever model return is used as benchmark).

For Growth Portfolio A, an actual return of **17%** implies alpha of **17 − 16.3 = 0.7%**. At **15%**, alpha is **−1.3%**.

Investors should buy portfolios generating positive alpha and sell or short those generating negative alpha. Demand for positive alpha raises its price; as the price rises, expected return falls, reducing and ultimately eliminating the alpha that created the excess demand. Conversely, a drop in demand for a negative-alpha fund lowers its price and raises its expected return, pushing alpha back to zero. Such buying and selling pressure should leave most securities with **zero alpha most of the time**, and positive or negative alphas are unlikely to persist for many securities. **Unless analysis shows otherwise, alpha should be assumed to be zero.**

**e. Overvalued and undervalued securities.** The SML identifies over- and undervaluation.

- Securities **above** the SML provide **above-average expected returns** for their betas and are **under-priced**.
- Securities **below** the SML offer **lower returns** than predicted for their risk and are **overpriced**.

In a perfectly efficient market all securities should lie **on** the SML. If they do not, the market mechanism forces them back: a security above the SML attracts demand, pushing its price up and reducing its expected return; a security below attracts selling, pushing its price down and raising its expected return. Buying and selling continues until equilibrium, when all securities lie on the SML.

**f. Expensive or cheap markets.** The SML also reflects the value of markets at a point in time.

- A **steep** SML indicates a large incremental return for a small increase in systematic risk. The market may be **excessively risk-averse** and therefore **cheap**.
- A **flat** SML indicates little incremental return for additional systematic risk. The market appears to be **ignoring risk** and may be **overvalued**.

### 8.4.10 Arbitrage Pricing Theory

A main criticism of CAPM is the **inability to observe or properly measure the return on the market portfolio**, which consists of all risky assets. Moreover, CAPM's assumptions are largely unrealistic. CAPM is essentially a **single-factor model** where price fluctuations are explained only by the market factor.

In response, **Stephen Ross** developed the **Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)** in the early 1970s — a **multifactor equilibrium pricing model**.

APT shares these assumptions with CAPM:

- Investors have **homogeneous beliefs**.
- Investors are **risk-averse utility maximisers**.
- Markets are **perfect**, and frictions such as transaction costs are irrelevant.
- Returns are generated by a **factor model**.

But APT does **not** assume:

- A single-period investment horizon.
- Absence of taxes.
- Borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate.
- Investors selecting portfolios on the basis of expected return and variance.

A factor model holds that underlying **risk factors** affect realised and expected returns. These are not firm-specific factors but broad economic forces with three characteristics:

- The risk factor must have a **pervasive influence** on share returns. Firm-specific events are not APT risk factors.
- The risk factor must **influence expected return**.
- At the beginning of each period the risk factor must be **unpredictable by the market as a whole**. The **rate of inflation is not an APT risk factor** because it is partially predictable; **unexpected inflation** — the difference between actual and expected inflation — **is**.

Economic factors might include real economic growth, unexpected inflation, changes in interest rates and political uncertainties.

$$R_i = E(R_i) + b_{i1}f_1 + b_{i2}f_2 + \dots + b_{in}f_n + e_i$$

where $$R_i$$ is the actual return on security $$i$$ in a period, $$E(R_i)$$ the expected return, $$f$$ the deviation of a systematic factor from its expected value, $$b_i$$ the sensitivity of security $$i$$'s returns to a factor, and $$e_i$$ a random error term unique to security $$i$$.

The actual return equals the expected return if the factors are at their expected levels and the error term is zero.

**The problem with APT** is that the factors are **not well specified**, at least for the future. Most empirical studies suggest **three to five factors** influence security returns. **Chen, Roll and Ross (1986)** identified four systematic factors:

- Unanticipated changes in **inflation**.
- Unanticipated changes in **industrial production**.
- Unanticipated changes in the **default-risk premium**.
- Unanticipated changes in the **term structure of interest rates**.

Investors can better understand returns by identifying the risk factors affecting asset returns. A manager can structure a portfolio to increase exposure to one or more factors, or hedge against them.

## 8.5 Portfolio Management Services

Portfolio management services vary by client type.

**Dedicated and personalised services** are available to institutional funds, sovereign wealth funds, corporate funds and funds of accredited individual investors. With dedicated services, funds (for example **USD50m AUM**) may be separately managed from other funds, and the manager provides regular monthly updates and one-to-one performance reviews.

**Retail investors**, who generally have smaller amounts, can use unit trust funds traded on global exchanges and private funds sold by banks. **Berkshire Hathaway** is a notable listed investment company solely involved in managing investments. Some funds are country-specific, others offer regional or global diversification; there are also sector-specific and thematic funds. By pooling investor monies, retail investors benefit from diversification through economies of scale and shared resources.

### 8.5.1 Investment Approach and Style

Portfolio management services are not suitable for all investors. Some do not trust anyone else to manage their funds and prefer a hands-on approach. Others do not wish to incur management fees, which average **1.0%–1.5%**. Some prefer a hybrid approach: managers run the core conservatively while the investor directly manages a portion.

Regardless of preference, both individual and institutional investors benefit from a **disciplined** portfolio management approach.

| Clients who self-manage | Clients with both self-managed and professionally managed | Clients who prefer professional management |
|---|---|---|
| Likes trading ideas and strategies | Manages some investments directly and trades occasionally | No time for dedicated focus on the portfolio |
| Wants good research | Has a proportion of funds managed externally | Wants investments professionally managed in a portfolio approach |
| Believes in their own ability | May use external managers as well as trade directly | Wants to minimise emotional influence |
| Wants a cost-effective, efficient execution platform | Long-term oriented but will take short-term opportunities | Willing to trust external professionals over the longer term |
| Has time to manage own portfolio | | |
| Can trade listed diversified and thematic funds | | |

**a. Advisory portfolio management.** For investors who wish to remain actively engaged in decisions. Banks provide such services only for **affluent (accredited) clients** willing to place significant amounts — for example **USD10 million** — to justify the added expertise and resources. Less affluent clients rely on their relationship manager or internet portal for research, advice and asset allocation recommendations. At the lowest end, retail banking clients may receive no support services when purchasing funds.

**b. Discretionary portfolio management.** For most investors buying portfolio investment mandates, the general practice is to give the fund manager **discretion**. The manager decides asset allocation and securities selection **without obtaining permission for each transaction**, guided by the client's investment profile and policy statement.

**The portfolio management process** has three layers:

- **Style of management** — quantitative vs qualitative; fundamental vs technical; use of derivatives and hedging, currency overlay, alternative investments; use of computer models and trading algorithms; in-house vs external research.
- **Management process** — active management vs rigid model limits; asset allocation discipline; the amount of cash held on average and in crisis; policy on redemptions; rebalancing frequency.
- **Audit and governance** — resource capabilities; performance review and reward; performance attribution; fees for performance.

These sit between **economic and market conditions** (what the markets will allow) and **client objectives and specifications** (what the client wants). The process runs: research outlook → expected return and risk targets for the model portfolio → portfolio construction (strategic asset allocation, securities selection, implementation and execution, currency overlay or hedging) → create the **investment policy statement** → portfolio monitoring and rebalancing (tactical asset allocation, profit taking and risk management) → performance measurement and review (against client expectations, market benchmark, peer managers, and performance attribution) → performance reporting and review with the client.

### 8.5.2 Portfolio Management Process for Retail Investors

Portfolio management evaluates investment risk and return to construct an optimal portfolio, taking account of the investor's objectives, risk profile, and any unique constraints and circumstances.

Understanding personal goals helps the representative determine objectives, identify a suitable fund or service, and recommend strategies. Conservative strategies may suit money set aside for retirement or children's education. Individual clients generally have **shorter time horizons** than institutional clients, but an investor setting aside dedicated funds through a trust for grandchildren may have a long enough horizon for the higher volatility of a growth-oriented fund.

The process for individual investors is **dynamic, continuous and systematic**:

- **Setting investment objectives** — analyse objectives, requirements, preferences and constraints to define an acceptable level of risk and a reasonable expected return for that risk.
- **Developing and implementing strategies** — set guidelines for asset allocation and define the circumstances in which the allocation may vary.
- **Monitoring market conditions** — track market conditions and changing relative values, and periodically re-evaluate the investor's needs, circumstances and objectives.
- **Reviewing and adjusting the portfolio** — make adjustments to reflect changes in the market or the investor's requirements.
- **Measuring portfolio performance** — measure performance and evaluate it relative to a benchmark.

Objectives, constraints and strategy should always be stated in a **written document**. A well-drafted investment policy provides the discipline for efficient management, minimising **whipsaw reactions** to temporary price swings or protracted volatility.

### 8.5.3 Determinants of the Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

The IPS, or **investment mandate**, **belongs to the client, not the portfolio manager**. The manager must therefore spend sufficient time with the client to define investment goals and acceptable risk levels, and to note constraints and preferences.

The IPS typically covers:

- **Client description and circumstances.**
- **Investment objectives** — return requirement and risk tolerance.
- **Investment constraints** — liquidity, time horizon, tax, regulatory and legal, and unique needs or preferences.
- **Asset allocation** — strategic asset allocation ranges, and a rebalancing policy. Investors often specify a **policy portfolio** (conservative, balanced or aggressive) as part of their objectives.

### 8.5.4 Investment Portfolio Objectives — Risk and Return Requirements

In defining objectives, emphasis should be placed on determining the client's **sensitivity to risk**. Return requirement and risk tolerance are usually established through an interview and discussion session, and a **risk profiling questionnaire**.

Understanding the client's profile goes beyond regulatory compliance: it embodies taking a genuine interest in the client's needs and financial well-being.

**The life cycle approach** is a useful framework for analysing an individual's risk appetite.

| Stage | Circumstances | Appropriate risk |
|---|---|---|
| **1st — early career** | Priorities are saving for liquidity, buying a home, obtaining life insurance, then investments. A large housing loan and possibly a car loan mean assets are small relative to liabilities. But the individual is young with a long horizon and a growing stream of discretionary income | Can undertake **high return, high risk, growth-oriented** investments |
| **2nd — mid-career** | Can establish a serious investment programme; liabilities substantially lower. The horizon remains long but not so long that capital preservation is unimportant | Can continue high risk, high return growth investments, but should **reduce overall risk exposure** |
| **3rd — late career** | Near retirement, with greater emphasis on income needs and capital preservation. Assets significantly exceed liabilities; the horizon is shorter | Shift to **significantly lower return, lower risk** assets with large dividend or interest income and relatively secure asset values |

Each individual's risk tolerance is unique. The risk profile is also affected by net worth, health, family situation, future financial commitments, familiarity with investments, job stability and emotional temperament.

### 8.5.5 Investment Constraints

**a. Liquidity requirements.** These vary considerably and must be clearly communicated. If funds are for the next mortgage payment or the coming semester's tuition, **immediate liquidity is essential** and financial assets are preferred. Such needs should be met with high-quality, short-term debt instruments that can be easily sold — **Treasury bills and commercial paper**. If assets held have poor marketability, such as real estate or shares in a closely held business, liquidity needs must be monitored carefully.

In practice few investment companies hold a liquidity reserve, because most financial assets are highly liquid: shares and bonds can generally be sold within minutes at a price close to market value. Shortfalls are normally met by selling financial assets.

**b. Time horizon.** The investment period, which affects the nature of investments selected and the strategies employed. Investors with a **long** horizon can absorb and smooth out the ups and downs of risky assets such as ordinary share portfolios, and can build riskier portfolios. Investors with a **short** horizon tend to have less volatile portfolios, typically more bonds than shares, with shorter duration bonds and lower-volatility shares.

**c. Tax considerations.** The manager must consider:

- The client's **income tax bracket**.
- Different tax treatments of different assets (tax-exempt or not).
- Different components of total return (dividend or interest income versus capital gains).

Investors in **high tax brackets** should consider tax-exempt fixed income securities if the **taxable equivalent return** exceeds the return on comparable taxable bonds. For such investors, equities with a large **capital gain** component may be more attractive than high dividend income-yielding shares.

**d. Regulatory and legal constraints.** Institutional investors such as insurance companies are regulated with regard to their investments, and are generally more regulated than private individuals. An individual using personal savings has greater freedom, but if using **CPF** money the investment is governed by the **CPF Investment Scheme**, which specifies the types and quantum of permissible investments.

**e. Clients' unique needs or preferences.** It is the **fiduciary duty** of fund managers to ensure client instructions are adhered to. Some clients specify a limit on equity investments or foreign currency exposure. These preferences and instructions must be clearly spelt out in the investment policy **in writing** to avoid misunderstandings.

### 8.5.6 Asset Allocation

The purpose of asset allocation is to **maximise portfolio return at a level of risk consistent with the investor's objective**. Whether asset allocation matters more than securities selection has been vigorously debated. A study by **Brinson, Hood and Beebower** showed that **91.5% or more** of a portfolio's return comes from asset allocation, leaving **less than 10%** attributable to security selection. Asset allocation also has a much greater impact on reducing total risk exposure than picking an investment in any single asset category.

At the asset allocation stage, economists and market strategists provide input on macroeconomic expectations and market trends, because these affect the returns of the various asset classes.

**a. Strategic asset allocation (SAA).** How a portfolio's funds are divided among asset classes, given the client's risk profile and risk-return requirements, and the manager's long-term forecasts of expected returns, variances and covariances. SAA is the **"policy" or "long-run" asset mix**, normally expressed as percentages of total portfolio value.

| Asset class | Allocation range |
|---|---|
| Equities | 45–55% |
| Bonds | 35–45% |
| Cash equivalents | 5–15% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

Such an approach is a **constant-mix strategy**. Maximum and minimum limits can be set for each asset class to accommodate different risk profiles and market conditions. Permissible asset classes must be clearly defined by the nature of the assets (cash, bonds, shares) and by sector, industry and geographical region or country, with possible sub-categories.

The optimal asset mix depends on the investor's return objectives and risk tolerance: **the lower the tolerance for risk, the more conservative the asset mix**. A conservative allocation relies heavily on bonds and short-term securities for stable returns, emphasising current income and capital preservation. An aggressive portfolio seeking capital gains has the highest exposure to ordinary shares and less to bonds and short-term securities, increasing expected return and risk.

SAA is not expected to change often. But for an individual, the long-run attitude to risk can change — through family circumstances, health, business circumstances, or a negative impact on wealth that makes the client much more risk averse. The SAA should then be adjusted. **Annual reviews** should check whether the client's view of risk-taking has changed.

**b. Tactical asset allocation (TAA).** For a given SAA mix, the manager is typically allowed to reduce or increase the allocation to an asset class to exploit market opportunities, **as long as the allocation stays within a certain range**. TAA is action taken to **overweight or underweight** asset classes over the **short run** in response to changing economic and market conditions, to optimise returns given perceived risks. It also seeks to exploit relative inefficiencies in security prices across asset classes.

**Worked example.** If the outlook for equities is favourable:

| Asset class | SAA range | Original TAA | New TAA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 45–55% | 45% | **55%** |
| Bonds | 35–45% | 50% | **40%** |
| Cash equivalents | 5–15% | 5% | 5% |
| **Total** | | **100%** | **100%** |

TAA models fall into three groups: **valuation approaches**, **cyclical considerations**, and a combination.

- The **valuation approach** identifies the relative value of an asset class: expensive classes are sold and cheap ones bought. The most common is the **risk premium or spread approach**: if the expected return on equities relative to bonds is **above average**, allocate more to equities; if **below average**, allocate less. This is the **equity-bond yield gap**.
- The **cyclical consideration approach** holds that cycles in share and bond prices are closely related through macroeconomic factors. Equity market movements typically **precede** changes in economic activity, so managers try to identify components of economic activity that are good predictors of market movements.

## 8.6 Performance Measurement

Two questions must be answered:

- How well or badly is the manager performing relative to a benchmark?
- What factors contributed to the success or failure of the portfolio?

### 8.6.1 Measuring Portfolio Returns

A proper measure of total return captures both the **income** and the **capital gain or loss** component. Performance is typically measured over a regular period, monthly or quarterly. Return measurement is simple with no cash flow movements, and more complicated when the client deposits or withdraws.

**a. No deposits or withdrawals.**

$$R_p = \frac{V_E - V_B}{V_B}$$

where $$V_E$$ is the end value and $$V_B$$ the beginning value.

**Worked example.** A portfolio worth **$30 million** at the start of the quarter and **$35 million** at the end:

$$R_p = \frac{35 - 30}{30} = 16.7\%$$

**b. Deposits or withdrawals at the end of the period.** The deposit must be **deducted from the ending value** first.

$$R_p = \frac{(V_E + \text{Withdrawals} - \text{Deposits}) - V_B}{V_B}$$

**Worked example.** A portfolio worth **$50 million** at the start; the client deposits **$5 million** just before the end of the quarter; the end value is **$53 million**:

$$R_p = \frac{(53 - 5) - 50}{50} = -4.0\%$$

**c. Deposits or withdrawals at the beginning of the period.** Adjust the **beginning value**: increase it by a deposit, decrease it by a withdrawal.

$$R_p = \frac{V_E - (V_B + \text{Deposits} - \text{Withdrawals})}{V_B + \text{Deposits} - \text{Withdrawals}}$$

**Worked example.** If the $5 million deposit was received at the **beginning** of the quarter:

$$R_p = \frac{53 - (50 + 5)}{50 + 5} = -3.6\%$$

**d. Dollar-weighted rate of return (DWR).** When cash flows occur **between** the beginning and end of the period, the DWR can be used. It is equivalent to the **internal rate of return**: it equates the present value of all cash flows, including the ending market value, with the beginning market value.

**Worked example.** If the $5 million deposit was made in the **middle** of the quarter, solve for $$r$$:

$$50 = \frac{-5}{(1+r)} + \frac{53}{(1+r)^2}$$

$$r = -1.92\%$$ (semi-quarterly return)

The quarterly return is $$(1 - 0.0192)^2 - 1 = -3.8\%$$. In the first half the loss was 1.92%, leaving 0.9808 of the original value; in the second half there is another 1.92% loss on that 0.9808, so $$(0.9808)^2$$ of the original remains.

**e. Time-weighted rate of return (TWR).** An alternative when cash flows occur within the period. Calculating TWR requires the portfolio value **before each cash flow**.

**Worked example.**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Portfolio value at beginning of quarter | $50 million |
| Portfolio value just before deposit | $49 million |
| Deposit in middle of quarter | $5 million |
| Portfolio value immediately after deposit | $54 million |
| Portfolio value at end of quarter | $53 million |

First half: $$(49 - 50)/50 = -2.0\%$$. Second half: $$(53 - 54)/54 = -1.85\%$$.

Quarterly return by linking: $$[(1 - 0.02)(1 - 0.0185)] - 1 = -3.81\%$$.

With frequent cash flows, many calculations may be needed to derive the TWR.

**f. Annualising returns.** From quarterly returns:

$$\text{Annual Return} = [(1 + r_1)(1 + r_2)(1 + r_3)(1 + r_4)] - 1$$

Over several years, the annualised geometric mean return — the **compound annual growth rate (CAGR)** — is used.

**Worked example.** A portfolio grew from **$100 to $250 in 5 years**:

$$250 = 100(1 + r)^5 \implies (1 + r)^5 = 2.5 \implies r = 20.1\%$$

**g. Dollar-weighted versus time-weighted return.** The two methods can produce different results, sometimes substantially. The **DWR is inappropriate for evaluating a portfolio manager**, because it is strongly influenced by the **size and timing of cash flows**, over which the manager has no control. The **TWR is more appropriate**, because the objective is to measure the manager's performance independent of the client's actions. Fund managers are evaluated using the TWR because they have little or no control over inflows and outflows.

**h. Modified Dietz method.** A true TWR requires valuing the portfolio just before each cash flow. In practice cash flows often occur intra-month when valuations are unavailable. The Dietz method overcomes this by assuming a **constant rate of return** during the period. The original Dietz method assumes all cash flows occur at the **midpoint**; the **modified Dietz** method weights each cash flow by the amount of time it is held.

$$R_p = \frac{V_E - V_B - CF}{V_B + WCF}$$

where $$CF$$ is the net cash flow over the period and $$WCF$$ the weighted cash flow:

$$WCF = \sum_i CF_i \frac{n - t_i}{n}$$

where $$CF_i$$ is the cash flow on day $$t_i$$, $$t_i$$ the number of days from the beginning of the period until $$CF_i$$, and $$n$$ the number of days in the period. The numerator assumes the cash flow occurs at the **end of the day**; if assumed at the beginning, use $$(n - t_i + 1)$$.

**Worked example.** A portfolio worth **$10 million** at the start of a month. Cash inflows of **$100,000 on day 12** and **$200,000 on day 18**. End-of-month value **$11 million**.

$$WCF = 100{,}000 \times \frac{30 - 12}{30} + 200{,}000 \times \frac{30 - 18}{30} = 60{,}000 + 80{,}000 = 140{,}000$$

$$R_p = \frac{11{,}000{,}000 - 10{,}000{,}000 - 300{,}000}{10{,}000{,}000 + 140{,}000} = 6.90\%$$

**Advantage:** no portfolio valuation is needed on the date of each cash flow. **Disadvantage:** it is a **less accurate** estimate of the true TWR, and can be affected by cash flows, especially when they are large relative to the portfolio or occur during periods of high volatility.

**i. Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS).** Before **February 2005**, the Association of Investment Management and Research (AIMR) issued minimum standards for presenting portfolio performance. On **4 February 2005** the CFA Institute Board of Governors approved the revised GIPS, creating a **single global standard** for performance reporting and raising minimum standards worldwide. GIPS replaced country-specific standards and are widely accepted internationally.

Fundamental principles:

- **Total return**, accounting for realised and unrealised capital gains and income, must be used.
- **TWR must be used.**
- Portfolios must be valued (marked to market) **at least monthly**, and returns **geometrically linked** — CAGR for annualised returns.
- Performance must be calculated **after deducting actual trading expenses** (brokerage, commissions).
- Dividends, interest income and other time-based income must be **accrued**.
- **Annual returns for all years must be shown.** If the firm is more than **10 years old**, a **10-year** record must be presented; otherwise, the record since inception.
- **Disclose** whether results are gross or net of investment management fees.
- The use of **leverage and derivatives** must be expressly disclosed.

### 8.6.2 Risk-Adjusted Measures of Performance

Evaluating a manager on returns alone is inadequate without considering the risk taken. Three generally recognised methods of measuring risk-adjusted return — the **composite measures** of portfolio performance:

**a. Sharpe ratio.** Developed by William Sharpe, it relates the portfolio's **excess return** (return above the risk-free rate, also called the risk premium) to the portfolio's **total risk**, measured by standard deviation.

$$\text{Sharpe ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$

where $$R_p$$ is the average portfolio return, $$R_f$$ the risk-free rate, and $$\sigma_p$$ the standard deviation of portfolio returns.

The question it answers: "How much return above the risk-free rate am I getting for the portfolio volatility I am bearing?" It is the excess return per unit of **total risk**. **The higher the Sharpe ratio, the better the performance.**

**Worked example.** Risk-free rate **6%**.

| Portfolio | Annual return | Standard deviation | Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.10 | 0.15 | (0.10 − 0.06)/0.15 = **0.27** |
| B | 0.16 | 0.20 | (0.16 − 0.06)/0.20 = **0.50** |
| Market | 0.13 | 0.18 | (0.13 − 0.06)/0.18 = **0.39** |

Portfolio A has the lowest excess return per unit of total risk and underperforms both B and the market. Portfolio B performs better than A and the market.

**b. Treynor ratio.** Relates the same average excess return to **systematic risk**, measured by the portfolio's beta.

$$\text{Treynor ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}$$

This is the excess return per unit of **systematic risk**. The Treynor measure implies systematic risk is the proper measure for evaluating performance. The question it answers: "How much return above the risk-free rate am I getting for the market sensitivity the portfolio exposes me to, as captured by β?"

**Worked example.** Risk-free rate **6%**.

| Portfolio | Annual return | Beta | Treynor |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.14 | 0.9 | (0.14 − 0.06)/0.9 = **8.9%** |
| B | 0.18 | 1.1 | (0.18 − 0.06)/1.1 = **10.9%** |
| Market | 0.20 | 1.0 | (0.20 − 0.06)/1.0 = **14%** |

**c. Sharpe versus Treynor.** The main difference is the **definition of risk**: Sharpe uses **total risk** (standard deviation), Treynor uses **systematic risk** (beta).

If portfolios are **perfectly diversified** — correlation between portfolio and market returns is **1.0** — the two measures give **identical rankings**, because total risk is only systematic risk once all unsystematic risk is diversified away. As portfolios become **less diversified**, the possibility of differences in rankings increases.

The Sharpe measure therefore accounts for the extent of diversification during the measurement period. If a portfolio is **inadequately diversified**, its ranking using the **Treynor** ratio is likely to be **higher** than with the Sharpe measure, because Treynor ignores non-systematic risk, which is present in an inadequately diversified portfolio.

**d. Jensen's alpha.** Based on CAPM, measuring risk-adjusted return as alpha. The Jensen measure uses CAPM to determine the expected return on a portfolio given the market's performance, the risk-free rate and the portfolio's beta:

$$R_e = R_f + \beta_p (R_m - R_f)$$

The portfolio's alpha is the difference between the actual return and the CAPM-expected return:

$$\alpha_p = R_{\text{actual}} - R_{\text{predicted}} = R_{\text{actual}} - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)]$$

A **positive alpha** reflects superior performance; a **negative alpha** inferior performance.

**e. Information (appraisal) ratio.** Looks at the amount of excess return relative to the excess risk. When comparing two portfolios with positive alpha, it compares alpha per unit of excess risk. A higher information ratio suggests the manager generates more outperformance per unit of excess risk taken.

$$\text{Information ratio} = \frac{\alpha_p}{\sigma_p}$$

The higher the information ratio, the better.

**f. Sortino ratio.** The Sharpe ratio applies to portfolios whose returns are expected to be **normally distributed**, and includes both positive and negative deviation in the standard deviation. But when managers protect downside risk with derivatives, short securities and the market, and enjoy the effects of leverage on positive volatility, they can create **positively skewed** portfolios. It would be unfair to penalise a manager for high positive deviation and a positively skewed distribution. The Sortino ratio therefore focuses on **downside risk**, with the denominator being an average of **downside volatility** for the period. This is particularly useful for comparing **hedge funds**.

$$\text{Sortino ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{negative}}}$$

The Sortino ratio calculates the return above the risk-free rate per unit of **negative volatility**.

### 8.6.3 Performance Attribution

Performance attribution determines **why** a manager did better or worse than a properly constructed benchmark. The purpose is to decompose total performance into specific components associated with specific decisions.

Attribution often begins from the broadest asset allocation choices and progressively focuses on finer details. The difference between the managed and benchmark portfolio performance can be expressed as the sum of contributions from decisions made at each level of portfolio construction. One common system decomposes relative performance into three components:

- **Asset allocation** choices across equity, fixed income and money markets.
- **Industry (sector) choice** within each market.
- **Security choice** within each sector.

Part of the analysis is identifying a performance benchmark — the **bogey** — designed to measure returns if a **passive** strategy were adopted. Weights assigned to the bogey depend on the investor's risk tolerance. Any difference between the portfolio's results and the passive benchmark must be due to **asset allocation bets** or **security selection bets**.

**a. Characteristics of good benchmarks.** Useful benchmarks should be:

- **Unambiguous** — the asset class, securities and composition clearly stated.
- **Investable** — practically and realistically investable as a passively managed portfolio.
- **Measurable** — returns obtainable from the market and performance measurable.
- **Consistent** with the investment manager's philosophy.
- **Bought in** by the manager as an acceptable benchmark.

**Worked example — bogey portfolio.**

| Managed portfolio | Weight | Actual return | Weighted return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 0.70 | 7.50% | 5.25% |
| Bonds | 0.10 | 2.00% | 0.20% |
| Cash | 0.20 | 0.50% | 0.10% |
| | | | **5.55%** |

| Bogey portfolio | Benchmark weight | Benchmark return | Weighted return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 0.60 | 6.00% | 3.60% |
| Bonds | 0.30 | 1.50% | 0.45% |
| Cash | 0.10 | 0.50% | 0.05% |
| | | | **4.10%** |

**Excess return = 5.55% − 4.10% = 1.45%.**

**b. Asset allocation decision.** Compare the weights of the managed and bogey portfolios, multiplied by the **benchmark** return:

| Asset class | Actual weight | Benchmark weight | Excess weight | Benchmark return | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 0.70 | 0.60 | 0.10 | 6.00% | **0.60%** |
| Bonds | 0.10 | 0.30 | −0.20 | 1.50% | **−0.30%** |
| Cash | 0.20 | 0.10 | 0.10 | 0.50% | **0.05%** |
| | | | | | **0.35%** |

Asset allocation contributed **0.35%** of the 1.45% excess return. The major factor was the higher equity weighting, which provided a 6% return.

**c. Sector and security selection.** The remaining **1.10%** is attributed to selection, using the **actual** weights and the **excess** returns:

| Asset class | Actual return | Benchmark return | Excess return | Actual weight | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 7.50% | 6.00% | 1.50% | 0.70 | **1.05%** |
| Bonds | 2.00% | 1.50% | 0.50% | 0.10 | **0.05%** |
| | | | | | **1.10%** |

**Summary:**

| Component | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Asset allocation | **0.35%** |
| Sector and security selection — equities | 1.05% |
| Sector and security selection — bonds | 0.05% |
| **Selection subtotal** | **1.10%** |
| **Excess return of portfolio** | **1.45%** |

## 8.7 Portfolio Management and Fund Sales

Depending on whether the representative works in a fund management team, a bank, an insurer or another segment, fund or portfolio sales and after-care may be integral to fund management or a separate function. Operationally there will be differences, but there must be proper communication between portfolio managers and the originators of products and services.

The sales force must ensure the right message is conveyed and that there is **no misrepresentation** about:

- What the portfolio is targeting to do — what it can and cannot do.
- The underlying assets and instruments used.
- The risks the client would be allowing the fund manager to assume.
- The level of service and after-care rendered by portfolio managers and service teams.
- Reliance on third-party vendors who receive payments, such as **retrocessions**, for promoting the fund.

Often institutions have strong expertise within the portfolio management team but not within the sales force. **Mis-selling** can result from poor communication and a lack of enforcement of required practices when marketing portfolio management services.

## 8.8 Summary

**Efficient Market Hypothesis**

- **Weak form** — all past price information is factored into the current price; past price and volume data have no relation to future direction; technical analysis cannot consistently produce excess returns.
- **Semi-strong form** — all available market and non-market public information is factored in; neither fundamental nor technical analysis reliably produces excess returns.
- **Strong form** — all market, non-market and inside information is factored in and no one has monopolistic access; no one can earn excess returns.

**Passive versus active**

- EMH subscribers favour **passive** management such as buy-and-hold, believing it produces results as good as active management over the long term.
- Non-subscribers favour **active** management seeking to outperform on a risk-adjusted basis, believing they have superior analytical skills or better information access. Examples: security selection, sector rotation, market timing.

**Portfolio theory**

- Portfolio risk is reduced as the number of securities increases.
- Two sources of risk: **systematic**, attributed to macroeconomic factors and not eliminable by diversification; **non-systematic** or firm-specific, controllable and minimisable through diversification.
- Correlation between assets changes over time, and during major crises correlations between financial asset classes **rise**.
- For successful diversification, seek securities that are **uncorrelated**; otherwise risk cannot be significantly reduced. Portfolio risk is lowest when returns are **negatively correlated**.
- Portfolio expected return is a weighted average of constituent returns, but portfolio risk is **not** a weighted average of constituent risks.
- The **efficient frontier** represents optimal portfolios; adding less-correlated assets can shift it toward the upper left.
- **CAPM** relates expected return to systematic risk through beta. The **CML** applies to efficient portfolios and uses total risk; the **SML** applies to all assets and uses beta.
- **APT** is a multifactor model that does not require a single-period horizon, absence of taxes, risk-free borrowing and lending, or mean-variance portfolio selection.
- The **IPS** belongs to the client and records objectives, constraints and asset allocation.
- **SAA** is the long-run policy mix; **TAA** is short-run overweighting and underweighting within SAA ranges.
- **DWR** is the IRR and is influenced by cash flow size and timing; **TWR** is preferred for evaluating managers.
- **Modified Dietz** approximates TWR without needing valuations at each cash flow.
- **GIPS** requires total return, TWR, at least monthly valuation, geometric linking, and disclosure of fees, leverage and derivatives.
- **Sharpe** uses total risk; **Treynor** uses systematic risk; **Jensen's alpha** is the excess over the CAPM-predicted return.
- **Performance attribution** decomposes excess return into asset allocation and selection components against a bogey.

## Exam cues

- **Three EMH forms:** weak (past prices), semi-strong (all public information), strong (all information including private). Evidence supports weak and semi-strong; strong form is **not** supported, which is why insider trading is regulated.
- **Weak form implies technical analysis fails; semi-strong implies fundamental analysis also fails; strong implies even insider information fails.**
- **The average fund manager underperforms the benchmark even before fees**, and outperformance does not persist.
- **Passive** suits EMH believers: buy-and-hold or index funds, judged on **tracking** the index, with minimal expenses.
- **Active** strategies: **security selection** (undervalued, low P/E), **sector rotation** (10 industries; interest-sensitive, cyclical, defensive), **market timing** (active asset allocation).
- **Efficient frontier:** highest return for a given risk. Portfolios **below** the frontier are inefficient. Adding less-correlated assets shifts the frontier **up and to the left**.
- **Diversification:** about **half** of portfolio standard deviation is eliminated with **10 securities**; benefits then diminish. A market index eliminates all non-systematic risk.
- **Total risk = systematic + non-systematic.** Systematic cannot be diversified but can be **hedged** with options and futures. Non-systematic can be reduced to **zero** by diversification.
- **Correlation:** ρ = +1 gives **no** risk reduction (portfolio risk is the weighted average); ρ = 0 gives **some**; ρ = −1 can **eliminate** risk. Correlation is **not stable** and **rises toward 1 in a crisis**.
- **Portfolio return** is a weighted average; **portfolio risk is not**. Two-asset formula: $$\sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$.
- **Portfolio worked example:** A (18%, 20%, 40%) and B (9%, 10%, 60%), ρ = 0.8 → return **12.6%**, risk **13.30%**. At ρ = 1 risk is **14%**; at ρ = 0 it is **10%**; at ρ = −1 it is **2%**.
- **International diversification** reduces risk because country returns are fairly uncorrelated, but introduces **FX risk**, which can be hedged with forwards. It does **not always** raise risk-adjusted return.
- **CAPM assumptions (ten):** risk-return evaluation, rationality, infinite divisibility, risk-free borrowing and lending, no taxes or transaction costs, no inflation or fully anticipated, single-period horizon, free and instant information, homogeneous expectations, equilibrium.
- **Market portfolio M** contains all assets in proportion to market value and has only systematic risk. In practice the **S&P 500** is the proxy.
- **CML:** $$E(R_p) = R_f + [(E(R_m) - R_f)/\sigma_m]\sigma_p$$. Applies to **efficient portfolios** only; uses **total risk**. Slope is the **market price of risk**.
- **SML:** $$E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i(R_m - R_f)$$. Applies to **all assets**; uses **beta**. Market beta = **1**.
- **CAPM worked example:** β = 1.20, $$R_f$$ = 2.5%, $$R_m$$ = 14% → **16.3%**. Actual 17% → alpha **+0.7%**; actual 15% → alpha **−1.3%**.
- **Above the SML = under-priced (undervalued); below the SML = overpriced (overvalued).** In equilibrium all securities lie on the SML.
- **Steep SML = cheap market** (large return for small risk increase); **flat SML = overvalued market** (risk being ignored).
- **Alpha should be assumed zero** unless analysis shows otherwise, because arbitrage eliminates it.
- **APT:** multifactor, by **Stephen Ross**, early 1970s. Does **not** assume a single-period horizon, no taxes, risk-free borrowing and lending, or mean-variance selection. Factors must be **pervasive**, **influence expected return**, and be **unpredictable**. **Unexpected** inflation is a factor; the inflation **rate** is not.
- **Chen, Roll and Ross (1986)** four factors: unanticipated inflation, industrial production, default-risk premium, term structure.
- **APT's weakness:** the factors are **not well specified**; empirical work suggests **three to five** factors.
- **IPS belongs to the client**, not the manager. It records objectives (return requirement, risk tolerance) and constraints (liquidity, time horizon, tax, regulatory and legal, unique needs).
- **Life cycle:** early career — high risk growth; mid-career — still growth but reduce risk; late career — lower risk, income and capital preservation.
- **Liquidity needs** are met with **T-bills and commercial paper**. **CPF** money is governed by the CPF Investment Scheme.
- **Asset allocation explains 91.5% or more** of portfolio return (Brinson, Hood and Beebower); security selection less than **10%**.
- **SAA** is the long-run policy mix, a **constant-mix** strategy, e.g. equities 45–55%, bonds 35–45%, cash 5–15%. **TAA** overweights and underweights within those ranges.
- **TAA approaches:** valuation (risk premium or **equity-bond yield gap**) and cyclical (equity markets lead economic activity).
- **Return with no cash flow:** $$(V_E - V_B)/V_B$$. Worked: 30 → 35 = **16.7%**.
- **Deposit at end of period:** deduct from the ending value. Worked: 50 → 53 with a 5 deposit = **−4.0%**.
- **Deposit at start of period:** adjust the beginning value. Worked: **−3.6%**.
- **DWR = IRR.** Worked: mid-quarter deposit → semi-quarterly **−1.92%**, quarterly **−3.8%**.
- **TWR** links sub-period returns. Worked: −2.0% and −1.85% → **−3.81%**.
- **CAGR:** 100 → 250 in 5 years = **20.1%**.
- **DWR is inappropriate for judging managers** because it is driven by client cash flows; **TWR is preferred**.
- **Modified Dietz:** $$R_p = (V_E - V_B - CF)/(V_B + WCF)$$, with $$WCF = \sum CF_i(n - t_i)/n$$. Worked: WCF **140,000**, return **6.90%**.
- **GIPS:** approved **4 February 2005**; requires **total return**, **TWR**, **monthly** valuation, geometric linking, after-trading-expense returns, accrued income, **10-year** record for firms over 10 years old, and disclosure of fees, leverage and derivatives.
- **Sharpe:** $$(R_p - R_f)/\sigma_p$$. Worked: A **0.27**, B **0.50**, market **0.39**.
- **Treynor:** $$(R_p - R_f)/\beta_p$$. Worked: A **8.9%**, B **10.9%**, market **14%**.
- **Sharpe vs Treynor:** identical rankings only when correlation with the market is **1.0**. An **inadequately diversified** portfolio ranks **higher on Treynor** than on Sharpe.
- **Jensen's alpha:** $$R_{\text{actual}} - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)]$$.
- **Information ratio:** $$\alpha_p/\sigma_p$$. **Sortino:** $$(R_p - R_f)/\sigma_{\text{negative}}$$ — useful for hedge funds and positively skewed returns.
- **Attribution worked example:** managed **5.55%** vs bogey **4.10%** → excess **1.45%**; asset allocation **0.35%**, selection **1.10%** (equities 1.05%, bonds 0.05%).
- **Good benchmarks are unambiguous, investable, measurable, consistent with the manager's philosophy, and accepted by the manager.**
- **Trap:** portfolio **risk** is not the weighted average of constituent risks — only portfolio **return** is.
- **Trap:** the **CML** uses total risk and applies only to efficient portfolios; the **SML** uses beta and applies to all assets. Do not mix them.
- **Trap:** a **beta of zero** does not make an asset risk-free — it can still carry unsystematic risk.
- **Trap:** **DWR** and **TWR** answer different questions. Use DWR for the client's actual experience, TWR for the manager's skill.
