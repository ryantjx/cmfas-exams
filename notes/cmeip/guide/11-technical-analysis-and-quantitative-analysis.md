# Chapter 11 — Technical Analysis and Quantitative Analysis

Technical analysis is the alternative to fundamental analysis: it studies past price and volume to predict future direction, and it is not concerned with fair value. This chapter covers its philosophy and assumptions, the challenges to it, the indicators, the chart patterns, Elliott Wave Theory, and the role of quantitative analysis.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Explain the philosophy and assumptions in the application of technical analysis to investment decisions.
- Discuss the challenges to technical analysis.
- Describe the various technical indicators.
- Distinguish between support and resistance levels.
- Describe share price and volume analysis techniques.
- Explain chart patterns such as head and shoulders, double tops and bottoms, consolidation rectangles, triangles, flags and pennants, wedges and gaps in identifying price trends.
- Explain Elliott Wave Theory as a technical analysis approach.
- Describe the essentials of quantitative analysis.

## 11.1 Overview

### 11.1.1 What is Technical Analysis?

Besides fundamental analysis, another approach to security evaluation is **technical analysis**. Though controversial, many traders employ technical trading rules or indicators to make investment decisions.

### 11.1.2 Basic Philosophy and Assumptions

Technical analysis is the methodology of **forecasting fluctuations in securities prices through an examination of historical market data**. It can be applied to individual securities, the entire market, and any asset class — foreign exchange, gold or bonds.

Technical analysis uses **charts, technical trading rules and indicators**. It is sometimes called **market or internal analysis** because it studies demand and supply conditions based on market data such as price changes and trading volume. It differs from fundamental analysis, which focuses on factors affecting a company's business profitability — economic, industry and company-specific factors.

Technical analysis holds that prices are determined by **demand and supply**, which are affected by both **fundamental** factors (macroeconomic forces) and **non-fundamental** factors (market sentiment and speculative forces). Technical analysts study past price and volume behaviour to identify shifts in supply and demand so as to predict future price behaviour. **They are not concerned about a security's fair value.**

**The five underlying assumptions:**

- A security's **market value is determined only by its supply and demand**.
- Supply and demand are affected by both **rational and irrational** factors — not only economic variables but also hopes, fears, opinions, moods and guesses of potential buyers and sellers. The market constantly assesses all these factors.
- Prices of individual securities and the overall market **exhibit trends**, which tend to persist over an appreciable length of time.
- Trends develop in reaction to **changes in the supply-demand balance**, and such changes can be detected by studying market actions.
- **Chart patterns tend to repeat themselves.**

**Where analysts agree and disagree.** Generally, technical and non-technical analysts accept the first two assumptions: at any point in time price is determined by supply and demand, and supply and demand is governed by many variables. The key difference concerns **irrational factors** — technical analysts expect them to **persist**, while fundamental analysts expect only a **temporary** effect, with rational beliefs prevailing in the long run.

Opinions differ on the third assumption, about the **speed of adjustment**. Technical analysts believe new information is priced by the market **over a period of time** rather than at one point, because there are different sources of information and some investors have earlier access. Prices therefore adjust gradually to the gradual flow of information, and because the adjustment is gradual, prices exhibit **trends that persist over long periods**.

The fourth assumption implies that as prices move from one equilibrium to a new one, the change can be detected sooner or later in charts. Technical analysts hope to benefit by being among the **first to detect a shift in trend**.

### 11.1.3 Challenges to Technical Analysis

The **Efficient Market Hypothesis** presents the greatest challenge. Studies testing weak-form market efficiency generally support the notion that prices adjust **rapidly** to new information, leaving little opportunity for technical analysts to exploit market data.

According to the **random walk theory**, share prices follow a random walk, meaning price movements are **independent over time**. Whether past price data can predict future prices is therefore questionable.

Another criticism: many technical analysts are familiar with chart patterns and act on them **at the same time**. If a popular rule is followed by many participants, some act first, causing prices to change quicker than expected, so only those who act earliest benefit. The popularity and resulting competition **eventually negate the value** of the rule. That said, chart reading is a **very subjective** exercise, and several interpretations of the same pattern are not unusual, so there is always an element of doubt and disagreement.

## 11.2 Price-based Technical Indicators

Price-based technical indicators attempt to mine information from current and historical market prices to "beat" the market. They typically **measure market momentum and the relative state of the market** — whether it is **overbought or oversold**.

### 11.2.1 Moving Average

A moving average is the average of a security's closing price over a given period. Typically each day is given the **same weight**: in a 100-day moving average, each day has a weight of **1/100**. A more sophisticated moving average gives **higher weights to more recent prices**. The moving average is **more stable than the daily price** because it includes past prices, making it more useful for detecting trends. If the current market price starts to **diverge directionally** from the moving average, this may indicate a **trend reversal**.

### 11.2.2 Advance-Decline Line (Breadth Index)

Daily index movements can be misleading because an index is based on selected shares that may not represent the overall market. The **advance-decline (A/D) line**, an index covering **all securities traded**, overcomes this by capturing the **breadth** of the market.

The A/D line measures, on a **cumulative daily basis**, the net difference between the number of shares with price advances and those with price declines in a period (usually a day or a week). The net advance or decline is the number of advances minus the number of declines, and the line plots a **running total** over time.

The A/D line is compared with an index such as the **Straits Times Index (STI)** and normally rises and falls in line with it. If both are rising (declining), the market is **technically strong (weak)**.

- In a **rising** market, any **divergence** between the index and the A/D line is of concern, because it means most shares are not participating — indicating a **market peak**.
- If the A/D line is **rising** while the index is **declining**, it signals a **reversal of the falling trend**.

### 11.2.3 Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD)

MACD is a **trend-following momentum indicator** showing the relationship between two moving averages of a security's price. It reveals changes in the **strength, direction, momentum and duration** of a trend.

The MACD indicator is a collection of **three time series** calculated from historical price data, most often the closing price:

- The **MACD series** is the difference between a **"fast"** (short-period) **exponential moving average (EMA)** and a **"slow"** (longer-period) EMA of the price series.
- The **signal or average series** is an EMA of the MACD series itself.

An exponentially weighted moving average reacts **more significantly to recent price changes** than a simple moving average (SMA), which applies equal weight to all observations.

The indicator therefore depends on **three time parameters** — the time constants of the three EMAs. The notation **MACD (a,b,c)** denotes the indicator where the MACD series is the difference of EMAs with characteristic times $$a$$ and $$b$$, and the average series is an EMA of the MACD series with characteristic time $$c$$. Parameters are usually measured in **days**, and the most common values are **12, 26 and 9** — MACD (12,26,9).

A fast EMA responds more quickly than a slow EMA to recent price changes. By comparing EMAs of different periods, the MACD series indicates changes in trend:

- When the MACD **crosses and falls below** the signal line, it is a **bearish** signal — possibly time to sell.
- When the MACD **crosses and rises above** the signal line, it is a **bullish** signal, suggesting upward momentum.

### 11.2.5 Bollinger Bands

**Bollinger Bands** are a type of **price envelope** developed by **John Bollinger**. Price envelopes define upper and lower price range levels. Bollinger Bands are plotted at a **standard deviation level above and below a simple moving average**. Because the distance is based on standard deviation, the bands **adjust to volatility swings** in the underlying price.

Calculation:

- Compute the **simple moving average**, typically a **20-day SMA**. The first data point averages the first 20 closing prices; the next drops the earliest price, adds day 21 and averages, keeping the data set at 20.
- Obtain the **standard deviation** of the price over that data set.
- Multiply the standard deviation by **two** (a commonly used multiplier) and both **add and subtract** that amount from each point along the SMA, producing the upper and lower bands.

The purpose is to provide a **relative definition of high and low prices**. By definition prices are high at the upper band and low at the lower band. Many traders believe the closer prices move to the **upper** band, the more **overbought** the market; the closer to the **lower** band, the more **oversold**. An overbought or oversold market may be primed for a **trend reversal or price correction**.

Individual indicators are typically **not used in isolation** but in conjunction with others. Moving averages and the relative strength index are also common.

## 11.3 Trends, Support and Resistance Levels

Technical analysts rely on **trend lines** and **trend channels** to identify trends.

- An **uptrend** is a rising trend line connecting a series of **ascending lows**.
- A **downtrend** is a falling trend line connecting a series of **descending highs**.

An uptrend is in place when **current highs are above previous highs and current lows are also above previous lows**. When current highs are no longer above previous highs and, more importantly, current lows fall below previous lows, a **trend reversal** may be occurring.

A downtrend occurs when current lows are lower than previous lows and current peaks are lower than previous peaks. A potential change may occur when this pattern is no longer intact.

The trading range between the top and bottom trend lines is the **trend channel**, which may be rising, flat or declining. If share prices stay within a rising channel, the technician would **hold the share and ride the trend**.

**Support and resistance.**

- A **support level** is a price range where a **substantial increase in demand** is expected. At this level prices are relatively attractive to buyers who missed the opportunity earlier.
- As prices increase, a **resistance level** develops where buyers refuse to pay higher prices, while sellers may be attracted by higher prices to take profits. This supply is the market **"overhang"**.

A movement through and beyond either level is a **breakout**, signalling strong confirmation and continuation of the primary trend. A breakout **above resistance** is **bullish**; a breakout **below support** is **bearish**.

Technical analysts are also alert to **reversals**. A penetration below a support line is a **sell signal**, indicating a reversal of the uptrend. In the standard illustration, the move to point **C** creates a new low past the previous low at **A**, and the move to **D** is lower than the previous high at **B** — confirming the trend has reversed downwards.

## 11.4 Significance of Volume

Volume — the number of securities transacted — shows the **intensity of excess demand or supply** at a point in time and reflects changes in investor attitudes.

- If prices advance on **low** volume, the enthusiasm implied is **not as strong** as when a rise is accompanied by very high volume.
- A price increase with **heavy** volume relative to normal is an indication of **bullish** activity.
- A price decline with **heavy** volume is **very bearish**, reflecting a strong and widespread desire to sell.
- In a sustained bull market, if prices **correct downward on declining volume**, this may indicate a **lull** followed by continuation of the rally.
- A rally that develops on **contracting** volume could signal a possible **trend reversal**.

## 11.5 Share Price and Volume Techniques

Three techniques: the Dow Theory, the Moving Average Line, and the Relative Strength Index.

### 11.5.1 The Dow Theory

Named after **Charles Dow**, the oldest and most popular method for identifying **long-term trends** in the equity market. It is based on the movements of the **Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA)** and the **Dow Jones Transportation Average (DJTA)**.

**Three movements:**

| Movement | Duration | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Several months to several years | The market trend, either **bullish** or **bearish**. Historically, **bear markets last for a shorter period than bull markets** |
| **Secondary** | Several weeks to months | Shorter than the primary and **opposite in direction**. Usually retraces **one-third to two-thirds** of the previous advance (decline) in a bull (bear) market |
| **Daily fluctuations** | Day to day | Considered **random**, with no forecasting value for long-term investors and no impact on long-term trends |

Dow Theory forecasts are based on the **primary and secondary** movements. Its purpose is to determine **where the market is and where it is going, but not the magnitude** of the movements.

In practice: if the swings of the market index are **successively higher** and the successive lows are also higher, the trend is **up** and investors are in a bull market. If successive highs and successive lows are **lower**, the direction is **down** and investors are in a bear market.

**Confirmation.** A new primary trend is confirmed only when **both the DJIA and DJTA give the same signals**. When the two averages **diverge**, the Dow Theory assumes the **prior trend is maintained**.

**Five weaknesses:**

- It relies too heavily on the **DJIA**, which contains a limited selection of shares — old, large "blue chip" companies, with aggressive high-tech companies **under-represented**.
- It is **slow** in identifying a major trend and may miss an important price move or be **"whiplashed"** — caught by a short-term reversal even though the long-term trend is intact but not yet visible.
- It uses the **Transportation Average**, comprising mainly railway shares that no longer have a major impact on the economy.
- It is designed to identify the **major** trend and is therefore **not useful for trading the short trend**.
- It **does not help identify which shares to buy or sell**.

### 11.5.2 Moving Average Line

Moving averages smooth out erratic price changes so the underlying trend can be clearly observed, and are used to **confirm the direction** of price trends. Popular averages are the **200-day** and **50-day**; the 200-day identifies **long-term** trends while a **20- to 30-day** average identifies **short- and intermediate-term** trends.

**Trend changes are identified not by a change in the direction of the moving average, but by a crossover between the moving average and the index itself.**

- If the overall trend has been negative, the moving-average line lies **above** current prices. If the price **reverses and breaks upwards through the moving-average line from below**, accompanied by **heavy volume**, most technical analysts consider this a very positive change and expect a **reversal of the downtrend**.
- In an uptrend the moving-average line is rising but **below** current prices. If prices **break downwards through the line from above** with heavy volume, this is a **bearish** pattern signalling a reversal of the long-run uptrend.

Because the moving average is an average of past prices, if prices keep rising the line also rises but at a **slower rate** than current prices. If current prices (which lead the averages) fall, the line follows the downtrend. The price line therefore naturally cuts the moving average when a trend reversal occurs.

The signal is **stronger** if:

- The price cuts **both** short- and long-term moving averages, and
- The **short-term average cuts the long-term average in the same direction**.

### 11.5.3 Relative Strength Index (RSI)

The RSI is a **momentum indicator** measuring the degree of buying or selling interest in a security. Developed by **J. Welles Wilder**, it measures the **speed of change** of price movements.

$$RSI = 100 - \left[ \frac{100}{1 + \dfrac{\text{Average of upward price change}}{\text{Average of downward price change}}} \right]$$

The index ranges from **zero to 100**. An RSI **above 70** is considered **overbought**; below **30** signals an **oversold** condition.

A better way to use the RSI is to track the **past peaks and troughs** of the underlying and examine the typical RSI levels for that market and security, to calibrate the overbought and oversold levels.

The RSI is useful in situations of **surprises and shocks**:

- **Emotional selling** under unwarranted conditions produces oversold conditions. For the contrarian, a low RSI of **20–30 or lower** could signal a **buying opportunity**.
- In situations of **overly optimistic demand**, a strong RSI of **70–80 or higher** could signal a **profit-taking opportunity**.

The RSI is **less useful when trends are steady and consistent**. Where constant demand for a growth stock keeps its RSI persistently high, the level should **not** be read as a sell signal; instead look for an opportunity to accumulate when the RSI is not as high.

In practice, periods of overselling and overbuying can last **one to two weeks**, during which prices can continue to rise or fall. Technical analysts therefore usually **wait for a few days** of overbuying before recommending profit-taking, and of overselling before recommending accumulation.

## 11.6 Charts and Chart Patterns

Charts are the technical analyst's main tool. Types include:

- **Line charts.**
- **Bar charts.**
- **Candlestick charts.**
- **Point and figure charts.**

Most show the **high, low, opening and closing** prices for each trading day.

There are two types of price pattern:

- **Reversal patterns** indicate that an important reversal in trend is occurring.
- **Continuation patterns** suggest the market is only taking a pause, possibly to correct a short-term overbought or oversold condition, after which the existing trend is expected to resume.

### 11.6.1 Head and Shoulders

The head-and-shoulders pattern is a **reversal** pattern and probably one of the more reliable indicators of major reversals. The **upright** formation occurs at **market peaks** and consists of a left shoulder, a head and a right shoulder.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Left shoulder** | First strong price rally, normally accompanied by **heavy volume**, followed by a decline on **considerably less** volume |
| **Head** | Prices advance again on **high volume** to a level **higher than the top of the left shoulder**, then fall on **lower volume**. Prices fall below the top of the left shoulder but stay **above or near the low** of the left shoulder |
| **Right shoulder** | A third rally on **less volume** than during either the left shoulder or the head. Prices **fail to reach the height of the head** and fall through the **neckline** |

A breakout is confirmed if the price **closes below the neckline by 3% of the share price**. Once penetrated, the potential magnitude of decline is measured by the **distance between the head and the neckline, projected downward from the neckline**.

Formation time varies: with rapid price reactions it can take **a few weeks**; otherwise **a few months or years**. Formation tends to be quicker for **currencies** and slower for **commodities**.

**Inverse head and shoulders** indicates a **market bottom**.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Left shoulder** | Begins with a significant **selloff on high volume**, followed by a brief rally, sometimes supported by short covering, usually on **lower volume** |
| **Head** | A second wave of selling as participants in the initial rally give up their positions. Heavy volume drives the price to bottom out and form the head. The head may be extended and consolidated while selling is absorbed by bargain hunters. The **RSI would typically indicate an oversold position** at this point. Prices are driven up slightly by demand from parties seeing value |
| **Right shoulder** | Profit-taking by shorter-term traders and those who did not sell earlier. This sell-off is **lighter and shorter-lived**, met with demand, so the right shoulder becomes a **low higher than the previous low**. The resistance at the right shoulder creates the **neckline** |

When demand picks up and price breaks past the neckline, **volume typically rises**. Once fully formed, the pattern implies the prior downtrend is over and an **uptrend reversal** is likely.

### 11.6.2 Double and Triple Tops and Bottoms

A **double top** is a **reversal** pattern indicating a **market top**. It consists of two tops separated by a valley. The main feature is that the **second top forms with distinctly lower volume** than the first. A double top is **confirmed when the neckline is broken on the way down**.

There can be more tops rising to a resistance level, resulting in a selloff. Once resistance is reached, the **volume of each subsequent rise falls** and the number of buyers pushing prices toward the peak dwindles, forcing a correction as buying interest is overwhelmed by selling.

A **double bottom** occurs in a **bear market** and signals a potential reversal. The **first bottom** is normally accompanied by **heavy volume**, while the **second bottom is usually dull** with little volume. **Volume increases significantly on the breakout.** Some patterns extend to **triple** tops or bottoms. As with the double top, a double bottom is **confirmed if the neckline is broken on the way up**.

### 11.6.3 Consolidation Rectangles

A consolidation rectangle forms when prices move **sideways within a narrow range** for an extended period, usually as a **continuation** pattern. A breakout is technically significant: **upwards** indicates a bullish trend, **downwards** signals further weakness.

During formation it is **difficult to predict** which way price will break, so it is assumed the **prevailing trend continues until proven otherwise**.

### 11.6.4 Triangles

Triangles form with **four points of contact** — two support and two resistance points. Three types:

| Type | Boundaries | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Symmetrical** | Upward-sloping support line and downward-sloping resistance line | A breakout may occur on **either** side and indicates a **major move in the same direction** |
| **Ascending** | Upward-sloping support line and an **almost horizontal** resistance line | **Bullish.** Buyers are more aggressive than sellers. Once the limited supply is absorbed, prices are expected to **advance rapidly** |
| **Descending** | Almost horizontal support line and a **downward-sloping** resistance line | **Bearish.** Sellers are more aggressive, lowering rally peaks while buyers buy at a set price. Once limited demand is filled at the floor, prices **fall sharply** if selling continues |

### 11.6.5 Flags and Pennants

A **flag** looks like a flag on the chart — a **parallelogram** of price fluctuations. It usually occurs after a **sharp, almost vertical** rise or fall, followed by **declining volume**. As the flag completes, prices **break out in the same direction** they were moving before.

- In a **rising** market the flag usually forms with a **slight downtrend**. Volume is **very heavy just before** formation, then **dries up** as the pattern develops, only to **explode** as price works out of the formation. Flags can take as few as **5 days** to as many as **3–5 weeks**. They represent a period of **controlled profit taking** in a rising market.
- In a **falling** market the flag usually forms with a **slight upward bias**, also on declining volume. Rising prices with declining volume is essentially **bearish**. When price breaks out downwards, the almost vertical slide resumes. Volume tends to pick up but **does not have to be explosive**, unlike the breakout from a flag in an upward move.

A **pennant** forms after a sharp rise or fall with lower volume during formation. The difference from a flag is that a pennant is formed by a set of **converging**, instead of parallel, lines.

### 11.6.6 Wedges

A **wedge** is formed by a series of **converging peaks and troughs**, with both converging lines moving in the **same direction**.

- A **rising wedge** has both boundary lines slanting **upwards**. It usually develops on a **falling** price trend. After completion, prices usually **fall very sharply**, especially if volume is high. Rising wedges are characteristic of **bear market rallies**.
- A **falling wedge** is bounded by converging lines slanting **downwards**. It represents a **temporary interruption of a rising trend**. When prices move out of a falling wedge they are likely to **drift sideways** before beginning to rise.

### 11.6.7 Gaps

A **gap** is a price range where **no transactions occur**. Gaps occur when the lowest (highest) traded price for a period is higher (lower) than the highest (lowest) traded price in the previous period. On a chart it appears as an **empty vertical space** between adjacent trading periods. **Daily gaps are more common** than weekly or monthly gaps.

A gap is **closed or covered** when price retraces the entire range of the gap. A gap may be covered in a few days, weeks or months, **if at all**. Although the general principle is that gaps will eventually be covered, this is **not always** the case, so trading strategies should not be implemented solely on the assumption that a gap will be filled in the immediate future.

| Gap type | Where it appears | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Breakaway** | When price breaks out of a price pattern | Emphasises the bullishness or bearishness of the breakout. An upside breakout should be accompanied by **relatively heavy volume** to confirm a change to bullish sentiment |
| **Runaway** (continuation) | During rapid, straight-line rises or falls when trends accelerate and emotions run high | Occurs in the **middle** of a move |
| **Exhaustion** | The **last** in a series of runaway gaps | Represents the **end** of a rapid rise or fall. It is **not easy** to tell whether a gap is another runaway gap or the exhaustion gap |

Generally: **breakaway gaps develop at the beginning** of a move, **runaway gaps in the middle**, and **exhaustion gaps at the end**.

## 11.7 Elliot Wave Theory

In his study of equity prices in **1938**, **R.N. Elliott** proposed the **Elliott Wave Theory**: prices move in **cycles of waves and sub-sets of waves** and exhibit the **Golden Ratio, 1.618**. Each cycle comprises a **5-wave advance and a 3-wave retreat**.

In Elliott Wave charts, markets move in 5-wave and 3-wave patterns, and the turning points creating these patterns are often at **Fibonacci support and resistance levels** (61.8, 161.8, and so on).

The Fibonacci series is **0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, …**, where each number is the sum of the two preceding numbers. As the series progresses, the ratio of consecutive numbers (for example 21 ÷ 13) approaches the **Golden Ratio of 1.618**.

## 11.8 Quantitative Analysis

Quantitative analysis involves the **abstraction of market reality** — the economy, financial markets and their environment — into relations incorporated into a **financial model**. The objectives are to identify **price anomalies and arbitrage possibilities** and, lately, to increase the **speed of access to market information** that gives an advantage.

Its significance is evidenced by the rapid growth in the use and development of quantitative techniques. Quantitative analysis uses **statistics and calculus** for pricing, algorithmic trading, asset allocation, risk management, trading strategy modelling, portfolio optimisation, and identifying trading and investment opportunities.

Quantitative analysts are typically called **"quants"** or **"rocket scientists"**. Within an organisation they may work in the **front office** developing trading strategies with complex mathematical tools, or in **risk management** measuring the risks bundled into a transaction or product.

**Quant funds** extensively use quantitative analysis in fund management decisions. This can be seen as an outcome of markets becoming more sophisticated in understanding and measuring risk. While less sophisticated traders look only at price changes, quants **decompose** those changes so specific risk profiles can be created to benefit from them: instead of looking only at the **first order** of price change (delta), quants look at **second and third order derivatives**.

There is controversy about the usefulness and fairness of such models. **Long Term Capital Management** was a hedge fund relying extensively on quantitative analysis; despite the elegance and apparent mathematical robustness of its strategies, it incurred **huge losses**.

**High frequency trading**, which relies on physical transmission speeds and complex price discovery algorithms, is under **intense scrutiny from regulators**.

Given rapid advances in computing and increasing global competition, quantitative analysis will continue to develop as a tool necessary to enhance industry standards in fund and risk management.

## 11.9 Summary

- Technical analysis, also called market or internal analysis, studies past prices and volume to predict future price direction for individual securities or the aggregate market.
- It assumes market prices discount everything and prices move in trends, and that shifts in trends can be detected by studying charts.
- The greatest challenge is the **Efficient Market Hypothesis**, which argues prices adjust rapidly to new information, leaving little opportunity. Popular technical rules are followed by many participants, and the resulting competition eventually negates their effectiveness.
- Indicators include the **A/D line** (breadth index), which measures the aggregate movement of all securities traded, and **moving averages**, which smooth erratic price changes so the trend is clearer.
- **MACD** is a trend-following momentum indicator showing the relationship between two moving averages. **Bollinger Bands** are envelopes plotted at a standard deviation level above and below a simple moving average; closer to the upper band is more overbought, closer to the lower band more oversold.
- Under the **Dow Theory** there are three movements: primary, secondary and daily fluctuations. Its objective is to identify the **primary trend**.
- The **moving average line** smooths erratic movements, and **crossovers** between the moving average and the index identify trend reversals.
- The **RSI** identifies overbought or oversold securities.
- Chart types include line, bar, candlestick, and point and figure.
- There are two types of chart pattern: **reversal** (head and shoulders, double tops and bottoms) and **continuation** (consolidation rectangles, triangles, flags, pennants, wedges).

## Exam cues

- **Technical analysis is not concerned with fair value.** It studies supply and demand through price and volume.
- **Five assumptions:** price determined only by supply and demand; supply and demand affected by rational **and irrational** factors; prices exhibit **persistent trends**; trends develop from changes in the supply-demand balance and can be detected; **chart patterns repeat**.
- **The key disagreement** with fundamental analysts is the **persistence of irrational factors** and the **speed of adjustment** — technical analysts believe information is priced **gradually**, producing trends.
- **Greatest challenge: the EMH.** Weak-form efficiency and the **random walk theory** both undermine technical analysis. Popular rules are self-defeating because many act at once.
- **Moving average:** equal weights by default; more stable than daily price; **divergence** from price may signal a reversal.
- **A/D line (breadth index):** cumulative advances minus declines across **all** securities. Index rising with A/D falling = **market peak** warning; A/D rising with index falling = **reversal of the downtrend**.
- **MACD:** three EMAs; **MACD (12,26,9)**; MACD crossing **below** the signal line is **bearish**, **above** is **bullish**.
- **Bollinger Bands:** **20-day SMA** ± **2 standard deviations**. Near the upper band = **overbought**; near the lower band = **oversold**.
- **Uptrend:** current highs above previous highs **and** current lows above previous lows. **Downtrend:** the reverse.
- **Support** = substantial increase in demand expected. **Resistance** = buyers refuse higher prices while sellers take profits — the **overhang**. Breakout **above resistance is bullish**; **below support is bearish**.
- **Volume:** price rise on heavy volume = **bullish**; price decline on heavy volume = **very bearish**; a correction on declining volume in a bull market may be a **lull**; a rally on contracting volume may signal **reversal**.
- **Dow Theory:** three movements — **primary** (months to years), **secondary** (weeks to months, **opposite** direction, retraces **one-third to two-thirds**), **daily** (random). **Bear markets last a shorter period than bull markets.** Confirmation requires **both DJIA and DJTA** to agree; divergence means the prior trend is maintained.
- **Dow Theory weaknesses:** over-reliance on the **DJIA** (blue chips, high-tech under-represented), **slow** and prone to **whiplash**, the **Transportation Average** is railway-dominated, useless for **short-term** trading, and it does not say **which shares** to trade.
- **Moving average crossovers:** price breaking **up** through the line from below on heavy volume = **bullish reversal**; price breaking **down** through the line from above on heavy volume = **bearish reversal**. Stronger if the price cuts **both** short- and long-term averages and the short-term average crosses the long-term in the same direction.
- **RSI:** ranges **0–100**; **above 70 overbought**, **below 30 oversold**. Contrarians buy at **20–30** and take profit at **70–80**. Less useful in steady trends.
- **Head and shoulders:** **reversal** at a **peak**. Left shoulder on heavy volume, head on high volume to a higher level, right shoulder on **less** volume failing to reach the head. Breakout confirmed by a close **3% below the neckline**; the target decline equals the **head-to-neckline distance** projected down.
- **Inverse head and shoulders:** **reversal** at a **bottom**; the **RSI typically shows oversold** at the head; the right shoulder is a **higher low**.
- **Double top:** second top on **lower volume**; confirmed when the neckline breaks **downwards**. **Double bottom:** first bottom on **heavy volume**, second **dull**, volume **increases on the breakout**; confirmed when the neckline breaks **upwards**.
- **Consolidation rectangle:** sideways range, **continuation**; assume the prevailing trend continues until proven otherwise.
- **Triangles:** **symmetrical** (breakout either way), **ascending** (bullish), **descending** (bearish). Four points of contact.
- **Flags and pennants:** **continuation** after a sharp move. Flag = **parallel** lines, **5 days to 3–5 weeks**; pennant = **converging** lines. In a rising market the flag slopes **down**; in a falling market it slopes **up**.
- **Wedges:** **converging** lines both moving the same direction. **Rising wedge** appears in a **falling** trend and is followed by a sharp fall — characteristic of **bear market rallies**. **Falling wedge** interrupts a **rising** trend.
- **Gaps:** **breakaway** at the beginning, **runaway** in the middle, **exhaustion** at the end. Gaps are **not always** filled, so do not trade on that assumption alone.
- **Elliott Wave:** **R.N. Elliott, 1938**; **5-wave advance and 3-wave retreat**; **Golden Ratio 1.618**; turning points at **Fibonacci** levels (61.8, 161.8).
- **Quantitative analysis:** abstracts market reality into models to find **price anomalies and arbitrage**; uses statistics and calculus; quants look at **second and third order** derivatives, not just delta.
- **LTCM** is the cautionary example of quantitative models failing despite mathematical elegance.
- **Trap:** the Dow Theory identifies **direction, not magnitude**.
- **Trap:** a **rising wedge** is bearish and a **falling wedge** is a continuation of an uptrend — the names describe the shape, not the implication.
- **Trap:** an **ascending triangle** is bullish and a **descending triangle** is bearish, but a **symmetrical triangle** can break either way.
- **Trap:** the RSI being persistently high in a steady uptrend is **not** a sell signal.
