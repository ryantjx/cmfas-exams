# Chapter 6 — Equity Securities

Equity is the asset class most retail investors hold, and the one where valuation judgement matters most. This chapter covers what a share is and what rights it carries, the categories of equity an adviser recommends, how indices are constructed, and the three valuation routes — net asset, discounted cash flow and relative ratios — with the arithmetic the examination tests.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Describe the characteristics of equity securities, namely ordinary shares and preference shares.
- Distinguish between blue-chip, growth-oriented, income-oriented, cyclical and defensive equity investments, as well as those with high net tangible asset backing.
- Understand the differences between absolute and relative valuation approaches.
- Understand how to apply relative price valuation ratios, firm valuation ratios and yield-oriented ratios used in equity analysis — dividend yield, price-earnings, price-to-book value, price-to-sales, price-to-cash flow and EV/EBITDA — and their relevance to different types of companies.
- Describe the composition and computation of equity market indices.
- Perform calculations relating to rights and bonus issues.
- Explain the use and computation of discounted cash flow in estimating fair market value (intrinsic value).
- Apply relative valuation ratios in comparing stocks and explain how they are calculated and used.
- Understand how prospectuses for initial public offerings are read and analysed.
- Describe depositary receipts as a means of investing in foreign markets.

## 6.1 Introduction

Equity is possibly the most popular asset class among investors in Asia. Despite the volatility, the potential for substantial capital gains remains a major draw. This chapter covers share ownership, the characteristics of equities, strategies for investing in equities, relative pricing and valuation using cash flow models, and the composition and application of equity indices.

Shares represent ownership rights in a corporation and let investors participate in the company's performance. When companies perform well, management may pay dividends on top of price appreciation. When they perform poorly, dividends fall or disappear and the shareholder faces capital loss as the stock falls; in the worst case the price goes to zero. Many stocks worldwide, including in Singapore, have fallen to or near zero. The representative's job is to help clients select better companies and avoid lemons.

## 6.2 Share Ownership

### 6.2.1 Why Share the Ownership of a Great Business?

Why would the owners of a booming company give other investors a chance to participate? Aside from altruism, original owners may want to **share the risk of ownership**, **realise and monetise** the value of the company, and **redeploy funds** to other businesses for even greater gains. Usually, once growth has slowed from high double digits, owners of privately held companies seek to monetise part of their ownership of a still-profitable business.

For example, the owners of a 100% family-owned business could reduce their stake to **51%** by selling **49%** to common shareholders through an IPO and placement. Families may lack the right successors and prefer to professionalise management, and different family members may have different ownership agendas and motives for cashing out.

Through a well-planned IPO, ownership control can stay in the family, the business value can be partly monetised, additional capital can be raised from new shareholders, management is professionalised, and the risks and returns of the future are shared. Conversely, if the share value of a listed company falls below its perceived intrinsic value, the company is sometimes **taken private** by its major shareholders.

Owners of new growth businesses — high-tech, biotech, internet, disruptive technology — often need capital to fund growth. Founding owners may have initial support from private equity partners looking to realise the potential of the business, and raise sizeable capital through an IPO.

### 6.2.2 Types of Shares

Share ownership carries rights and privileges, but not all shares carry the same ones. Before listing, original owners must decide how much ownership to give up, because majority ownership usually dictates the stance on major decisions.

In Singapore there are traditionally two types of listed shares:

- **Common or ordinary shares** with voting rights.
- **Preference shares** with non-voting rights.

Typically one common share carries one vote. Since **June 2018**, SGX has allowed listing of companies with **dual class shares**: in addition to ordinary voting shares, companies can have multiple-vote shares. The intent is to encourage founders who need substantial funding to grow rapidly but want to retain majority control. Safeguards protect non-controlling shareholders: extra votes cannot be exercised on important matters where founders and minority shareholders may have conflicting interests, and to prevent founder entrenchment **each multiple-vote share can carry a maximum of 10 votes**.

In the US and Europe there are also share classes with different voting rights.

| Company | Classes | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Berkshire Hathaway** | Class A and Class B common stock | Class A priced at **USD284,570** per share and Class B at **USD189.39** (24 October 2017). Each Class A share is convertible at the holder's option into **1,500 Class B** shares; Class B is non-convertible and carries **1/200th** the voting power of Class A |

Berkshire Hathaway is one of the most successful equity investment holding companies in the United States. Its gain in net worth during **2016** was **USD27.5 billion**, which increased the per-share book value of both Class A and Class B stock by **10.7%**. Over the last **52 years** — since present management took over — the per-share book value has grown from **USD19 to USD172,108**, a rate of **19% compounded annually**. Warren Buffett is CEO and Chairman and owns **36.8%** of the company.
| **Google (Alphabet)** | Class A, B and C | Class A: **1 vote** per share; Class B: **10 votes** per share; Class C: **no voting interest**. Class A trades at a premium to Class C; Class B is not traded but distributed internally to founders and insiders |

In Europe many companies are controlled by founding families with different share classes carrying different numbers of votes.

### 6.2.3 Ordinary Shareholders

In Singapore companies mainly issue ordinary or common shares for ownership interest. Holders are granted:

- **Profits, price gains and dividends.** Shareholders gain when the company makes profits, when the share price rises, and when management declares a dividend. Ordinary dividends are at management's discretion and depend on performance; common shareholders have **no right** to dividends and payouts are **not guaranteed**.
- **Voting rights.** Shareholders are legally entitled to vote on major policy decisions such as electing directors and issuing new shares. The more shares owned, the greater the voting power; minority shareholders may lack sufficient votes to pressure management.
- **Information rights.** Shareholders have a right to be informed of major events and decisions affecting the company and its value.
- **Residual claim.** In bankruptcy or liquidation, shareholders receive their share of remaining asset value only after all other creditors and claimants are paid — bondholders, suppliers, other creditors, employees and deferred taxes.
- **Limited liability.** If a corporation fails, the most shareholders can lose is their original investment. Creditors have recourse only to the corporation's assets; shareholders are not personally liable for the company's obligations.

### 6.2.4 Preference Shares

Technically an equity security, a preference share is a **hybrid** because it resembles both fixed income and equity. As equity it has an infinite life and pays dividends. Although most preference shares are callable by the issuer, there is typically a period of protection against calls — usually the issuer will not call within the first **5 years**. Non-callable preference shares may also be issued and carry a **lower** rate of return than callable ones, because the issuer has no option to redeem.

A preference share resembles a fixed income security with the dividend fixed and specified in advance, providing an income stream very similar to a bond. The difference is that the income continues **indefinitely** unless the shares are called or retired. Given the stipulated payments, most investors view preference shares as roughly equivalent to fixed income and as an alternative to bonds.

Dividends are specified as an annual dollar amount or as a percentage of par value. Unlike a bond, the issuer can decide **not to pay** the preference dividend if earnings are insufficient, and failure to pay does **not** constitute default.

Investors regard preference shares as **less risky than ordinary shares** because preference dividends are specified and must be paid before ordinary dividends. They are **riskier than bonds** because bondholders are paid first in liquidation. Preference shareholders have **no voting rights** except in financial distress. Companies may issue several classes of preference shares, distinguished by the method and amount of dividend payment and by associated special benefits.

| Feature | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Cumulative** | Once common, now rare. Requires all past unpaid preference dividends to be paid before any ordinary dividend is declared. Protects the preference holder because preference dividends do not enjoy the same legal right as bond interest |
| **Callable** | Most preference shares are callable at a stated redemption price, usually only after several years. Lets the issuer replace the shares if rates fall. Callable shares normally carry a **higher** yield than similar non-callable shares. The initial redemption price is usually set at a **premium above par**, protecting the investor from an early call with no premium |
| **Participation** | Lets the preference holder receive extra dividends when earnings are sufficient. Attractive but uncommon |
| **Convertibility** | Gives the holder the right to exchange the preference share for a specified number of ordinary shares. Attractive to investors and reduces the issuer's cost: the issuer has effectively sold an option, and the option premium is reflected in the **lower yield**. Investors generally convert when ordinary share prices appreciate |

### 6.2.5 Rights Issue, Bonus Issue, Stock Split and Reverse Stock Split

**a. Rights issue.** A rights issue injects new capital by issuing new shares. Rights to subscribe are first offered to **existing shareholders**, who may buy a stated number of new shares at a specified price. This prevents dilution of their share of profit and voting interest.

Rights may be **renounceable** (tradable) or **non-renounceable** (not transferable, cannot be traded). Companies may now issue pro-rata renounceable rights shares of up to **100% of share capital**, up from the previous limit of **50%**. If an existing shareholder declines to exercise, the rights may be taken up by other investors in the market.

**Worked example — Dragonfly Ltd.** It is September 2022. Of an authorised capital of **500 million** shares, Dragonfly has **300 million** outstanding, trading at **$2.00** at end-September 2022. Management announces a rights issue of **100 million** shares at **$1.90** to raise capital, mainly to acquire a company developing navigation and tracking parts for commercial drones, with part of the funds for refinancing. Forecast EPS for the financial year ending December 2022 is **$0.11**.

**(1) Likely price reaction.** The reaction depends on the market's perception of the purpose and benefits of the capital raising and the company's prospects. If the purpose is seen as positive — the acquisition adds to future revenue, earnings and value — the price may **rise** after the announcement. If the purpose is seen as risky, or the reasons are unclear, the price may **fall below the issue price**.

**(2) Theoretical ex-rights price.** Before the issue, 300m shares at $2.00 implies market capitalisation of **$600m**. After a **1-for-3** rights issue there are **400m** shares. New capital is **$190m** (100m × $1.90), so the new theoretical market capitalisation is **$790m** ($600m + $190m). The theoretical ex-rights price is **$1.975** ($790m / 400m).

**(3) Weighted-average shares and revised EPS.** Weighted-average shares = (300m × 9 + 400m × 3) / 12 = **325m**. Revised EPS forecast = ($0.11 × 300m) / 325m = **$0.1015** per share.

**b. Bonus issue.** No additional capital is raised. It is usually a **signalling** exercise when the company has done well and expects the stock to rise on good news. A 1-for-3 bonus issue for Dragonfly gives **400m** shares, but the price is adjusted down **more sharply** because no new capital is injected. From the same $2.00 and 300m shares, the theoretical ex-bonus price is $600m / 400m = **$1.50** — sharply lower than the rights-issue price.

**c. Stock split.** A stock split makes shares more accessible to retail shareholders. **OCBC made a 2-for-1 split in 2005** when its price was above **$13**; after the split and another rights issue, its stock was **$11.55** as of October 2017. When prices reach a level where the shareholder base is excessively skewed to institutions, a company may split to make the unit price more affordable. A split also gives majority holders more convenience in selling units. **Stock splits do not raise capital.**

**d. Reverse stock split.** A reverse split combines a number of shares into one or fewer. **Citibank carried out a 1-for-10 reverse split in 2011.** During the Global Financial Crisis, banks such as Citigroup that had traded above **$60** found themselves below **$5** (Citigroup's low was **$0.97**). Certain institutional investors are governed by rules discouraging investment in "penny stocks"; the SEC defines a penny stock as one priced **below $5**. That made it difficult for institutions to own Citigroup. The 1-for-10 reverse split took the shares from **$4.52** just before to **$44.16** at the close of the first trading day after. By mid-2022 Citigroup traded at **$47.10**.

## 6.3 Choice of Equities

Strategies depend on the return sought and the client's risk threshold. The basic strategy is to search for undervalued stocks (Section 6.5); this section covers the types of stock available.

### 6.3.1 Blue-Chip Companies

A simple approach for long-term investors is to buy **blue chips**: major conglomerates with great financial strength and well-established, stable, mature businesses. With a long, consistent earnings record they can generally pay steady dividends over long periods in both good and bad years.

Blue chips hold leading positions in their industries and economies, are usually pacesetters, and frequently set the standards by which others are measured. Their management has shown foresight in growing the company without jeopardising current earnings. Such companies have the advantage of size and resources for sustainable earnings. Investors seeking safety and stability, and conservative investors, usually turn to blue chips.

Blue chips had periods of high earnings growth in their early years, but growth slows with size. Many mega-growth stocks dominating today are likely to become blue chips. Because of financial strength, connections and operating capability, many Asian blue chips have diversified into new businesses or geographies to sustain growth. In smaller markets blue chips also account for a large share of market capitalisation.

Singapore blue chips include **Keppel Corporation, SembCorp Industries, City Developments, CapitaLand, DBS, OCBC, UOB and SIA**. US blue chips include **3M, Procter & Gamble, General Electric, Walmart, IBM, Hewlett Packard, Apple and Microsoft**.

Do not be complacent: the conditions fundamental to any company's success can always change, especially with technological disruption.

### 6.3.2 High-Growth Companies

High-growth companies usually come from industries and businesses in the new economy — at the forefront of research and development for new technologies, new business processes, renewable energy and new ways of doing business. Some of their products and services disrupt traditional ways of operating.

"Growth companies" usually means companies generating high double-digit sales and earnings growth over several years — for example **more than 20% compounded annually over several years**. There are two stages of high growth:

- High growth in sales but still reporting **negative profits**.
- High growth in **both sales and earnings**.

**a. High growth, negative profits.** On NASDAQ there are many concept companies with strong future sales growth potential that keep incurring higher costs and cannot break even. Many technology and biotech companies are bought for potential research breakthroughs. For many, the **burn rate** from R&D catches up before they show profit and they become bankrupt. Others persist thanks to key investors who keep funding them until profitability.

Tesla reported many years of losses but was heavily supported until its **first quarterly profit in 2017**. Twitter reported almost **12 years of losses** until Q4 2017. When it becomes clearer that high sales growth will translate into profitability, the rest of the investment community becomes interested, and such companies sometimes become acquisition targets.

**b. High growth, high profits.** The second group has excellent sales **and** earnings growth, part of which is reflected in the stock price. The key is identifying companies with **sustainable** high earnings growth to justify continued appreciation. These companies are usually aggressive, with strong research teams, and pay relatively **small dividends** because most earnings are retained for expansion. Key characteristics: a solidly entrenched position in an expanding market, distinctive or unique products, and high profitability, usually by pre-empting a special part of the market. Examples of phenomenal earnings growth from **2014 to 2018**: Amazon, Alibaba, Tencent, Nvidia and Facebook.

**c. Characteristics.** High-growth companies typically carry **high P/E multiples** because investors pay a premium for higher earnings growth relative to industry peers. Growth cannot last forever as companies get bigger; once earnings growth slows, premiums narrow and the growth stock is **re-rated** at lower ratios. These are usually **high-beta** stocks with greater market sensitivity: in a general sell-off they correct much faster. Growth-stock investors must cope with higher volatility.

### 6.3.3 Companies with High Dividend Yields (Income Stocks)

Companies paying higher average dividend yields appeal to retirees and others buying shares for current income. Income stocks are often sought by trust funds, pension funds, endowment funds, and charitable and educational foundations. Higher payers include mature firms generating large cash flows; being mature, they may not need cash for expansion and can afford higher dividends.

A benefit is that when markets correct, high-yield stocks usually find **price support** on the strength of their yields, provided there is confidence the dividends will be paid. For example, if the fixed deposit rate is **3%**, stocks with dependable payouts are unlikely to fall below a dividend yield of **4–5%** before tax — the price is supported where dividend/price = 4%.

Drawbacks exist. The share may belong to a lacklustre company in a socially unpopular industry such as tobacco. With low growth potential, it pays high dividends. In some companies the high dividends are offset by a tendency to call rights issues, which can be dilutive — some REITs. And high dividends persist only if earnings can be sustained; companies in difficulty have been forced to cut dividends.

### 6.3.4 Cyclical Stocks

The market rises and falls with the economy, but some stocks benefit more in a boom and do worse in a downturn. These are **cyclical stocks**.

For economies driven by domestic consumption, cyclicals are among the first to benefit from increased consumer and business spending during growth and low unemployment. Automobile manufacturers (GM, Ford) are representative consumer-based cyclicals. Their earnings and prices fluctuate with the business cycle or are accentuated by it: in good conditions they enjoy strong earnings growth and price appreciation; when conditions deteriorate they are the first affected by the spending slowdown and their prices drop as earnings growth declines.

Because economies differ in composition, which stocks are cyclical differs by market. Taiwan and South Korea are driven by high-tech and electronic manufacturing, so those sectors are cyclical there. In Singapore and Hong Kong, cyclicals also come from **property and banking**, usually among the first sectors affected in a downturn.

### 6.3.5 Defensive Stocks

Defensive companies' earnings are more likely to withstand downturns or uncertainty. They tend to provide necessities — **pharmaceuticals, food, utilities and transportation** — and their shares are less volatile than cyclical and growth shares. **Nestlé and Coca-Cola** are typical defensive stocks. If a recession is expected, there is strong interest in "recession-proof" companies, especially if they pay a reasonable dividend yield.

### 6.3.6 International Equities

With internet brokers and local brokers offering execution and research on foreign stocks, international investing is now common even for retail investors. The need for diversification and exposure to growth companies forces investors to look abroad.

| Route | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Direct investment** | Buy foreign shares on their exchanges through local brokers. Diversifies away home-market risk, but may be complicated by withholding taxes, significant transaction costs and regulations limiting foreign ownership |
| **Depositary receipts (DRs)** | Indirect ownership of foreign shares, letting investors buy and hold foreign securities without going to the foreign exchange. Provide ready access to markets with barriers to foreign ownership and capital flows |
| **Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)** | Replicate a broad index such as the STI or S&P 500. Buying an ETF assumes the market risk of the foreign index's underlying shares |
| **Foreign shares with dual listings** | Listed both in the home country and another jurisdiction. Dual listing enhances liquidity and lets a share keep trading on the other exchange after one has closed |

## 6.4 Equity Market Indices

The general movement of the market is measured by an equity market average or index — a group of securities supposed to reflect the entire market. The composition, the weight given to each security, and the calculation method all matter. Three weighting schemes are used:

- **Value-weighted.**
- **Price-weighted.**
- **Un-weighted or equal-weighted.**

### 6.4.1 Value-Weighted Series

A value-weighted series derives the initial total market value of all shares in the series (market value = shares outstanding × current price). That figure becomes the **base**, assigned an index value, normally **100**. A new market value is then computed and compared with the base to determine the percentage change, which is applied to the beginning index value.

**Worked example.**

| Day 1 | Shares | Price ($) | Market value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1,000,000 | 5.00 | 5,000,000 |
| B | 3,000,000 | 3.00 | 9,000,000 |
| C | 5,000,000 | 10.00 | 50,000,000 |
| | | **Total** | **64,000,000** |

Base value = index of **100**.

| Day 2 | Shares | Price ($) | Market value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1,000,000 | 5.20 | 5,200,000 |
| B | 3,000,000 | 3.10 | 9,300,000 |
| C | 5,000,000 | 10.50 | 52,500,000 |
| | | **Total** | **67,000,000** |

$$\text{New Index Value} = \frac{\text{Current Market Value}}{\text{Base Value}} \times \text{Beginning Index Value} = \frac{67{,}000{,}000}{64{,}000{,}000} \times 100 = 1.05 \times 100 = 105$$

An advantage of the value-weighted index is that it **automatically adjusts** for bonus and rights issues and other capital changes, because those actions typically do not affect market capitalisation — a fall in price is offset by a rise in shares outstanding. A constituent's importance depends on its market value, so price changes in high-capitalisation shares move the index more than those in low-capitalisation shares.

The **S&P 500** is a value-weighted index of the top 500 companies, principally by market capitalisation, accounting for around **75%** of total US stock market capitalisation. In Singapore the **STI** is also weighted by market capitalisation; it replaced the Straits Times Industrial Index, which was **price-weighted**.

### 6.4.2 Price-Weighted Series

A price-weighted series uses an **arithmetic average of current prices**: prices are added and divided by the number of securities. The divisor must be adjusted for capital changes (rights and bonus issues) and changes in the sample. Because the series is price-weighted, a **high-priced share carries more weight** than a low-priced one. A stock split therefore **reduces** a share's weight even though market capitalisation is unchanged.

The **Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA)** is a price-weighted average of **30** large, well-known industrial shares. It has been criticised for using only 30 non-randomly selected blue chips, which are not considered representative of shares listed on the NYSE.

### 6.4.3 Un-Weighted or Equal-Weighted Series

In an un-weighted or equal-weighted index, all shares carry **equal weight** regardless of price or market capitalisation. A $2 share is as important as a $10 share, and total market value is ignored. Changes may be based on the arithmetic or geometric average of percentage price changes.

**Worked example — geometric average.**

| Share | Day 1 ($) | Day 2 ($) | 1 + % change |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5.00 | 5.20 | 1.04 |
| B | 3.00 | 3.10 | 1.03 |
| C | 10.00 | 10.50 | 1.05 |

$$\text{Geometric Average} = (1.04 \times 1.03 \times 1.05)^{1/3} = 1.040$$

Index value (Day 2) = index value (Day 1) × 1.040.

The **Value Line Composite Average** is an equal-weighted geometric average of share prices; more than **80%** of its constituents are listed on the NYSE.

## 6.5 Valuation Approaches

The time value material in Chapter 2 provides the foundation. There are three ways to value a listed company and its businesses:

- **Net asset valuation** — book value, liquidation value and revised net asset value.
- **Discounting of future cash flows** — the absolute valuation approach.
- **Relative valuation** — comparison against historical trends and other companies in the industry.

| Net asset value approach | Absolute valuation (DCF) | Relative valuation — shareholder's perspective | Relative valuation — firm value perspective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Book value | Constant growth DDM | Price-to-earnings | Enterprise value/sales |
| Revised net asset value (RNAV) | Multi-stage DDM | Price-earnings growth | Enterprise value/EBITDA |
| Liquidation value (salvage value) | Discounted free cash flow | Price-to-book value | Enterprise value/cash flow |
| | | Price-to-RNAV | |
| | | Dividend yield | |

## 6.6 Net Asset Valuation

The net asset valuation approach is a basic balance-sheet approach: net assets are valued at book value, and revised to market value or liquidation value depending on circumstances. It is particularly relevant for companies whose value is predominantly determined by tangible assets on the balance sheet — **loans of banks, premiums of insurance companies, real estate holdings of property companies**.

### 6.6.1 Book Value

The book value of equity reflects total assets as stated on the balance sheet, net of all liabilities. It is one of the key numbers investors and acquirers examine. Book value divided by shares outstanding gives **book value per share**.

When conditions are severe — a market crash — or when a company is making losses with a challenging outlook, book value is one way to assess the value of the equity. Book value also represents the accumulation of retained earnings over the years. In bad times, if shareholders' equity has not been wiped out by losses, book value often represents the **floor value**.

The problem with book value in a crisis is assessing the deterioration in asset values, which can be **understated**. When that happens, book value must be revised downwards.

### 6.6.2 Liquidation Value (Salvage Value)

Liquidation value assumes the company will be discontinued and viable operations sold to other companies. Assets and businesses are priced for **ease of sale** so money can be recovered quickly to pay creditors. The price obtained may be much lower than market or book value, so the residual liquidation value can be much lower than equity book value. Listed companies have encountered severe business failures and unexpected losses leading to eventual liquidation.

### 6.6.3 Revised Net Asset Value

When the value of assets or liabilities has changed significantly before the end of the reporting period, book value may be revised to reflect actual value, from which **revised net asset value per share (RNAVPS)** is calculated in the same way as BVPS.

The real estate industry is the standard example. For property companies with substantial land bank and property holdings, professional real estate consultants provide a valuation to mark property asset values to market. But real estate prices can appreciate or fall rapidly within a financial year, so before year-end analysts may have to revise net asset value to reflect major appreciation or correction.

When revising asset values to market, **also check whether debt and other liabilities need revising**. The market value of USD-denominated debt may have appreciated in the interim; if so, the market value of liabilities should be revised too.

## 6.7 The Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Approach

For the CM-EIP examination, representatives are expected to know how to **explain** the process of valuing stocks and the variables used, but will **not** be asked to compute and derive fair value.

The investment community mostly uses the DCF method to derive fair value, so this module focuses on DCF. The dividend discount model (DDM), used only occasionally for companies with high dividend payouts (**DPS/EPS above 80%**), is set out in Section 6.13.

### 6.7.1 Introduction to the DCF Approach

The key principle is the **time value of money**: money received in the future is worth less than money received today. Under DCF, a company's current fair value is estimated by **discounting** future free cash flows to present values.

Several DCF methods exist depending on assumptions, and their results can vary significantly. This module focuses on the **Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)** approach, using the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** as the discount rate.

- **Free cash flow to firm (FCFF)** — a commonly used measure of projected cash flows. Derive the after-tax cash flow from operations, deduct cash set aside for capital expenditure and to sustain business activities, then add back depreciation and other non-cash expenses to reflect free cash flow available to the company.
- **Terminal value** — project FCFF over a reasonable period, for example **10 years**. Beyond that it makes more sense to assume a terminal value at the end of year 10 encompassing all future cash flows thereafter, indefinitely if the company is a going concern. The terminal value must also be discounted to present value.
- **Discount rate** — depends on how the company is financed, by equity, debt or both. It is therefore the **WACC** of the cost of equity and cost of debt used to generate future cash flows.
- **Enterprise value** — discounting future FCFF and terminal value at the WACC gives the current value of the company, the enterprise value: the sum of all discounted future FCFF and the discounted terminal value.

If the market value is **below** intrinsic value by a significant percentage (undervalued), buy. If **above** (overvalued), sell.

### 6.7.2 Calculating the Weighted Average Cost of Capital

FCFF is discounted at the WACC because the firm's value and future cash flows are generated with funds from both debtholders and shareholders. The returns these investors demand are the **cost of capital** to the company — the cost of raising funds through debt (bonds) and/or equity (share issues). WACC is a weighted average of the cost of equity and the cost of debt.

**a. Cost of equity.** The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) is applied:

$$R = R_f + \beta (R_m - R_f)$$

where $$R$$ is the cost of equity, $$R_f$$ the risk-free rate, $$R_m$$ the market return, $$R_m - R_f$$ the **equity market risk premium**, and $$\beta$$ the sensitivity of the stock price to the market.

The typical steps in computing firm and equity values:

- Calculate the WACC.
- Estimate FCFF over a more predictable period, typically **10 years**.
- Estimate the long-run terminal value at the end of the estimation period (the price at year 10).
- Sum the present values of the FCFFs and the terminal value to obtain **enterprise value**.
- Subtract the market value of net debt and preferred shares from enterprise value to get the estimated **market value of equity** (fair value of equity), the estimated market value of the common shares.
- Divide by shares outstanding to get the **intrinsic share value** (fair value per share).

**Worked illustration — Procter & Gamble.**

| Input | Value and source |
|---|---|
| Risk-free rate $$R_f$$ | **2.33%** — the US 10-year Treasury rate at the time. For Singapore stocks, the 10-year SGS rate can be used |
| Required market return $$R_m$$ | The average price return of the S&P 500 over the last 20 years, including the GFC and dot-com bubble, was **+7.36%**; the annual dividend yield was about **1.92%**, giving a total return of about **9.28%**. So $$R_m = 9.28\%$$ |
| Beta $$\beta$$ | **0.68** (providers quote 0.65–0.68 over five years; the higher figure is used, assuming more market sensitivity) |
| Cost of equity | $$R = 2.33 + 0.68(9.28 - 2.33) = 7.056\%$$ |

**b. Cost of debt.** The effective interest rate the company pays on debt. From the balance sheet, P&G's effective interest cost (interest expense / borrowings) was **1.4%** in 2016 and about **1.1%** for the first nine months of 2017. Another way is to identify the interest cost of each debt item from the notes. The conservative approach uses the higher **1.4%**, since rates were expected to rise with Fed hikes.

**c. Weighting.** Inputs:

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Market value of debt | **USD30.414 billion** — the book value of debt as at 8 July 2017. Where there are no significant credit issues and the debt is in local currency, book value is a reasonable estimate of market value |
| Market value of equity | **USD224.17 billion** — always use market value: **$87.65 × 2.5576 billion shares** |
| Effective tax rate | **23%** (22.6% for the last nine months, 25% in 2016) |
| Total capital | Debt + equity = 30.414 + 224.17 = **254.584** |

$$\text{WACC} = (1 - 0.23)(0.014)\left(\frac{30.414}{254.584}\right) + (0.07)\left(\frac{224.17}{254.584}\right) = (0.0108 \times 0.1195) + (0.07 \times 0.8805) = 0.0629 = 6.29\%$$

That **6.29%** is the discount rate for all future cash flows. With the low-rate environment, P&G had locked in long-term debt priced very cheaply relative to its cost of equity.

### 6.7.3 Estimating Free Cash Flows to the Firm

Most analysts build projections of the **pro-forma income statement**, balance sheet and cash flow statement, for about **10 years**. In primary research the analyst interviews management about prospects and expenditure needs, and earnings estimates come from projections of sales, other income and cost items.

Analysts also rely on historical ratios and relationships, reviewing past major expenditure patterns and asking management about likely major capital outlays. Alternatively, FCFF is approximately the **sum of cash flow from operating activities and cash flow from investing activities**, since the underlying numbers are often the same.

### 6.7.4 Calculating the Enterprise Value

Discount the estimated FCFF and terminal value over the chosen period:

$$\text{PV of Free Cash Flows} = \frac{E(FCFF_1)}{(1+\text{WACC})^1} + \frac{E(FCFF_2)}{(1+\text{WACC})^2} + \frac{E(FCFF_3)}{(1+\text{WACC})^3} + \dots + \frac{E(FCFF_{10}) + TV}{(1+\text{WACC})^{10}}$$

The terminal value uses the estimated FCFF in year 11 divided by (WACC − $$g_c$$), where $$g_c$$ is the **terminal growth rate** at which FCFF are expected to grow indefinitely:

$$\text{PV of Terminal Value} = \frac{\dfrac{E(FCFF_{11})}{\text{WACC} - g_c}}{(1+\text{WACC})^{10}}$$

**Worked illustration — P&G.** Cash flows from operating and investing activities in 2016 are the base. P&G's cash flows grew at an average of **1.5%** over 2006–2016 and about **3%** over the last five years. The analysis applies **2.5%** growth for 2017–2021, slowing to **2%** for 2022–2026, settling at a terminal growth rate of **1.5%** — consistent with the past pattern. WACC is **6.29%**. The resulting firm value is **USD260,391 million**.

### 6.7.5 Calculating the Fair Value of the Stock

Enterprise value is the sum of the estimated market value of common shareholders, preferred shareholders and debt. To derive the fair value of the common stock, subtract the estimated market value of preferred shares and debt from enterprise value, leaving the estimated market value of common shareholders (fair value of common equity). Divide by shares outstanding for the **fair value per share** or **intrinsic share value**.

**Worked illustration — P&G.**

| Item | USD million |
|---|---|
| Enterprise value | 260,391 |
| Less market value of debt | (30,514) |
| Less value of preference shares | (1,038) |
| **Estimated market value of share capital** | **228,839** |
| **Intrinsic share value** (228,839 / 2,557.6m shares) | **USD89.47** |

Against P&G's price of **USD87.65** on 7 July, the estimate implies about **2% upside** — fairly valued. The intrinsic value can serve as the target price accompanying the qualitative research report.

### 6.7.6 Criticism of the DCF Model

DCF involves many assumptions and estimates. Different research teams arrive at different target prices. Determining fair or intrinsic value by DCF is **not an exact science**; it provides an estimate based on assumptions. Cynical observers suggest the estimate is open to "fudging" until the assumptions produce a price range aligned with the qualitative story. The model depends heavily on the **terminal value**, which can vary significantly with different assumptions.

### 6.7.7 Actual Market Practice for Cash Flow Models

The industry discounts free cash flows to estimate intrinsic value or a target price, typically discounting 10 years of cash flows and the terminal value at year 10. DCF is used **in conjunction with relative valuation** to reach a more accurate view of whether a stock is over- or undervalued.

The multi-stage DDM is **seldom applied day to day**. On occasion it is used for companies with extremely high dividend payouts, such as tobacco stocks like British American Tobacco.

## 6.8 Relative Valuation Ratios

DCF is an **absolute** valuation approach: intrinsic value comes from projected cash flows. A **relative** approach derives value by comparison with companies in the same industry.

Relative valuation can be made from either:

- A **shareholder's perspective** — for example the price-earnings ratio.
- A **firm-wide perspective** — for example EV/EBITDA, which takes both debtholders and shareholders into account.

In practice multiple ratios are used together for a more holistic assessment.

### 6.8.1 Price-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

The P/E ratio indicates how much investors are willing to pay for a dollar of earnings: current price per share divided by EPS.

$$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price Per Share}}{\text{Earnings Per Share (EPS)}}$$

| Form | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Trailing** (current) P/E | Current market price divided by the most recent **four quarters** of EPS. This is what newspaper share listings publish |
| **Leading** (prospective) P/E | Current market price divided by the **consensus forecast** earnings for the next year, as projected by research analysts |

Questions to ask when applying P/E:

- How much are you paying for the future earnings generated by the company?
- How does this compare with the company's historical P/E in past years?
- How does this compare with the P/E of other similar companies?
- Are there qualitative reasons for this company to remain at a premium or discount?

**Worked example — semiconductor comparison.** INTC closed at **USD34.68**. FY2016 EPS was **USD2.72**, with consensus forecasts of **USD2.86** for FY2017 and **USD2.97** for FY2018.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Trailing P/E for 2016 | 34.68 / 2.72 | **12.8x** |
| Prospective P/E for 2017 | 34.68 / 2.86 | **12.1x** |
| Prospective P/E for 2018 | 34.68 / 2.97 | **11.7x** |

An investor pays just **USD12.1** for a dollar of FY2017 earnings from INTC, against **USD16.7** for TSMC and **USD22.1** for UMC. INTC's lower leading P/E across all three projected years implies paying less for future earnings. But it is **not** as simple as buying the lowest P/E and selling the highest.

**Reading the historical range.** The historical P/E chart is constructed from 12 months of prices, with the last month being three months before the financial year end — for example, October 2012 to September 2013 prices divided by actual 2013 EPS. All three companies have December year ends, so if expected earnings are close to actual, the chart reflects what the market decided to trade at given its expectations.

- **INTC:** the prospective FY2018 P/E of **11.7x** is at the **lower end** of its normal range of **10.3x to 16.0x**. INTC traded in tandem with TSMC historically but has traded at a larger discount to TSMC since 2016 — check whether that discount is justified. If INTC should trade at **14x**, that implies **18–20% upside**, a possible **BUY** signal.
- **TSMC:** to sustain its premium over INTC it must maintain stronger EPS growth, and consensus forecasts show it does. That justifies the premium; if TSMC does not deliver, its price falls relative to INTC. Its prospective 2017 P/E of **16.7x** is beyond its normal range, and FY2018's **14.9x** is at the high end. The justification is that TSMC was vying with Samsung to be the main supplier for Apple's A11 chips for the new iPhone, so FY2018 earnings are partly tied to that phone's success. Combining P/E analysis with the qualitative picture, the premium appears justified — but on the prospective FY2018 P/E alone, TSMC looks fairly valued.
- **UMC:** prospective FY2017 and FY2018 P/Es of **22.1x** and **18.7x** look expensive against a historical range of **11–15x**. Its EPS growth is higher than TSMC's and INTC's, but from a much lower absolute EPS base, and there is no major qualitative information suggesting more sustainable growth beyond the US economic recovery. Without justification, the price may weaken.

**b. Factors affecting P/E ratios.** Investment is not simply buying low P/E and selling high P/E; examine the qualitative rationale. Companies remain at a perpetual premium or discount so long as they have stronger or weaker earnings potential than peers. Beyond projected earnings:

- **Low transparency and predictability of earnings.** When risks are high and predictability low, even high earnings growth justifies a **lower** multiple.
- **Impending events** that may affect earnings, such as renewal of major contracts or pending legal decisions. Analysts must make major assumptions and the P/E may be lower for the uncertainty.
- **Uneven growth trajectory.** Companies with potential exponential long-term growth but very low short-term earnings may show very high prospective P/Es in the first few years. The **PEG ratio** helps assess this.
- **Market inefficiency.** In less liquid markets where the stock is controlled by major shareholders, the market may not price efficiently, distorting the P/E through illiquidity.

**c. Growth versus mature companies.** Growth stocks typically command **higher** P/E ratios than the market average because investors pay more for higher expected earnings growth.

**Advantages and disadvantages of the P/E ratio.**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Widely used and easily understood | Earnings can be negative, making P/E meaningless |
| Easily used for comparison across companies and sectors | Accounting differences affect earnings and P/E |
| | Earnings can be volatile or lumpy, making P/E hard to interpret |
| | Earnings forecasts can be quite inaccurate |
| | P/E does not distinguish between valuation and expected growth differences |

**Shiller's inflation-adjusted P/E ratio.** Robert Shiller used inflation-adjusted P/E ratios to track periods when the S&P 500 was overvalued. According to Shiller, the S&P 500's P/E ratio passes the **30x** mark during periods of overvaluation — what former US Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan called **irrational exuberance**. Although the ratio has flirted with that level in recent years, there were only two earlier occasions when the inflation-adjusted S&P 500 P/E ratio was above **30x**: the Great Depression of **1929** and the **2000** dot-com bubble.

**P/E ratios and interest rates.** The P/E ratio is related to the required rate of return, which is in turn related to interest rates. As interest rates increase, required rates of return on all securities generally increase. When interest rates rise, the fall in price valuations is greater than the fall in earnings, so P/E ratios fall. Conversely, when interest rates fall, price valuations are more significantly affected than earnings, so P/E ratios rise.

### 6.8.2 Price-Earnings Growth (PEG) Ratio

A criticism of P/E is that it ignores expected future growth: a low P/E may mean undervaluation **or** simply poorer expected growth, and a high P/E may mean overvaluation **or** stronger expected growth. Strictly, P/E ratios should only be compared between companies with similar expected growth. The **PEG ratio** addresses this: the P/E ratio divided by the expected annual earnings growth in per cent, measured by EPS.

$$PEG = \frac{P/E}{\% \text{ Growth in EPS}}$$

where the growth is typically the prospective EPS growth compounded over **3 years**; when growth potential lies further out, a **5-year** projected growth rate may be used.

By controlling for expected earnings growth, PEG indicates whether the current price is under- or overstated relative to another company with different growth prospects. PEG is most widely used for **high-growth** companies and industries.

A **low PEG indicates an undervalued share**. As a rule of thumb a PEG **below 1** is attractive but typically very hard to find: such stocks may be relatively new, not actively covered, and carry higher risk because the earnings growth may not materialise. They are typically in high-tech, biotech or disruptive technologies in high demand. Once noticed, more investors become interested and the price rises, pushing PEG above 1.

**Worked illustration.** Among global high-growth tech companies, **Alibaba** had the highest prospective P/Es — **44.5** in 2017 and **33.8** in 2018 — yet its PEG was among the **lowest at 1.6**, reflecting its high **28%** projected annual growth over the next few years.

A caution on projections: when markets are bullish and the economy strong, the analyst community tends to **overestimate** the sustainability of high earnings growth.

### 6.8.3 Price-to-Book Value (P/BV) Ratio

$$\text{P/BV Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price Per Share}}{\text{Book Value Per Share}}$$

P/BV is best suited to companies whose value is tied directly to asset holdings. Book value matters more for **investment companies, banks, insurance companies and property companies**, because many of their assets are reflected in the books and can be liquidated.

**a. Financial institutions.** Because P/BV uses book rather than market value, it can mislead when market values differ substantially from book. If a bank's loans become non-performing, their market value may be near zero while book value is unchanged. Since the Global Financial Crisis several banks have seen book value eroded by proprietary trading losses, excessive risk-taking on distressed debt and credit derivative losses. With adequate provisions and proper recapitalisation, book value can be adjusted accurately; with inadequate provisions, additional write-downs follow over time.

The lack of transparency on loan book and investment position quality is a problem when applying P/BV to banks. The ratio has also become less dependable because banks continue to encounter credit losses after the crisis — most recently provisioning for non-performing loans to oil-related companies and commodities trading losses.

**b. Property companies.** P/BV is actively applied to property companies because the bulk of asset values is well reflected on the balance sheet. Property companies and REITs with sizeable commercial, office and residential holdings may enjoy significant book value appreciation when the property market rises, and suffer substantial falls in a downturn. Accountants rely on independent professional real estate consultants to revalue property assets annually.

During major market and economic downturns, stock prices can fall much more sharply than book value. Companies with diffused ownership can then become good takeover targets: with the price below book value, control of the assets can theoretically be acquired cheaply, and investors with deep pockets can acquire distressed property companies with hard assets. After the **1997 Asian Currency Crisis**, some small listed property developers caught in a liquidity crunch were acquired by larger developers at prices below book value.

**c. Hidden reserves in book value.** Market values of land bank and property can differ significantly from book value — when land is rezoned for a different use (agricultural to commercial or residential), or when land becomes more accessible through better road and rail links. Such land may remain at book value while worth much more at market value; where accounting limits allow this, a **hidden reserve** is created. Hidden reserves also arise when provisions made in prior years are no longer needed but are not written back.

In such cases book value is **understated**, so the true P/BV is **lower** than calculated, making undervaluation more likely.

**Advantages and disadvantages of P/BV.**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Book value is cumulative and usually positive, unlike EPS | Companies with intangible assets such as patents and proprietary research cannot be fairly valued using P/BV |
| Book value is usually more stable than EPS, which can be volatile | Validity depends on the transparency and proper treatment of provisions and charges against book value |
| Well suited to companies whose value is closely tied to tangible asset holdings — banks, insurers, real estate companies | Book value may be understated or overstated when assets rise or fall in value, affecting P/BV |

### 6.8.4 Price-to-Sales (P/Sales) and Enterprise Value-to-Sales (EV/Sales) Ratios

**a. Price-to-sales.** A feasible comparison for companies generating very low, zero or negative earnings. It indicates the number of times the market is willing to pay for a firm's sales revenue per share.

$$\text{P/Sales Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Market Price Per Share}}{\text{Sales Per Share}}$$

Sales per share is annual sales divided by ordinary shares outstanding. P/Sales is useful where sales are strong but earnings are lacking or negative — some high-tech and biotech companies generate sales but remain unprofitable. Proponents argue that consistent sales indicate a strong company and that sales are a fundamental requirement for earnings.

Use it with caution. It does not present a complete picture because it ignores expenses and liabilities, and a **lower** P/Sales is not necessarily positive — it may simply indicate the company is unprofitable. Conceptually the ratio is flawed: the numerator (price) relates to shareholders only, while sales are generated from funds supplied by both shareholders and debtholders, so numerator and denominator are inconsistent.

**b. Enterprise value-to-sales.** Some investors consider EV/Sales more appropriate because both numerator (EV) and denominator (sales) are consistent — both debt and equity financing generate sales. Enterprise value became widely adopted in the **1980s** and is a total firm value concept.

**c. P/Sales versus EV/Sales.** P/Sales is easier and more convenient, and intuitively asks "how much am I paying for the projected or current sales?" But it ignores risks from debt financing, especially where large debt generates sales, so a highly indebted company can appear inexpensive on a low P/Sales. Because EV encompasses both debt and equity, EV/Sales accounts for the value of debt and is more appropriate for cross-company comparison.

**Advantages and disadvantages of EV/Sales.**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Sales figures are generally less subject to manipulation than earnings | High sales revenue does not necessarily mean the company is profitable |
| Sales are positive, unlike EPS which can be negative — useful for start-ups and companies with no earnings | Sales revenue recognition practices, even without manipulation, vary between companies and can hinder comparison |
| Sales are more stable than EPS, which can be volatile | Expenses are excluded, so the comparison may be incomplete or misleading |
| Applicable to almost all companies | |
| EV/Sales directly reflects the impact of higher debt on relative valuation, which P/Sales does not | |
| Conceptually correct, since numerator and denominator both relate to the total enterprise | |

### 6.8.5 Enterprise Value-to-Cash Flow (EV/CF) Ratio

EV/CF is used because of concerns about earnings manipulation: cash flows are much less prone to manipulation. **Operating cash flow** (or free cash flow) is usually used — the total cash generated through core operations, found in the statement of cash flows under "cash generated from operations".

**Worked example — Fulton Corporation.** Market price **$18** per share, **1 billion** shares outstanding at 31 December 2018, **$10 million** of preference shares and **$400 million** of borrowings. No associates, minority interest or unfunded pension obligations. Cash generated from operations is **$2,210 million** and cash and cash equivalents at year end are **$8,000 million**.

$$\text{EV/CF} = \frac{\text{Market Cap} + \text{Debt} + \text{Preferred Shares} - \text{Cash}}{\text{Cash Flow from Operations}} = \frac{(18 \times 1{,}000) + 400 + 10 - 8{,}000}{2{,}210} = \frac{10{,}410}{2{,}210} = 4.71$$

A **lower** EV/CF is desirable when comparing companies, because it implies more cash flow generated from the capital supplied.

### 6.8.5.1 Advantages and Disadvantages of Enterprise Value-to-Cash Flow from Operations

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cash flows are less likely to be manipulated than earnings | Cash flows can be from operating, investing or financing activities, and the choice affects the comparison |
| Cash flows are independent of accounting methods | |
| EV/CF considers the impact of debt, since debt funding also generates operating cash flow | |

### 6.8.6 Enterprise Value-to-EBITDA Ratio

EV/EBITDA is also called the **enterprise multiple** or EBITDA multiple and is actively used in relative valuation. It is **more popular** than EV/Sales or EV/operating cash flow.

**a. EBITDA versus net earnings and operating cash flow.** Four reasons EBITDA is preferred:

- EBITDA is closer to **cash-based earnings**: unlike net earnings it does not subtract non-cash depreciation and amortisation. Adding back D&A to EBIT gives the bulk of cash-related earnings.
- EBITDA is a **firm value concept**: it adds back interest cost because it considers total earnings available to creditors and shareholders. Net earnings are a shareholder concept because interest has been subtracted.
- EBITDA is preferred over operating cash flow because the latter swings widely with **working capital** changes, which may not reflect business performance since they are influenced by inventory, payables and receivables decisions.
- It is **easier to forecast** EBITDA than operating cash flow.

**b. Why enterprise value is preferred over price.** EV considers the impact of debt and other capital on total firm value, while price relates only to equity. Hence EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E. EV/EBITDA became actively used in the **1980s** because P/E did not directly reflect the value of debt. Unlike P/E, EV/EBITDA **cannot be manipulated by changes in capital structure**, allowing fair comparison of companies with different capital structures.

$$\text{EV/EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Market Cap} + \text{Debt} + \text{Preferred} + \text{Minorities} + \text{Pension} - \text{Associates} - \text{Cash \& Near Cash}}{\text{Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortisation}}$$

**c. Application.** Typically the current EV is used in the numerator and projected annual EBITDA for one to three years ahead in the denominator, asking: "what total capital, net of cash, is deployed to generate the cash earnings for a given year?" All else equal, a **lower** EV/EBITDA means less capital is deployed to generate cash earnings. Some companies will have perpetual EV/EBITDA discounts or premiums for the reasons in 6.8.1(b). EV/EBITDA appears in Bloomberg and most brokerage equity research.

**Advantages and disadvantages of EV/EBITDA.**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| The numerator (EV) captures the impact of debt used to generate earnings, enabling comparison across companies with different debt levels | Less intuitive for retail investors, who prefer and identify with share price comparisons |
| The denominator (EBITDA) reflects cash-based earnings unaffected by working capital swings | EV is more tedious to measure, requiring estimation of the market value of debt, associates, preference shares and minority interest |

### 6.8.7 Absolute versus Relative Valuation

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute (DCF) method** | Conceptually relates intrinsic value to the earnings and cash flows due to the firm over time; DCF is a widely accepted way of determining current value; flexible enough to incorporate changing growth rates over time | DCF depends heavily on the terminal value, which varies significantly with different WACC and growth assumptions; the terminal value calculation always requires the discount rate to be **higher than the growth rate** |
| **Relative valuation** | Allows easy comparison across companies; intuitive and easy to implement | Market sentiment and cycles can influence relative valuation; a comparable set of entities is needed for proper relative valuation |

### 6.8.8 Dividend Yield

Dividend yield matters especially to income-oriented investors. Companies with stable, non-cyclical businesses paying a constant stream of healthy dividends have **downside support** for their share prices. Dividend yield is the income component of a share's return, stated as a percentage of the current price.

$$\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Dividend Per Share}}{\text{Market Price Per Share}} \times 100$$

**Worked example.** A share priced at **$2** with an annual gross dividend of **$0.10** per share has a dividend yield of **5%** (0.10 / 2.00 × 100).

The **dividend payout ratio** is dividends to earnings, indicating the percentage of earnings paid out as cash dividends. A company earning **$0.40** per share and paying **$0.10** has a payout ratio of **25%**. The **retention ratio** is 1 minus the payout ratio, indicating the percentage of current earnings retained for reinvestment.

**Worked example — expected dividend yield.** KW Corp, an engineering, property and manufacturing company, was expected by ABC & Partners to pay a **1.5 cent** dividend in 2017, rising to **3.0 cents** in 2018. A 3-cent dividend on a share price of **31 cents** implies a high dividend yield of **9.7%**. The analyst expects **yield compression**: as the price rises, the yield falls. With a dividend yield expectation of **7.5%** on a 3-cent dividend, the implied share price is 3 cents / 7.5% = **40 cents**.

REITs can be valued using a dividend discount model because they are required to pay out **90% of distributable income** and their earnings are relatively predictable.

### 6.8.9 Cash Per Share and Valuations

Related to dividend is **cash per share**. When a company holds a significant proportion of cash, there is potential for large dividend payments, and such companies are potential acquisition targets.

Consider two companies with the same P/E of **10x**:

| | Company A (cash rich) | Company B (cash poor) |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 2.50 | 3.00 |
| EPS | 0.25 | 0.30 |
| Net cash per share | **0.50** | Net debt |

Company A holds excess idle cash of **$0.50** per share even after paying off its debt; Company B is in net debt. Company A is **undervalued**: if acquired at 2.50 and the idle cash of $0.50 per share is netted out, the buyer is paying a P/E of only **2.00 / 0.25 = 8x**, lower than Company B's 10x.

### 6.8.10 Strategic Value and Break-up Value of a Listed Company

In corporate acquisitions, consider the **strategic** and **break-up** value. An acquirer hopes the acquisition produces more earnings and higher combined value than the offer price.

**Strategic value.** When perceived strategic value exceeds the offer price (which is usually at or above market price), both the acquirer's and target's share prices may rise. If perceived strategic value is **less** than the offer price, the acquirer's stock is likely to **fall**.

**Break-up value.** Some acquisitions expect the sum of the parts sold to exceed the target's value as a going concern — the company is undervalued because of how it is managed. In the **1970s** in the US there were many hostile takeovers carried out expecting to sell parts of the businesses and assets at a higher total value than the takeover market value (share price × shares outstanding).

### 6.8.11 Earnings Yield

The earnings-to-price (E/P) ratio measures earnings against share price; expressed as a percentage it is the **earnings yield**. It is the **inverse of the P/E ratio**.

Earnings yields are usually compared against **bond yields** to assess whether share prices are over- or undervalued. The difference between the two is the **yield gap**. If the S&P 500 earnings yield is still **1.5–2.0%** above US Treasury bond yields, there is still room for the S&P to rise. As at October 2017 the US Treasury bond yield was **2.45%** against an S&P 500 earnings yield of about **4%**.

After the Global Financial Crisis of 2007/08 there was a sharp drop in earnings yields, but the extremely low rates through most of 2009–2016 supported earnings recovery, and earnings yields rebounded to as high as **6.0–7.0%** immediately after the crisis.

## 6.9 Initial Public Offerings

In Singapore, listing on the **SGX Mainboard** requires fulfilling listing requirements set by the exchange. Listing on **Catalist**, SGX's secondary board, is supervised by a **sponsor** (the equivalent of an Issue Manager for a Mainboard listing) and **no quantitative requirements** are needed: sponsors decide on the suitability of the candidate. Sponsors are qualified professional firms with experience in corporate finance and mergers and acquisitions.

In any IPO the issuer prepares to obtain SGX approval to list on the Mainboard or the secondary board. The issuer engages an investment bank or an SGX member company as **lead manager** or sponsor, and **underwriters** such as brokerages and other financial institutions are involved. The lead manager earns **management fees** and the underwriters earn **underwriting fees**. Lawyers draft the prospectus and related documents, and public accountants prepare the audited financial reports.

Once SGX approves the draft prospectus, it can be lodged on the MAS **Offers and Prospectuses Electronic Repository and Access (OPERA)** website. The issuer distributes the prospectus to institutional and retail investors; with pre-placement activity, institutions may view the offering before retail investors. IPOs are usually distributed in two tranches:

- A **private placement tranche**, typically placed with institutional and accredited investors, usually preferred customers of brokerages and banks.
- A **public retail tranche** of retail investors.

Usually the private tranche is a small group taking a major portion of the offering, while the retail tranche is smaller and much more widely distributed.

The public offering begins from the date the prospectus is registered with MAS and typically opens for at least **2 market days**, excluding the date of commencement. Applications are allocated or balloted if oversubscribed, within a few days. Trading begins the next business day after allocation, on a "ready" basis — bought or sold for settlement **2 days** after the transaction date (**T+2**).

**Risks to watch when subscribing:**

- **Vendor share sales** — the sale of the issuer's own holdings rather than new shares. The risk is that the issuer is offloading shares because it knows the outlook is poor. Watch for a **lock-up option**, a moratorium on the sale of the issuer's shares for a stipulated period, which is the issuer's expression of confidence.
- **Deferred shares or share options** awarded before the IPO may **dilute** new investors' earnings, because EPS is depressed once they are exercised.
- The **over-allotment option** (the **Greenshoe option**) gives the issuer the right to increase the offering through an over-allotment of shares. Underwriters can use it to stabilise the price if the shares perform poorly on initial trading by buying back the additional shares. Not all IPOs carry it.
- The issuer's right to **claw back** — reallocation of shares between institutional and retail investors. If institutional take-up is poorer than expected, the claw-back may be invoked and retail investors get a higher allocation of a share institutions probably avoided for good reason.

Beyond these, investors must do their own due diligence by studying the prospectus, available on the OPERA Public Portal at www.mas.gov.sg. When evaluating the prospectus, note:

- **Underwriters** — they generally purchase any remaining unsubscribed shares.
- **Business model** — examine the model, strategy and industry competition, usually in the Management's Discussion, Business Overview and Competition sections.
- **Use of proceeds** — if proceeds repay a large amount of debt, little may be left for expansion; if they buy out existing shareholders, it may be a bail-out at new investors' expense.
- **Management** — examine the experience and background of key appointment holders such as the CEO and CFO to validate their track record.
- **Financial analysis** — analyse the balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement using the ratios covered earlier. Pay special attention to overly rapid expansion of sales, inventory growing faster than sales, an unusual fall in free cash flows, or aggressive acquisitions.
- **Timing** — note the **lock-up period**, the moratorium during which pre-IPO investors and promoters may not sell.
- **Conflicts of interest** between existing owners and management.
- **Secondary offerings** — after the IPO, shares can be issued through secondary offerings, typically as a financing exercise. This may be a **rights issue**, renounceable or non-renounceable, offering existing shareholders additional shares in proportion to their holdings. To avoid dilution they must take up the rights, which means putting up more money. In a rights issue the company lodges an **Offer Information Statement (OIS)** rather than a prospectus with MAS.
- A secondary offering can also be a **compliance placement** made to meet regulatory requirements on share distribution.

## 6.10 Valuing Companies in Initial Public Offerings

Both DCF and relative valuation are used to identify a target price or price range for IPOs. DCF follows Section 6.7: discount the free cash flow to derive firm value, subtract debt to obtain equity value, and divide by total shares outstanding for the target IPO price.

**Worked example — Q-Star Pharmaceuticals.** A sovereign wealth fund owns Q-Star, a pharmaceutical and biotech company preparing over **2–3 years** for a major listing on NASDAQ or the NYSE with a secondary listing on an Asian exchange. The owners want an estimate of current value.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Sales | USD7.65 billion |
| EBITDA | USD2.20 billion |
| Assets | USD18.30 billion |
| Shares post-issue | 750 million |
| Debt | USD11.0 billion |

The relative valuation uses EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA and EV/Assets of the top five pharmaceutical companies. To be conservative, the **lowest** multiple is selected in each category, and a further **20% discount** is applied because Q-Star lacks the patent pipeline and brand image of the top pharmaceutical companies.

| Selected ratio | Lowest (times) | After 20% discount | Q-Star base (USD bn) | Firm value (USD bn) | Market cap (USD bn) | Price (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| EV/Sales | 4.42 | 3.53 | Sales 7.65 | 27.00 | 16.00 | **21.33** |
| EV/EBITDA | 12.33 | 9.86 | EBITDA 2.20 | 21.69 | 10.69 | **14.25** |
| EV/Assets | 1.40 | 1.12 | Assets 18.30 | 20.50 | 9.50 | **12.67** |

Multiplying Q-Star's sales, EBITDA and assets by the discounted multiples gives possible enterprise values. Netting off the **USD11 billion** debt leaves market capitalisation; dividing by **750 million** post-IPO shares gives a target range of **USD12.67 to USD21.33** per share.

Some prefer to apply the discount to the target price range rather than to the multiples, which produces a **higher** target range. A more common approach is to compare P/E and P/BV of similar companies and use a selected average or discounted multiple. The eventual price is a function of market conditions and the owners' expectations.

## 6.11 Depositary Receipts

### 6.11.1 Depositary Receipts (DRs)

DRs are equity securities that trade in an investor's home market and represent an underlying security in the foreign issuer's home market. They overcome obstacles to issuing and investing overseas — inefficient cross-border settlement, imperfect information flow and unfamiliar market practices.

For issuers, DRs offer:

- Broader and more diversified investor exposure, which may improve liquidity.
- Enhanced visibility for the company's products or services in an overseas market.
- A flexible mechanism for raising capital.

A variant is the **non-voting depositary receipt (NVDR)**, which facilitates foreign investment in an issuer's home market. The main difference from direct investment is that NVDRs carry **no voting rights**, so foreign investors cannot participate in corporate decisions. That feature enables foreign investment in firms that would otherwise be prohibited by regulatory restrictions on foreign ownership.

### 6.11.2 American Depositary Receipts (ADRs)

ADRs are the most common type of DR and trade on the NYSE, NASDAQ or the US OTC market. An ADR is a non-US company's equity security that trades in the US as though it were a US equity security.

| Level | Where it trades | Capital raising |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1** | The traditional US OTC market; not listed on any US securities exchange | No |
| **Level 2** | Listed on a US securities exchange such as the NYSE or NASDAQ | No |
| **Level 3** | A listed ADR programme used to offer **new shares** to US investors | **Yes** |

**Sponsored** Level 2 and 3 ADRs are issued by a depositary bank on behalf of, and under a deposit agreement with, the non-US company. Level 2 ADRs do not involve capital raising; Level 3 ADRs allow new capital to be raised. **Unsponsored** ADRs are issued by depositary banks without a formal agreement with the non-US company.

**Global Depositary Receipts (GDRs)** are generally issued offshore from the US and are commonly listed on the **London** and **Luxembourg** stock exchanges.

### 6.11.3 Risks of Investing in DRs

- **Currency risk** — from the currency the DR is denominated in, the currency the share is denominated in, and the investor's home currency.
- **Price risk** — fluctuations in the underlying share price. ADRs often trade at a **premium** to their underlying shares because of regulatory restrictions in the issuer's home market on foreign holdings.
- **Counterparty risk** — financial or operational failure of the depositary bank or its custodian in processing DR obligations.
- **Single-listed ADRs** — surrendering ADRs whose underlying shares are not listed in their home markets leaves the investor holding common shares that are not traded and have no transactional liquidity. Converting ADRs into unlisted shares is not advisable.
- **Fungibility** — not all DRs have **two-way fungibility**, meaning DRs can be converted into underlying shares and underlying shares into DRs. All DRs have underlying shares backing them; the DRs are issued and traded outside the issuer's country, while the underlying shares are lodged in custody with a custodian in the issuer's country.

## 6.12 Summary

- Valuation can use relative valuation and discounted cash flow methods to produce an estimated value or range of values.
- The two most important characteristics of an ordinary share are its **residual claim** and **limited liability**. Residual claim means ordinary shareholders are paid last in liquidation; limited liability means losses are limited to the original investment.
- Legally a preference share is an equity security, but it is often viewed as a **hybrid** because it has characteristics of both fixed income and equity. Different classes are distinguished by the method and amount of dividend payment and by associated special benefits.
- Preference shares rank above ordinary shares for dividends and in liquidation, but below bonds. Preference dividends can be omitted without default, and preference holders have no vote except in distress.
- Rights issues raise new capital from existing shareholders and prevent dilution; bonus issues raise no capital and are a signalling device; stock splits make shares more accessible and raise no capital; reverse splits consolidate shares, often to escape penny-stock restrictions.
- Equity indices are value-weighted, price-weighted or equal-weighted. Value-weighted indices adjust automatically for capital changes; price-weighted indices give high-priced shares more weight; equal-weighted indices ignore price and size.
- Three valuation approaches exist: net asset value (book, liquidation, revised net asset value), absolute (DCF), and relative (ratios).
- Net asset valuation suits companies whose value is dominated by tangible assets — banks, insurers, property companies.
- DCF discounts projected free cash flow to the firm at the WACC, adds the discounted terminal value, subtracts debt and preferred shares, and divides by shares outstanding.
- DCF is sensitive to the terminal value and to the WACC and growth assumptions, so it is used alongside relative valuation.
- Relative valuation ratios are either shareholder-perspective (P/E, PEG, P/BV, P/RNAV, dividend yield) or firm-perspective (EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA, EV/CF).
- P/E must be read against history and peers, and against the qualitative reasons for a premium or discount.
- PEG controls for expected growth and is most useful for high-growth companies; a PEG below 1 is attractive but rare.
- P/BV suits banks, insurers and property companies, but is distorted by intangibles, inadequate provisions and hidden reserves.
- P/Sales is useful where earnings are negative but ignores expenses and debt; EV/Sales is conceptually consistent because both numerator and denominator relate to the whole enterprise.
- EV/CF uses cash flow because it is harder to manipulate than earnings.
- EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E because it is unaffected by capital structure and closer to cash earnings.
- Dividend yield is the income component of return; the payout ratio is dividends to earnings and the retention ratio is 1 minus the payout ratio.
- Cash per share matters because excess cash supports dividends and makes a company an acquisition target.
- Strategic value and break-up value matter in acquisitions.
- The earnings yield is the inverse of P/E and is compared with bond yields; the difference is the yield gap.
- IPOs on the SGX Mainboard must meet listing requirements; Catalist listings are sponsor-supervised with no quantitative requirements.
- IPO risks include vendor share sales, deferred shares and options, the over-allotment option and claw-back provisions.
- Depositary receipts give access to foreign markets; ADRs have three levels, and only Level 3 raises new capital.
- DR risks include currency, price, counterparty, single-listing illiquidity and limited fungibility.

## 6.13 Additional Reference Materials

The dividend discount model is used only occasionally, for companies with high dividend payouts (**DPS/EPS above 80%**). It is set out here in full because the examination tests the formulas and the sensitivity of the result.

### 6.13.1 Dividend Discount Model

The intrinsic value of an asset can be estimated by the dividend discount approach, which determines the present value of a security. Under the DDM, the intrinsic value of a common share is the **present value of all future dividends**:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{(1+R)} + \frac{D_2}{(1+R)^2} + \frac{D_3}{(1+R)^3} + \dots + \frac{D_\infty}{(1+R)^\infty}$$

where $$P_0$$ is the intrinsic or theoretical value of the share today, $$D_t$$ is the dividend expected at the end of period $$t$$, and $$R$$ is the required rate of return (discount rate) on the share.

The difficulty with implementing this as a valuation model is that investors must forecast and discount an **infinite** stream of dividends — both insurmountable tasks. The model can be modified to account for a subsequent sale of the shares:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{(1+R)} + \frac{D_2}{(1+R)^2} + \dots + \frac{D_n}{(1+R)^n} + \frac{P_n}{(1+R)^n}$$

where $$n$$ is the holding period and $$P_n$$ the expected selling price at the end of period $$n$$.

**Worked example.** A 3-year holding period, dividends of **$1.00**, **$1.05** and **$1.10** at the end of each year, an expected selling price of **$10** at the end of year 3, and a **12%** required return:

$$P_0 = \frac{1.00}{1.12} + \frac{1.05}{(1.12)^2} + \frac{1.10}{(1.12)^3} + \frac{10.00}{(1.12)^3} = 0.8929 + 0.8371 + 0.7830 + 7.1178 = \$9.63$$

Compare the estimated value with the price to decide whether to buy.

### 6.13.2 Constant Growth Dividend Discount Model

When dividends grow at a constant rate $$g$$, the model generalises to:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_0(1+g)}{(1+R)} + \frac{D_0(1+g)^2}{(1+R)^2} + \frac{D_0(1+g)^3}{(1+R)^3} + \dots + \frac{D_0(1+g)^\infty}{(1+R)^\infty}$$

where $$D_0$$ is the current dividend and $$g$$ the constant growth rate. This simplifies to:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{R - g}$$

where $$D_1$$ is the dividend expected at the end of period 1. The model suits the valuation of **very large, broadly diversified companies** and is often called the **Gordon model**, after Myron J. Gordon.

Three assumptions:

- Dividends grow at a **constant rate**.
- The constant growth continues **indefinitely**.
- The required rate of return $$R$$ is **greater than** $$g$$. If not, the model does not apply.

Rearranging gives the required rate of return:

$$R = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$

**Worked example.** Alpine Limited pays dividends per share of **$0.20**, expected to grow at **5%** a year for the foreseeable future; investors require **12%** for investments of similar risk.

$$P_0 = \frac{0.20 \times 1.05}{0.12 - 0.05} = \frac{0.21}{0.07} = \$3.00$$

The current dividend must be compounded for one period because the formula uses the dividend expected one period from now.

**Sensitivity.** A small change in either estimate has a large effect:

| Change | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Base case | 0.21 / (0.12 − 0.05) | **$3.00** |
| $$R$$ rises from 0.12 to 0.13 | 0.21 / (0.13 − 0.05) | **$2.63** |
| $$g$$ rises from 0.05 to 0.06 | 0.212 / (0.12 − 0.06) | **$3.53** |

A **1%** change in either $$g$$ or $$R$$ produces a large difference in estimated value. The crucial relationship is the **spread** between $$R$$ and $$g$$: anything that narrows the spread raises the estimated value, and anything that widens it lowers the value.

### 6.13.3 Estimating Expected Growth Rate of Dividends

A starting point is the company's **sustainable growth rate** — the maximum growth achievable while maintaining a constant debt-equity ratio and without external equity financing:

$$g = \text{Retention Rate} \times \text{Return on Equity} = RR \times ROE$$

A firm can raise sustainable growth by increasing its retention rate (reducing the payout ratio) and reinvesting at its historical ROE, or by maintaining the retention rate while raising ROE.

**Worked example.** A firm retains **60%** of earnings with an ROE of **15%**: $$g = 0.6 \times 0.15 = 0.09 = 9\%$$.

**Applying the model to P&G.** For 2016, P&G's EPS was **3.80** and DPS **2.66**.

*Required return.* The average price return of the S&P 500 over the last 20 years, including the GFC and dot-com bubble, was **+7.36%**; the annual dividend yield was about **1.92%**, giving a total return of about **9.28%**. So $$R_m = 9.28\%$$.

*Cost of equity by CAPM.* With $$R_f = 2.33\%$$ and $$\beta = 0.68$$:

$$R_{P\&G} = 2.33 + 0.68(9.28 - 2.33) = 7.056\%$$

*Growth rate.* Average ROE for 2016 was net income / average shareholders' equity = 10,508 / 60,516.5 = **17.36%**. The payout ratio was DPS/EPS = 2.66/3.80 = **0.70**. So:

$$g = 0.1736 \times (1 - 0.70) = 0.0521 = 5.21\%$$

As a consistency check, annual DPS growth over the last five years was $$(2.66/1.97)^{1/5} - 1 = 0.0619$$.

*Applying the constant growth model.*

$$P_0 = \frac{2.66 \times 1.0521}{0.07056 - 0.0521} = \frac{2.7986}{0.0185} = \$151.28$$

That is almost **twice** P&G's price of **$87.65** at the close of 7 July 2017. The wide difference highlights the model's problem: the estimated value swings widely with small changes in $$R$$ and $$g$$. When $$R$$ equals $$g$$ the value goes to **infinity**, and the model is invalidated when the required return is less than the growth rate.

### 6.13.4 Multi-Stage Dividend Discount Growth Model

Multi-stage growth describes a situation where expected dividend growth must be described using **two or more growth rates**. A two-stage model assumes near-term growth at a given rate for some period, typically **2 to 10 years**, followed by a different sustainable long-term rate.

**a. Two-stage DDM.** Many companies grow rapidly for several years and then slow to a stable rate; others pay no dividends for several years during early growth. The constant growth model is inappropriate for these firms because growth is not constant throughout.

The two-stage model forecasts a growth rate for the near term, when dividends are more predictable, and assumes a long-term rate close to long-run GDP growth, possibly **1–2 percentage points** above it:

$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{D_0(1+g_1)^t}{(1+R)^t} + \frac{\dfrac{D_n(1+g_c)}{R - g_c}}{(1+R)^n}$$

where $$g_1$$ is the growth rate when dividends are more predictable, $$g_c$$ the constant growth rate when they are less predictable, $$n$$ the number of periods of supernormal or subnormal growth, and $$D_n$$ the dividend at the end of the abnormal growth period.

The first term discounts the dividend stream for the first $$n$$ periods at $$R$$. The second term is the constant-growth version, dividing the dividend expected at the end of period $$n+1$$ by $$R - g_c$$; because the result is a present value at the end of period $$n$$, it must be discounted back to time zero.

**Worked example — P&G.** Assume confidence in projecting dividends for the first **five years**, with long-run constant growth thereafter. $$D_0 = \$2.66$$, $$R = 7\%$$, $$g_1 = 5\%$$, $$g_c = 3.5\%$$ (above the US long-run GDP growth of 2%).

Projected dividends:

| Year | Calculation | Dividend |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.66 × 1.05 | $2.7930 |
| 2 | 2.66 × 1.05² | $2.9327 |
| 3 | 2.66 × 1.05³ | $3.0793 |
| 4 | 2.66 × 1.05⁴ | $3.2333 |
| 5 | 2.66 × 1.05⁵ | $3.3950 |

The expected price at the end of year 5, when long-run growth of 3.5% begins:

$$D_6 = 3.3950 \times 1.035 = 3.5138 \qquad P_5 = \frac{3.5138}{0.07 - 0.035} = 100.3943$$

Summing the present values:

$$P_0 = \frac{2.7930}{1.07} + \frac{2.9327}{1.07^2} + \frac{3.0793}{1.07^3} + \frac{3.2333}{1.07^4} + \frac{3.3950}{1.07^5} + \frac{100.3943}{1.07^5}$$

$$= 2.6103 + 2.5615 + 2.5136 + 2.4667 + 2.4206 + 71.5797 = \$84.15$$

This comes close to P&G's current price, so on this model P&G is slightly **overvalued**. The model still suffers the same sensitivity: using **3%** instead of 3.5% for long-run growth reduces the valuation by about **10%** to **$74.90**. Because about **85%** of the value (71.5797 / 84.15) comes from the terminal value, anything affecting the terminal value affects the model significantly.

**b. Three-stage DDM.** The model can be extended by assuming a different growth rate for years 6 to 10. This is useful for gradually tapering dividend growth to the eventual terminal rate: Stage 1 (years 1–5) supernormal growth, Stage 2 (years 6–10) a gradual slowdown, Stage 3 (year 11 onwards) a long-run sustainable level.

**c. Practical application.** The DDM is best applied when dividend payouts are **much higher** and more of the estimate can rest on the more predictable early years. If the early years account for a larger proportion of estimated fair value, the model depends less on the terminal constant-growth assumption. Accuracy and effectiveness are better for companies in the **mature phase** with very high payouts — **70–120% of EPS**, where DPS can exceed EPS if the company pays out of retained earnings and has sufficient cash. The DDM therefore tends to be more useful for gaming companies, tobacco companies and other mature companies with huge payouts.

**d. Intrinsic value or estimated fair value.** Compare the estimated fair value with the prevailing price. If intrinsic value **exceeds** price, the share is undervalued and should be purchased. If price **exceeds** intrinsic value, the share is overvalued and should be avoided or sold.

### 6.13.5 Zero-Growth Model

The owner of a preference share receives a fixed dividend for an infinite period. Because the dividend is a **perpetuity**, the share value is the stated annual dividend divided by the required rate of return:

$$P_0 = \frac{\text{Dividend}}{\text{Discount Rate}}$$

**Worked example.** A preference share with a **$100** par value pays **$7** a year. At a required return of **8%**, the intrinsic value is $7 / 0.08 = **$87.50**.

## Exam cues

- **Two defining features of an ordinary share:** **residual claim** and **limited liability**.
- **Claim order in liquidation:** creditors (including bondholders) → preference shareholders → ordinary shareholders.
- **Preference shares are a hybrid:** fixed dividend, infinite life, no vote except in distress, dividends omissible without default, ranked above ordinary but below bonds.
- **Preference share features:** cumulative (rare now), callable (usual, with about **5 years** of call protection, priced at a premium to par, higher yield), participation (uncommon), convertibility (uncommon, lowers the yield because the issuer has sold an option).
- **Rights issue raises capital; bonus issue does not.** Rights: 300m shares at $2.00 plus 100m at $1.90 → 400m shares, theoretical ex-rights **$1.975**. Bonus 1-for-3: 400m shares, theoretical ex-bonus **$1.50**.
- **Weighted-average shares for EPS:** (300m × 9 + 400m × 3)/12 = **325m**; revised EPS = ($0.11 × 300m)/325m = **$0.1015**.
- **Renounceable rights are tradable; non-renounceable are not.** The pro-rata renounceable limit rose from **50% to 100%** of share capital.
- **Stock split raises no capital.** OCBC 2-for-1 in **2005** at above $13. **Reverse split:** Citigroup 1-for-10 in **2011**, from **$4.52 to $44.16**; SEC penny stock threshold is **$5**.
- **Dual class shares on SGX since June 2018**, with a maximum of **10 votes** per multiple-vote share.
- **Berkshire Hathaway:** Class A **USD284,570**, Class B **USD189.39**; 1 Class A converts into **1,500** Class B; Class B has **1/200th** the votes. **Alphabet:** Class A 1 vote, Class B 10 votes, Class C no votes.
- **Equity categories:** blue chip (safety, steady dividends), high growth (above **20%** compounded, high P/E, high beta, small dividends), income (high yield, price support at **4–5%** when deposits pay **3%**), cyclical (autos; property and banks in Singapore and Hong Kong), defensive (pharmaceuticals, food, utilities, transport — Nestlé, Coca-Cola).
- **Index weighting:** value-weighted (S&P 500, STI), price-weighted (DJIA, 30 shares), equal-weighted (Value Line, geometric average, over **80%** NYSE-listed).
- **Value-weighted worked example:** base 64,000,000 = 100; new 67,000,000 → **105**.
- **Equal-weighted worked example:** (1.04 × 1.03 × 1.05)^(1/3) = **1.040**.
- **Value-weighted indices adjust automatically for bonus and rights issues**; price-weighted indices need a divisor adjustment, and a split reduces a share's weight.
- **Three valuation approaches:** net asset value, absolute (DCF), relative.
- **DCF steps:** WACC → project FCFF for **10 years** → terminal value at year 10 → discount → enterprise value → subtract debt and preferred → divide by shares.
- **CAPM:** $$R = R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$$. P&G: 2.33 + 0.68(9.28 − 2.33) = **7.056%**.
- **WACC worked example:** (1 − 0.23)(0.014)(30.414/254.584) + (0.07)(224.17/254.584) = **6.29%**.
- **Terminal value:** $$E(FCFF_{11}) / (\text{WACC} - g_c)$$, then discounted 10 years. The discount rate must **exceed** the growth rate.
- **P&G DCF result:** enterprise value **USD260,391m**, less debt 30,514 and preferred 1,038 → equity **228,839m**, divided by 2,557.6m shares = **USD89.47** against a price of **USD87.65** (about **2%** upside).
- **P/E worked example:** INTC at 34.68 with EPS 2.72 → trailing **12.8x**; 2017 forecast 2.86 → **12.1x**; 2018 forecast 2.97 → **11.7x**. INTC's normal range is **10.3x–16.0x**; a move to 14x implies **18–20%** upside.
- **PEG:** P/E divided by expected EPS growth. Alibaba's P/E of **44.5** with **28%** growth gives a PEG of **1.6** — among the lowest. A PEG **below 1** is attractive but rare.
- **P/BV suits banks, insurers and property companies.** Hidden reserves make book value understated, so the true P/BV is **lower** than calculated.
- **P/Sales is conceptually flawed** because price relates to shareholders while sales are generated by debt and equity; **EV/Sales is consistent**.
- **EV/CF worked example:** (18 × 1,000 + 400 + 10 − 8,000) / 2,210 = **4.71**. A **lower** ratio is better.
- **EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E** because it is unaffected by capital structure and closer to cash earnings; it became widely used in the **1980s**.
- **Dividend yield:** $0.10 on a $2.00 price = **5%**. Payout ratio 0.10/0.40 = **25%**; retention ratio **75%**.
- **Yield compression:** a 3-cent dividend at a 7.5% required yield implies a price of **40 cents**.
- **REITs must distribute 90% of distributable income**, which is why a DDM applies.
- **Cash per share:** Company A at 2.50 with 0.50 net cash and EPS 0.25 is effectively **8x** after netting cash, versus Company B at **10x**.
- **Earnings yield is the inverse of P/E.** The **yield gap** is the earnings yield minus the bond yield; **1.5–2.0%** above Treasuries leaves room to rise. October 2017: Treasury **2.45%**, S&P 500 earnings yield about **4%**.
- **SGX Mainboard requires quantitative listing requirements; Catalist is sponsor-supervised with none.**
- **IPO tranches:** private placement (institutions and accredited investors, usually the larger portion) and public retail (smaller, widely distributed). Offer opens at least **2 market days**; trading starts the next business day on a **T+2** basis.
- **IPO risks:** vendor share sales, deferred shares and options, the **Greenshoe** over-allotment option, and **claw-back**.
- **Rights issues after listing lodge an Offer Information Statement (OIS)**, not a prospectus.
- **ADR levels:** Level 1 OTC (no listing, no capital raising), Level 2 exchange-listed (no capital raising), Level 3 exchange-listed **with** capital raising. **GDRs** are issued offshore from the US, commonly London and Luxembourg.
- **DR risks:** currency, price (ADRs often trade at a **premium**), counterparty, single-listing illiquidity, and limited **two-way fungibility**.
- **Trap:** a **bonus issue** and a **stock split** both raise no capital, but a bonus issue is a capitalisation of reserves while a split simply divides existing shares — the price adjustment differs.
- **Trap:** a **low P/E** is not automatically cheap and a **high P/E** is not automatically expensive; the difference may be expected growth, transparency, impending events or illiquidity.
- **Trap:** **P/BV** uses book value, not market value — for a bank with non-performing loans the market value of assets may be far below book.
- **Trap:** the **terminal value** usually dominates a DCF; in the P&G two-stage example about **85%** of value came from the terminal value.
