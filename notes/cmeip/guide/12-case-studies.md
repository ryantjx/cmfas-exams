# Chapter 12 — Case Studies

The examination includes **3 case studies carrying 9 questions** — 3 questions per case study, about a tenth of the paper. The ten case studies in the official guide are the model for those questions: each sets a named client or scenario, then tests one chapter's method applied to it. This chapter works through all ten, with the arithmetic shown.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Apply the investor-classification framework to a named client.
- Compute expected return, standard deviation, coefficient of variation and real future value.
- Read economic indicators and business-cycle phases to select asset classes.
- Attribute a price fall to the correct behavioural driver.
- Compute a carry trade's gain under three exchange-rate scenarios.
- Compute prospective P/E, EPS growth and PEG, and act on the result.
- Explain the DCF steps and compute enterprise value.
- Read bond prices, yields and duration to judge interest-rate sensitivity.
- Match a strategic asset allocation to a client's objectives and constraints.
- Compute warrant intrinsic value, minimum gain and read an FX quote.
- Distinguish unit trusts, business trusts and REITs, and select the vehicle that fits an income objective.

## 12.1 Individual versus Institutional Investor

**Scenario.** John Lim is moving from stockbroking to a private bank managing accredited investors. In his current job he works mostly with institutional investors, so he must adjust to the differences in client type. He asks you, as his soon-to-be colleague, to explain the key differences.

### Question 1 — Which of the following is an accredited investor? (Select all that apply)

- a. An entity with S$5 million worth of net assets.
- b. A university lecturer with $500,000 in net personal assets.
- c. An accountant that earns S$400,000 a year.
- d. A university endowment trust that has S$6 billion in investible funds.

**Answer: c and d.**

Options (a) and (b) are incorrect because neither the individual nor the entity qualifies. Option (c) is an accredited investor because the individual earns **more than S$300,000** a year. Option (d) is an accredited investor because the trust has funds **exceeding S$10 million** in value.

### Question 2 — Which of the following is not likely to be a return objective of an institutional investor?

- a. Current income to meet recurring liquidity needs.
- b. Long-term capital gain to beat inflation.
- c. Potential for higher yields.
- d. Regular savings to fund children's education.

**Answer: d.**

Options (a) to (c) can be return objectives of both individual and institutional investors. Option (d) is likely to be **unique to an individual investor**.

### Question 3 — Which of the following shows the key differences between an accredited investor and a retail investor? (Select all that apply)

- a. A retail investor is likely to be more risk averse than an accredited investor.
- b. The accredited investor can withstand a higher level of monetary risk than the retail investor.
- c. A retail investor has a longer investment time horizon than an accredited investor.
- d. An accredited investor is able to access a wider range of investment products than a retail investor.

**Answer: a, b and d.**

Option (a) is correct: retail investors usually trade in small amounts and less frequently, and have little influence over corporate decisions or the capital markets. Option (b) is correct: given their wealth, accredited investors are considered financially savvy and better able to understand financial instruments, so they can withstand more monetary risk. Option (c) is **incorrect** because the investment time horizon depends on the **needs of the investor, not the type**. Option (d) is correct: accredited investors may access more complex and riskier products such as **unrated bonds** and products requiring neither a prospectus nor a reasonable basis for recommendation by a CMI representative.

## 12.2 Risk, Return and Time Value Calculations

**Scenario.** Edna Wong has just turned **35** and wants to set aside **$200,000 for 30 years** in an investment with the following annual returns under different economic scenarios. The average long-term inflation rate is expected to be **2%**.

| State of economy | Probability | Return |
|---|---|---|
| Boom | 0.3 | 12% |
| Normal | 0.5 | 8% |
| Recession | 0.2 | −6% |

### Question 1 — The expected rate of return is:

- a. 5.30%
- b. 6.40%
- c. 8.45%
- d. 9.28%

**Answer: b.**

The expected return is the probability-weighted average of the returns across economic states:

$$E(R) = 0.3(0.12) + 0.5(0.08) + 0.2(-0.06) = 0.0640 = 6.40\%$$

### Question 2 — The coefficient of variation associated with this investment is:

- a. 0.47
- b. 0.68
- c. 1.01
- d. 1.33

**Answer: c.**

Variance is the probability-weighted average of the squared deviations from the mean:

$$\sigma^2 = 0.3(0.12 - 0.064)^2 + 0.5(0.08 - 0.064)^2 + 0.2(-0.06 - 0.064)^2 = 0.004144$$

$$\sigma = \sqrt{0.004144} = 0.064374 = 6.4374\%$$

$$CV = \frac{0.064374}{0.064} = 1.01$$

### Question 3 — Assuming the expected return is the average return for the next thirty years, the size of Edna's nest egg when she turns 65, in today's dollars, is:

- a. $709,264.72
- b. $1,610,176.77
- c. $2,279,904.66
- d. $2,865,831.06

**Answer: a.**

With expected inflation of 2%, the real rate of return is:

$$\frac{1 + 0.064}{1 + 0.02} - 1 = 0.0431 = 4.31\%$$

Future value in real terms:

$$200{,}000 \times (1.0431)^{30} = \$709{,}264.72$$

## 12.3 Key Drivers of Market Movement and Assets

**Scenario.** You are part of a team producing the market outlook for the US, China and Southeast Asia over the next 12 months. Your boss asks for a standard list of items to cover, forming an outline of the key topics the research team should provide for the sales force and clients.

### Question 1 — One key concern of your US clients is the timing of a future economic downturn. Which of the following suggests a potential downturn?

- a. Average duration of unemployment is increasing.
- b. The 10-year T-bond spread over the Fed Funds rate is increasing.
- c. Disposable personal incomes reported by the Bureau of Economic Analysis start to rise.
- d. New orders by suppliers start to decline.

**Answer: d.**

Option (a) is a **lagging** indicator, so it is incorrect. Option (c) is a **coincident** indicator and its direction is inconsistent with a downturn. Option (b) is a leading indicator, but an **increase** in the spread generally bodes **well** for economic health. Option (d) is correct because suppliers normally cut orders when they expect demand to drop.

### Question 2 — Strategists postulate a slowdown in the Chinese economy occurring with rising interest rates. Which asset classes are likely to do well?

- a. Cash, real estate
- b. Defensive stocks, investment grade bonds
- c. Cyclical stocks, high-yield bonds
- d. Growth stocks, precious metals

**Answer: b.**

A slowdown with rising interest rates is characteristic of the **late bull to early bear** phase. Defensive stocks and investment grade bonds are likely to do well.

Option (a) is incorrect: although raising cash may be wise, holding real estate is risky because it is typically **leveraged** and rates are rising. Option (c) is possibly the worst: cyclical stocks perform worse than the market when things turn down, and high-yield issuers may default. Option (d) is incorrect: some precious metals allocation may help in uncertainty, but growth stocks are less likely to meet growth expectations and may see **P/E multiple contraction**.

### Question 3 — Uninformed fund managers reduced positions out of fear because news spread that many were doing likewise. Even though the scenario was unlikely, massive selling triggered computer-driven strategies. What is the root cause of the price fall?

- a. Herd instinct
- b. Loss aversion
- c. Cognitive bias
- d. Algorithmic trading

**Answer: a.**

The managers simply followed the crowd without exercising independent decision-making or due diligence — **herd instinct**. Loss aversion may have influenced them and algorithmic trading may have **exacerbated** the fall, but neither provided the **original impetus**. There is insufficient information about how the original discussants formed their conclusions to attribute the fall to cognitive bias.

## 12.4 Foreign Exchange Carry Trade

**Scenario.** An investor is tempted by **IDR 100 billion** of Indonesian government bonds. IDR-denominated 1-year government bonds pay **6%**, while Singapore banks charge **1.5%** for investment-related loans. SGD/IDR stands at **10,076.68**. Assess three exchange-rate scenarios.

### Question 1 — Ignoring bond price changes, what is the annual gain in SGD if the exchange rate stays flat?

- a. SGD 148,858.55
- b. SGD 446,575.66
- c. SGD 595,434.21
- d. SGD 9,923,903.51

**Answer: b.**

With a flat rate there is no currency gain or loss, so work in SGD. The IDR 100bn purchase is funded by an SGD-equivalent loan:

$$\frac{100{,}000{,}000{,}000}{10{,}076.68} = \text{SGD } 9{,}923{,}903.51$$

The investor earns 6% on the bonds and pays 1.5% on the loan, so the annual gain is:

$$(0.06 - 0.015) \times 9{,}923{,}903.51 = \text{SGD } 446{,}575.66$$

### Question 2 — What is the total gain if the IDR weakens against the SGD by 10% over the year?

- a. −SGD 605,357.18
- b. −SGD 942,771.98
- c. SGD 605,357.18
- d. SGD 942,771.98

**Answer: a.**

The loan is **SGD 9,923,903.51**, so annual interest expense is:

$$0.015 \times 9{,}923{,}903.51 = \text{SGD } 148{,}858.55$$

Interest income is **IDR 6 billion** (6% of IDR 100bn). With the IDR weakening 10%, the conversion rate is:

$$\frac{10{,}076.68}{0.9} = 11{,}196.31$$

SGD-equivalent interest income:

$$\frac{6{,}000{,}000{,}000}{11{,}196.31} = \text{SGD } 535{,}890.84$$

The face value of the bonds converts back at 11,196.31:

$$\frac{100{,}000{,}000{,}000}{11{,}196.31} = \text{SGD } 8{,}931{,}514.04$$

Total gain:

$$(8{,}931{,}514.04 - 9{,}923{,}903.51) + (535{,}890.84 - 148{,}858.55) = -\text{SGD } 605{,}357.18$$

The interest income exceeds the borrowing expense, but the unfavourable FX movement has depreciated both the investment and the income. **The carry trade is not profitable.**

### Question 3 — If in a year SGD/USD is 0.7 and USD/IDR is 13,086.60, how does the gain compare with Question 1?

- a. Lower gain
- b. Same gain
- c. Higher gain
- d. Insufficient information

**Answer: c.**

The SGD/IDR cross rate is:

$$0.7 \times 13{,}086.60 = 9{,}160.62$$

Since **9,160.62 is lower than 10,076.68**, the IDR has **appreciated** against the SGD. The IDR-denominated investment and income appreciate in value, so the gain is **higher** than the SGD 446,575.66 in Question 1.

## 12.5 Equities Analysis

**Scenario.** Mr Quek has been recommended **Mvidia**, a semiconductor company with strong earnings growth. Demand for semiconductors in the PC industry remains sluggish, but companies focused on **high-performance computing (HPC)** — cloud computing, big data, artificial intelligence — are better positioned for strong growth. He has also heard that Mvidia's P/E is too high. Mvidia's stock price is **USD 40**.

| Year | EPS | EPS change % |
|---|---|---|
| FY18A | 1.12 | −5.0 |
| FY19E | 1.08 | −3.6 |
| FY20F | 2.57 | +138.0 |
| FY21F | 4.19 | +63.0 |
| FY22F | 4.67 | +11.4 |
| FY23F | 5.81 | +24.4 |

### Question 1 — What is the prospective P/E for Mvidia and why?

- a. The high ratio of 35.71 is likely because the market has overvalued Mvidia.
- b. The high ratio of 35.71 is likely because the market is factoring in Mvidia's growth potential.
- c. The high ratio of 37.04 is likely because the market has overvalued Mvidia.
- d. The high ratio of 37.04 is likely because the market is factoring in Mvidia's growth potential.

**Answer: d.**

$$\text{Prospective P/E} = \frac{40}{1.08} = 37.04x$$

P/E ratios can be high because of strong expected future growth, so the high prospective P/E is likely because investors are factoring in Mvidia's **high double-digit long-term growth potential**.

### Question 2 — What is the 5-year compounded annualised EPS growth rate?

- a. 31.57%
- b. 38.99%
- c. 40.01%
- d. 52.30%

**Answer: b.**

$$\text{5-year EPS growth} = \left[ \left( \frac{5.81}{1.12} \right)^{1/5} - 1 \right] \times 100\% = 38.99\%$$

### Question 3 — What recommendation would you give using Mvidia's prospective PEG ratio?

- a. Buy Mvidia given the PEG ratio of 0.92.
- b. Sell Mvidia given the PEG ratio of 0.92.
- c. Buy Mvidia given the PEG ratio of 0.95.
- d. Sell Mvidia given the PEG ratio of 0.95.

**Answer: c.**

$$\text{Prospective PEG} = \frac{37.04}{38.99} = 0.95$$

Because the PEG is **less than 1**, Mvidia is **slightly undervalued** even after accounting for its strong expected growth, so Mr Quek can consider **buying** the stock.

## 12.6 Estimating the Fair Value of a Stock

**Scenario.** A new client wants a brief explanation of the steps your firm's research team undertakes when estimating a company's fair value.

### Question 1 — Free cash flows to a levered firm should be discounted using the firm's:

- a. Cost of ordinary equity only.
- b. Cost of preferred equity only.
- c. Cost of debt only.
- d. Weighted average cost of capital.

**Answer: d.**

The appropriate discount rate must account for capital from **all sources** for which a return is required. Option (a) would be correct only if the firm were **all-equity funded**, which it is not because it is levered. Options (b) and (c) are incorrect under any circumstance, since a firm must have ordinary equity.

### Question 2 — Which of the following is NOT part of estimating free cash flows to the firm (FCFF)?

- a. Interviewing company management on its revenue growth potential.
- b. Consulting industry specialists on expected profitability margins.
- c. Estimating the amounts of future fixed asset investments.
- d. Analysing historical ratios to make interest expense projections.

**Answer: d.**

Revenue growth potential and profitability margins are needed to derive **EBIT**, a key FCFF input, so (a) and (b) are part of the estimate. Capital expenditure is another key input, so (c) is part of it. **Interest expense is excluded** from the FCFF computation, so (d) is not part of it.

### Question 3 — Enterprise value can be calculated using ________. (Select all that apply)

- a. Sum of the present values of the FCFFs for the projection period plus the terminal value.
- b. Sum of the present values of the FCFFs for the projection period plus the present value of the terminal value.
- c. Sum of the FCFFs for the projection period plus the present value of the terminal value.
- d. Sum of the estimated market values of debt, common stock and preferred stock.

**Answer: b and d.**

Enterprise value can be calculated in two equivalent ways: the worth today of the cash flows the firm generates, or the value today of the claims on those cash flows. Option (a) fails to discount the terminal value; option (c) fails to discount the FCFFs in the projection period.

## 12.7 Fixed-Income Investments

**Scenario.** David is offered three USD fixed income instruments. The market expects interest rates to **rise** over the next year as the commodities, offshore oil, coal mining and energy industries come out of a slump.

| Bond | Maturity | Yield to maturity | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Offshore Oil Corp: 10-year 6% **callable** bond | 5 years left | 4.5% | A− |
| Diversified Commodities Company: 10-year **4%** bond | 6 years left | 6.0% | BBB |
| Indonesian Coal Mining & Energy Company: **8%** bond | 4 years left | 7.0% | unrated |

### Question 1 — Why can some bonds be bought below face value?

- a. The Indonesian Coal Mining & Energy Company bonds are unrated.
- b. The New Offshore Oil Corp bonds are callable.
- c. The YTM of the Diversified Commodities Company bonds is higher than the coupon rate.
- d. The YTMs of the New Offshore Oil Corp and Indonesian Coal Mining & Energy Company bonds are lower than their respective coupon rates.

**Answer: c.**

A bond must be priced at a **discount** for its YTM to be **higher** than its coupon rate. Option (d) is wrong because those bonds will be priced at a **premium** (4.5% < 6%, and 7.0% < 8%). While investors may require higher yields for unrated and for callable bonds, that does not necessarily mean the yields exceed the respective coupon rates, so (a) and (b) are incorrect.

### Question 2 — If David holds his bond to maturity, what should he be most concerned with?

- a. A bond is callable.
- b. Interest rates are increasing.
- c. Interest rates are decreasing.
- d. The coupon rate of a bond.

**Answer: a.**

A callable bond can be redeemed before maturity, and if that happens the investor faces **reinvestment risk** from having to reinvest the proceeds at lower yields.

Holding to maturity means the **YTM**, not the coupon rate, is the relevant measure — though to realise the YTM, coupons must be reinvested at the same yield. Option (a) is a much bigger concern than (d). Changes in interest rates affect price, but that is of little concern to a hold-to-maturity investor because the price **converges to par** on maturity, so (b) and (c) are incorrect.

### Question 3 — Which scenario is likely in the coming year?

- a. Diversified Commodities Company bond price is likely to decrease the most in percentage terms.
- b. New Offshore Oil Corp bonds are likely to be called.
- c. Indonesian Coal Mining & Energy Company bonds are likely to default.
- d. Indonesian Coal Mining & Energy Company bonds are likely to increase the least in percentage terms.

**Answer: a.**

With rates expected to rise, bond prices are likely to fall. The Diversified Commodities Company bonds have the **longest maturity (6 years) and lowest coupon (4%)**, so their prices are **most sensitive** to rate changes.

Option (d) is incorrect: although the Indonesian bonds are least sensitive because of the shortest maturity and highest coupon, a rate rise is likely to **decrease** the price, not increase it. Option (b) is incorrect because rising rates make a call **unlikely**. Option (c) is incorrect: being unrated does not necessarily mean poor credit — the company may simply have chosen not to seek ratings — and with the industries expected to recover, concluding default is a stretch.

## 12.8 Portfolio Management

**Scenario.** Mr Walter Koh is a retiree with **$2 million** of investable assets, all currently passively managed. He is willing to have **$500,000** managed more actively and is prepared to risk **$100,000** of it in full. He plans to gift about **$100,000** from this actively managed fund **three months from now** to an art endowment fund. He is not a big spender but hopes to fund at least part of his annual expenses, which are **highly predictable**, from his active portfolio for the next **twenty years**. He is otherwise not concerned about liquidity.

| Asset class | Portfolio A | Portfolio B | Portfolio C | Portfolio D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Money market funds | 20% | 10% | 20% | 20% |
| Treasury bills | 10% | 0% | 10% | 10% |
| Long-term government bonds | 10% | 80% | 10% | **50%** |
| High yield bonds | 10% | 0% | 40% | 0% |
| Developed-market equities | 50% | 10% | 20% | 20% |

**Castor & Pollux** shares have a **beta of 1.2** and a **past return of 9.5%**, and have been recommended on their alpha potential after thorough fundamental analysis. The **risk-free rate is 3%** and the **market risk premium is 6%**.

### Question 1 — Mr Koh does not believe one can achieve superior returns through fundamental analysis. Which form(s) of market efficiency must he necessarily believe in? (Select all that apply)

- a. Weak form
- b. Semi-weak form
- c. Semi-strong form
- d. Strong form

**Answer: a and c.**

Conducting fundamental analysis to identify a mispriced stock assumes all **publicly available** information is not already reflected in the price. So Mr Koh must believe the market is **semi-strong form** efficient. Because all publicly available information **includes historical price and volume information**, he must also believe it is **weak form** efficient. There is insufficient information about his views on **private** information to conclude he believes in strong-form efficiency. "Semi-weak form" is not a standard classification.

### Question 2 — Jensen's alpha for Castor & Pollux is:

- a. −2.9%
- b. −0.7%
- c. 0.7%
- d. 2.9%

**Answer: b.**

Using CAPM, the required return is:

$$0.03 + 1.2(0.06) = 10.2\%$$

With an actual return of 9.5%:

$$\alpha = 9.5\% - 10.2\% = -0.7\%$$

### Question 3 — Which portfolio best aligns with Mr Koh's objectives?

- a. Portfolio A
- b. Portfolio B
- c. Portfolio C
- d. Portfolio D

**Answer: d.**

Mr Koh needs **$100,000**, which is **20%** of his $500,000 portfolio, as a gift shortly, so he should allocate that to **money market investments**, the most liquid asset class. Portfolio B is incorrect because of its low allocation to money market funds.

Portfolio C is incorrect because its combined **60%** allocation to high-yield bonds and equities is likely too risky given he is only willing to risk up to $100,000. Portfolio A is incorrect for the same reason, and its allocation to long-term government bonds is likely too low to fund his annual expenses.

Portfolio D is correct: sufficient money market funds for the gift, an equity allocation that respects his risk aversion, and a sufficiently high allocation to long-term government bonds so the coupons can fund his annual expenses.

## 12.9 Warrants and Foreign Exchange

**Scenario.** Ms JL Tan has two questions. First, she is looking at the structured warrants of **Poseidon Lines**, a shipping company. Poseidon shares trade at **$2.50** and its warrants at **$1.30**. The warrants have a **conversion ratio of 1:1** and an **exercise price of $1.50**. Second, she has sold some USD-denominated ADRs and will receive **USD 50,000 in 2 business days**, which she wants to convert to SGD.

### Question 1 — What is the intrinsic value of Poseidon's structured warrant?

- a. $0
- b. $1
- c. $1.50
- d. $2.50

**Answer: b.**

The warrant is in-the-money, so:

$$\text{Intrinsic value} = \frac{2.50 - 1.50}{1} = \$1$$

### Question 2 — You expect Poseidon's share price to rise to $4.00. What is Ms Tan's minimum gain on the warrants in percentage terms if she buys today?

- a. 92.31%
- b. 207.69%
- c. 307.69%
- d. Impossible to determine because the future price of the structured warrant is not known with certainty.

**Answer: a.**

If the share price rises to $4.00, the warrant's intrinsic value is:

$$\frac{4.00 - 1.50}{1} = \$2.50$$

Because the conversion ratio is 1, the **minimum value** of the warrant is its intrinsic value of $2.50. The minimum percentage gain is:

$$\frac{2.50}{1.30} - 1 = 92.31\%$$

Option (d) is incorrect: although the future warrant price is unknown, only the future **minimum** price is needed to compute the minimum percentage gain.

### Question 3 — Ms Tan has two quotes. The best quote at which she can sell her ADR proceeds for SGD is:

Quote A: USD/SGD 1.2500 – 1.2550
Quote B: USD/SGD 1.2475 – 1.2515

- a. USD/SGD 1.2500
- b. USD/SGD 1.2550
- c. USD/SGD 1.2475
- d. USD/SGD 1.2515

**Answer: a.**

All quotations give the number of SGD (the counter currency) per one USD (the base currency). For each quote, the **first rate is the price at which the market maker will buy USD** and the second is the price at which it will sell USD. Since Ms Tan wants to **sell** USD, the **first rate** of each quote applies, and the **highest** of those is best: **1.2500**.

## 12.10 Unit Trusts, REITs and Business Trusts

**Scenario.** Mr James Lim finds the jargon of collective investment schemes confusing. He asks you to demystify the differences between unit trusts, business trusts and REITs. He has investable funds and is deciding whether to invest in a property or one of these schemes. He is **more interested in a steady stream of income** than in large trading gains.

### Question 1 — The common features among unit trusts, business trusts and REITs is/are that: (Select all that apply)

- a. They are established under a trust deed.
- b. They are exchange-traded.
- c. They allow for diversification.
- d. They are professionally managed.

**Answer: a, c and d.**

All three are established under a **trust deed**. By pooling and investing funds, all three allow **diversification**, albeit to different extents. The assets under all three are **professionally managed**. Option (b) is incorrect: while all REITs are listed, unit trusts are typically **not** listed and some business trusts are unlisted too.

### Question 2 — The main difference(s) between business trusts and REITs is/are that: (Select all that apply)

- a. The trustee and manager are separate in a REIT but not in a business trust.
- b. Business trusts do not face investment restrictions, unlike REITs.
- c. Business trusts are unlisted whereas REITs are listed.
- d. There is a 75% gearing limit applicable on REITs but only a 45% gearing limit on business trusts.

**Answer: a and b.**

Unlike a REIT, a business trust is a **single responsible entity** organisation with a **trustee-manager**, so (a) is correct. Business trusts can actively engage in any business area, whereas REITs must invest only in real estate for rental income, so (b) is correct. Option (c) is incorrect because although some business trusts are unlisted, **most are listed**. Option (d) is incorrect: there is **no gearing limit** for business trusts and a **45%** limit for REITs.

### Question 3 — Given Mr Lim's objectives, the scheme most likely to suit his need is:

- a. A unit trust described as an aggressive fund.
- b. A business trust comprising investments in golf course assets.
- c. A REIT that focuses on heartland retail malls.
- d. All of the above.

**Answer: c.**

An **aggressive** unit trust generates returns from capital gains and is unlikely to provide a steady income stream, so (a) is incorrect. Cash flows from **golf course** assets are highly sensitive to economic fluctuations, and there is **no mandatory minimum distribution** for business trusts, so (b) will not provide steady income either. A **REIT** is often seen as an alternative to physical real estate and typically distributes at least **90% of annual income**, which is likely to be more stable for **heartland retail malls**. Option (c) is therefore the best answer, which also makes (d) incorrect.

## Exam cues

- **Case studies carry 3 questions each, 9 in total** — about a tenth of the 90-question paper. Each tests one chapter's method applied to a named client.
- **Accredited investor tests:** individual — net personal assets **> S$2m** (residence capped at S$1m), financial assets **> S$1m**, or income **≥ S$300k** in the preceding 12 months; entity — net assets **> S$10m**.
- **Time horizon depends on the investor's needs, not the investor type.** This is the standard distractor.
- **Expected return** $$= \sum r_i p_i$$. Worked: 0.3(12%) + 0.5(8%) + 0.2(−6%) = **6.40%**.
- **Variance and CV:** variance **0.004144**, σ **6.4374%**, CV **1.01**.
- **Real future value:** real rate = (1.064/1.02) − 1 = **4.31%**; $200,000 × 1.0431³⁰ = **$709,264.72**.
- **Leading indicators** predict; **coincident** confirm the present; **lagging** confirm the past. **New orders declining** signals a downturn; **average duration of unemployment** is lagging; **disposable personal income** is coincident.
- **Slowdown with rising rates = late bull to early bear** → **defensive stocks and investment grade bonds**.
- **Herd instinct** is the root cause when managers follow the crowd without independent analysis; loss aversion and algorithmic trading may **exacerbate** but do not originate.
- **Carry trade worked example:** IDR 100bn at 10,076.68 = **SGD 9,923,903.51** loan. Flat rate → **+SGD 446,575.66**. IDR weakens 10% (rate 11,196.31) → **−SGD 605,357.18**. Cross rate 0.7 × 13,086.60 = **9,160.62** (IDR appreciated) → **higher gain**.
- **Prospective P/E** = price / next-year EPS. Mvidia: 40/1.08 = **37.04x**. A high P/E usually reflects **growth expectations**, not overvaluation.
- **5-year EPS growth** = (5.81/1.12)^(1/5) − 1 = **38.99%**.
- **PEG** = prospective P/E / EPS growth = 37.04/38.99 = **0.95**. **Below 1 → undervalued → buy.**
- **FCFF is discounted at WACC**, because capital comes from all sources. **Interest expense is excluded** from FCFF.
- **Enterprise value** = PV of FCFFs + **PV** of terminal value = market value of debt + common stock + preferred stock.
- **Bond at a discount** means **YTM > coupon**. New Offshore Oil: 4.5% < 6% → **premium**. Indonesian Coal: 7.0% < 8% → **premium**. Diversified Commodities: 6.0% > 4% → **discount**.
- **Hold-to-maturity investor's main concern is a call feature** (reinvestment risk), not price volatility, because price converges to par.
- **Longest maturity and lowest coupon = most rate-sensitive.** Diversified Commodities (6 years, 4%) falls most when rates rise.
- **Unrated ≠ poor credit.** A company may simply choose not to seek a rating.
- **Fundamental analysis failing implies semi-strong efficiency**, which necessarily implies **weak-form** efficiency too. "Semi-weak" is not a classification.
- **Jensen's alpha** = actual − [$$R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$$]. Castor & Pollux: 9.5% − [3% + 1.2(6%)] = **−0.7%**.
- **Portfolio selection:** match the **liquidity need** (20% for the gift), the **risk budget** ($100,000 of $500,000), and the **income need** (long-term government bond coupons).
- **Warrant intrinsic value** = (SP − EP)/n. Poseidon: (2.50 − 1.50)/1 = **$1**.
- **Minimum gain** uses the **minimum value** (intrinsic value), not the unknown future warrant price: 2.50/1.30 − 1 = **92.31%**.
- **Selling USD:** use the **bid** (first rate) and pick the **highest** — **1.2500**.
- **Common to unit trusts, business trusts and REITs:** trust deed, diversification, professional management. **Not** exchange-trading — unit trusts are typically unlisted.
- **REIT vs business trust:** separate trustee and manager vs **single trustee-manager**; investment restrictions vs **none**; **45%** gearing cap vs **no cap**; **90%** mandatory distribution vs **no minimum**.
- **Income objective → REIT**, because of the **90%** distribution requirement and stable heartland retail cash flows.
- **Trap:** a high P/E is not automatically overvaluation — check the growth rate with PEG before concluding.
- **Trap:** an unrated bond is not automatically a default risk.
- **Trap:** for a hold-to-maturity investor, interest rate risk is largely irrelevant; the call feature is the real risk.
