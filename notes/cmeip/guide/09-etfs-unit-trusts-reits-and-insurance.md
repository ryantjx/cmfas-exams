# Chapter 9 — Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs), Unit Trusts, Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and Insurance

This chapter covers the pooled vehicles an adviser recommends: ETFs, unit trusts, REITs, business trusts, stapled securities, UCITS, the alternative investments (hedge funds, private equity, commodity and real estate funds), and insurance and annuities. The examination tests the mechanics, the fee structures and the suitability judgements.

## 9.1 Introduction

In Singapore, most pooled investments are **Collective Investment Schemes (CIS)** or **unit trusts**, governed by the **Code on Collective Investment Schemes (Code on CIS)** issued by MAS.

Three main structures currently allow a wide group of investors to participate in asset managers' capabilities:

- **Unit trusts.**
- **Investment holding or dealing companies.**
- **Limited partnerships.**

These structures are sometimes less than ideal for investment funds. A company can only pay dividends out of **profits**, and there are restrictions on return of capital. The **Variable Capital Companies Act** was therefore passed in **October 2018** to govern a new structure, the **Variable Capital Company (VCC)** — essentially a company with two key features:

- A VCC can **freely issue and redeem shares and pay dividends using its capital**.
- A VCC can adopt an **umbrella structure with multiple sub-funds**, increasing operating efficiency.

This encourages funds with similar structures in other jurisdictions to **re-domicile** in Singapore and can help Singapore enlarge its share of the global asset management market.

The chapter proceeds from conventional CIS — ETFs and unit trusts, which invest primarily in listed equity and fixed income securities — to alternative CIS focused on niche asset classes and strategies (REITs, business trusts, hedge funds, private equity), and finally to insurance products, which pool not only investment funds but **mortality and longevity risks** as well.

## 9.2 Exchange-Traded Funds

ETFs are funds designed to **track the performance of specific indices, or fixed baskets of stocks or debt securities**. They differ from most open-end and closed-end funds because they involve **no active management** of securities. Underlying assets may be local or international, and beyond stocks an ETF may be based on fixed income, commodities, money market instruments or currency. Through ETFs, investors gain access to a wide range of markets and asset classes.

Over the last two decades ETFs have proliferated on major bourses. Singapore has numerous SGX-listed ETFs tracking major indices and selected asset classes, mostly long ETFs. Popular examples include the **S&P 500 SPDRs** ("Spiders") tracking the S&P 500 Index, and the **DJIA DIAMONDS** tracking the Dow Jones Industrial Average.

**Worked illustration — SPDR Straits Times Index ETF.** Singapore's first locally created ETF, designed to track the STI. Originally listed as "Street TRACKS STI", it has traded on SGX-ST since **April 2002**. Its objective is to replicate the STI's performance as closely as possible, before expenses.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Trading name | STI ETF 100 |
| **Pricing** | About **1/100th** of the STI level. If the STI is at 1,700, each unit is priced at **$17.00** |
| Minimum bid size | $0.01 |
| Board lot size | **100 units** |
| Dividend yield | Expected to approximate the STI yield; dividends paid **twice a year** |
| **Annual costs** | **0.3%** (management fee, trustee fee and other fund expenses) |

The SPDR STI ETF is like a mutual fund tracking the STI, investing in component stocks in proportion to their index weights. ETF units can be bought and sold **during trading hours** like any listed security — unlike mutual funds, which can only be bought or sold at the **end of the day** with the fund manager.

ETFs are **passive** instruments seeking to replicate the price movement of a benchmark index. If replication is good, the ETF's price movement follows the benchmark.

**NAV versus market price.** The ETF's **Net Asset Value (NAV)** reflects the value of the units owned and is determined at the **daily close of trading** in all markets where the ETF is listed, using a process explained in the Trust Deed or Prospectus. NAV per unit represents **fair value**. But the ETF's **market price can deviate from NAV per unit**, because price is also determined by investor sentiment affecting supply and demand.

ETFs are designed to **minimise commissions and expense ratios** compared with an average mutual fund. Annual fees are lower because ETFs are passively managed. The main costs are the **total expense ratio** and **brokerage commissions** on buying or selling. Instead of buying on the exchange, investors can buy from **primary or participating dealers**, who purchase ETF shares from the sponsor and offer them to the market.

**Creation and redemption.** Unlike a typical unit trust that creates units for investors when they purchase, units of the STI ETF can only be created for, or redeemed by, **participating dealers** in sizes of **100,000 units**. To create an ETF share, the participating dealer deposits a specified portfolio of stocks closely approximating the index's underlying securities with the trustee. To redeem, ETF shares are delivered to the trustee in exchange for the underlying basket.

**Arbitrage.** ETFs are quoted at bid and ask prices that track but do not necessarily reflect NAV. If the listed price deviates sufficiently from NAV, **arbitrage** reduces the differential. When the traded price is **above** NAV, an arbitrageur sells ETF shares and buys the underlying portfolio. When the traded price is **below** NAV, the arbitrageur sells the underlying portfolio and buys ETF shares.

### 9.2.1 Types of ETF Structures

ETFs are either **physically** or **synthetically** structured to replicate the basket of stocks or bonds making up the index. Physically replicated ETFs hold the actual securities in index proportions. Synthetically replicated ETFs hold statistically correlated securities that together mimic the index's price behaviour, and may use swaps or derivatives.

**a. Cash or direct replication (physical ETFs).** More common. Two methods:

- **Full replication** — the ETF purchases **all** listed stocks in the exact index proportions.
- **Representative sampling** — the ETF invests in a **subset** of the index comprising the more dominant stocks. Used when index constituents are not liquid.

**b. Synthetic replication (synthetic ETFs).** Uses swaps and derivatives to mimic the index. **Advantage:** reduces the ETF's **tracking error**. **Disadvantage:** **counterparty risk**, because the derivative counterparty might default.

An example is an ETF using a **total return swap** — a contractual arrangement to swap interest or return obligations. Swap-based ETFs usually **do not pay dividends**; instead the counterparty delivers periodic payments of income and capital gains return on the reference basket, and the ETF pays a fee to the counterparty contractually obligated to deliver the index returns. There is therefore a risk to investors if the counterparty cannot fulfil its payments.

### 9.2.2 Investment Strategies with ETFs

- **Long-term buy-and-hold.**
- **Short-term tactical trading**, because of price transparency and liquidity.
- **Core-satellite allocations** — a core mimicking a broad index, with satellites of higher-risk securities offering potentially higher return.
- **Hedging** against other risks in the portfolio.
- **Cash equitisation** — converting cash to equities through ETFs to minimise the **cash drag** on a portfolio.

### 9.2.3 Securities Borrowing and Lending for ETFs

**Securities lending** is where ETFs pay agents to lend out shares in their portfolios to other investors, earning interest. For example, an ETF can transfer stocks to another investor over the lending period (say **30 days**) in exchange for collateral such as cash or other securities equivalent to, for example, **105%** of the stocks' value. If the ETF needs to sell the lent stocks, it does a reverse transaction with the borrower.

Borrowers are usually investors who have **sold the stock short** in anticipation of a fall and need to borrow for delivery, intending to buy back lower and return the stock.

If the borrower cannot fulfil its obligations, the ETF faces a loss equal to the value of the lent stocks. **Securities borrowing and lending therefore creates counterparty risk for the ETF.**

### 9.2.4 Advantages of Investing in ETFs

- **Diversification** — buying ETF shares gives exposure to a diversified portfolio.
- **Small capital outlay** — diversification with a small amount.
- **Low costs** — annual management fees normally **less than 1%**, against **1–2%** for actively managed unit trusts, with **no initial charges or redemption fees**.
- **Liquidity** — ETF shares trade at any time during exchange hours at prevailing prices; unit trusts transact only at the **daily closing NAV**.
- **Transparency** — investors always know the basket of securities in an ETF, whereas unit trusts usually disclose holdings only **quarterly, semi-annually or annually**.

### 9.2.5 Risks of Investing in Standard ETFs

Physical and synthetic ETFs differ in their risks:

| Risk | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tracking error** | **Greater in physical ETFs** than synthetic, principally because synthetic ETFs achieve more precise tracking using swaps and derivatives |
| **Counterparty risk** | Synthetic ETFs carry counterparty risk from swap and derivative arrangements. Physical ETFs can also have counterparty risk if third-party custodians have **securities lending** arrangements |
| **Market and sector risk** | When underlying securities fall, the ETF's value suffers. Sector funds may be hurt by the specific sector they track |
| **Foreign exchange risk** | ETFs with constituents traded in foreign currencies are subject to FX risk affecting overall returns |
| **Taxation** | Non-US investors holding US-established ETFs may be subject to **US withholding taxes** on dividends and other distributions |

### 9.2.6 Smart Beta ETFs

A **smart beta ETF** seeks better risk-return performance than an index of selected stocks. Selection and weighting are based on criteria relating to better-performing stocks — improvements in cash flow, dividends, revenue/earnings ratios.

In contrast to a standard ETF following market-capitalisation weighting, a smart beta ETF **selects and weights securities on positive performance criteria**. To some extent smart beta simply entails good fundamental stock selection, weighting top performers more heavily using quantitative criteria. Other smart beta ETFs exploit **market anomalies** or overlay technical measures such as **momentum and volatility**.

### 9.2.7 Volatility (VIX) Driven ETFs

A volatility index (VIX) or VIX futures ETF is meant for **very short-term trading**, not investing.

In a bullish market volatility generally falls. Traders may try to buy long volatility ETFs on days when volatility spikes, but this is difficult to predict and time consistently. Holding a long volatility position waiting for an upward spike can be **deadly** as volatility trends downward: a **1–2% fall each day** soon produces massive losses over time. High volatility generally does not last, and the beneficiaries tend to be those who **shorted volatility** — invested in the inverse of the VIX.

### 9.2.8 High Risk Leveraged ETFs and Long-Short ETF Pairs

A **leveraged ETF** uses financing and derivatives such as options and futures to **drastically amplify** potential returns from tracking an index. Such ETFs are usually leveraged **2x or 3x** the index value, and some track the **inverse** movement. Leveraged ETFs are often issued in **pairs** — long and short a particular index.

Large speculators with enough financial muscle sometimes try to drive the index one way or the other by moving key index securities, to make **2x–3x** leveraged profits on their ETF position. The practice is not exactly illegal unless there is proof of collusion to manipulate the index. Such manipulation tends to be greater for **smaller stock indices** such as the NASDAQ Biotech Index or the NYSE Arca Gold Miners Index. **In general, leveraged ETFs are not suitable for retail investors.**

Leveraged ETFs amplify the **daily** percentage price change. A **2–4% daily change** is normal, and a **±20–25%** change is possible in a single session, so there is potential for massive gains and losses.

Singapore has several inverse ETFs, such as the **Deutsche Bank x-trackers S&P 500 Inverse Daily UCITS ETF**, which traders use at specific points in the US market cycle. Its value was **USD15.99 in November 2017**, less than a quarter of its **USD79.72** at first trading. Betting against a bullish market is difficult and traders need to be nimble.

Inverse or short ETFs can be issued independently but are often introduced as part of a pair to long positions, letting traders profit or hedge when an index falls. They can offset loss-making long positions during a trading day or over a short period.

Because of sharp movements, issuers of leveraged ETFs often carry out **reverse splits** to reset prices at higher levels, so regular price resetting is common.

Some ETFs are **not meant for the average retail investor** or even institutional portfolio investment. They are intended for **speculative traders** willing to take substantial risk and volatility, with the time and emotional demeanour to manage the positions.

## 9.3 Unit Trusts

A **unit trust** is a CIS that pools funds from different investors to be invested and managed by one or more fund managers under the care of an **independent trustee**. In Singapore the Code on CIS sets out best practice on management, operation and marketing that trustees and managers are expected to observe.

| Jurisdiction | Term |
|---|---|
| **United States** | **Mutual fund** — may not be issued in the form of a trust |
| **United Kingdom** | **Unit trust** |
| **Germany** | **Investment fund** |
| **Switzerland and Luxembourg** | **Sicaf** (Société d'investissement à capital fixe) for a closed-end investment company; **Sicav** (Société d'investissement à capital variable) for an open-ended investment company |

The naming convention matters because the bulk of offshore investment funds are domiciled in **Luxembourg**.

In Singapore the unit trust is the most common form of CIS, pooling small investors' resources to invest in securities under professional management. The cash and investments are registered in the name of, and held by, the **trustee**, whose job is to safeguard investors and ensure managers operate within the **trust deed**. That document, an agreement between trustee and managers, sets out the aims and objectives, the charges for management and the basic rules of operation. For some offshore funds, redemption and purchase are handled directly by the asset manager's servicing team.

### 9.3.1 Fund Domicile

The **domicile** is where the fund is based and set up. A fund can be registered in multiple jurisdictions but can only have **one place of domicile**.

There is heavy competition between Singapore and other jurisdictions to be the fund domicile. Singapore benefits from being a fund management hub, with a business-friendly and well-respected regulatory framework, strong communication and transport infrastructure, and existing tax treaties. By launching new structures such as the **VCC**, Singapore hopes to encourage more alternative investment funds to be domiciled and managed there.

### 9.3.2 Local and Offshore Unit Trusts

Singapore allows marketing of offshore funds regardless of whether they are managed by Singapore managers, so Singapore-domiciled funds compete for investor attention with offshore funds actively marketed by foreign banks.

Under Singapore law there are three ways to offer participation in offshore funds:

- A **public offering on the local exchange** — for example foreign-domiciled ETFs with Singapore agents.
- A **private placement**.
- Invoking an exception under **Division 5A of Part IV of the Companies Act (Cap 50)**, which allows offshore funds to be marketed to **Institutional Investors, Expert Investors and Accredited Investors** in Singapore.

The presence of both offshore and locally domiciled funds benefits Singapore as a global wealth and asset management hub.

### 9.3.3 Open-End and Closed-End Unit Trusts

| Feature | Open-end | Closed-end |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Over-the-counter | Exchange traded |
| **Counterparty** | Issuer | Listed exchange or issuer |
| **Exit** | Sale or redemption through trustee | Sale in the market |
| **Issued shares/units** | **Continuous issuance** | **Fixed number of units** |
| **Price determined by** | **NAV** (fund performance net of management fees) | **Market demand and supply** as well as fund performance |

In Singapore most unit trusts are **open-end**: not listed on the exchange, offered to retail investors. The open-end feature describes the availability of **fresh units** the manager can sell to retail investors. **Closed-end** funds do not issue fresh units — the number is fixed — and are usually sold to accredited investors via private placements.

A unit trust divides a fund into equal portions called **units**, each representing the same proportion of the value of the trust's assets. If a trust's investments are worth **$10 million** and there are **5 million** units issued, each unit is worth **$2**. Most unit trusts' investments are valued **daily**, and each day the previous day's valuation prices are published in newspapers and serve as the opening dealing prices. The value of a unit trust is its **NAV** — the value of assets minus liabilities.

### 9.3.4 Role of Trustee

The managers **neither hold the investments nor issue or redeem units**. That is done by the **trustee**, who holds the trust's cash, shares and other investment assets. In an open-end trust the trustee creates new units on purchases and redeems units on liquidation, so the capitalisation is "open" with units outstanding changing **daily**.

A **prospectus** is typically issued with details of the investment objectives, focus and approach, performance and fee charges. Unit trusts are more suitable as **medium to long-term** investments. Foreign funds not incorporated in Singapore may be offered to the public if they are **recognised funds** for retail distribution.

### 9.3.5 Advantages of Investing in Unit Trusts

- **Diversification and divisibility** — pooling lets holders own fractional shares of many securities, shielding them from large losses.
- **Small capital outlay** — an initial investment of **$1,000 to $5,000** is affordable, giving access to a portfolio otherwise out of reach.
- **Professional management** — full-time analysts and portfolio managers who respond quickly to market changes.
- **Flexibility** — holders can **switch** between a "family of funds" within the same family, responding to changes in plans and markets. If the plan changes from low to moderate risk, the holder could switch from an income fund to an equity fund.
- **Liquidity** — holders can sell to the trustee, who must buy back at NAV less relevant charges. Selling shares in 30 companies individually would be more time-consuming.
- **Lower transaction costs** — trading large blocks achieves savings on brokerage and commissions.
- **Security** — assets are always legally held on investors' behalf by an **independent trustee**, not the fund manager.

### 9.3.6 Disadvantages of Investing in Unit Trusts

- **Sales charges** — relatively high charges on buying in, and high transaction costs with frequent buying and selling.
- **Management fees** — annual fees paid to the manager **regardless of performance**. Compare fees across funds, because high fees affect returns.

### 9.3.7 Major Types of Unit Trust Funds

Unit trusts are classified by the assets they invest in.

| Traditional funds — core asset classes | Diversified asset allocation strategies | Alternative investments | Others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money market | By risk profile (yield, income, balanced, growth equity) | Hedge funds | Index funds |
| Equity | By geography (global, country) | Commodity funds | Fund of funds |
| Fixed income | By industry or sector | Real estate funds and REITs | |
| | By theme | Private equity | |
| | | Quantitative and algorithm-driven | |

Fund characteristics to distinguish: OTC vs exchange-traded (open vs closed-end); passive vs actively managed; with or without derivatives; leveraged; inversed; load vs no-load; fund of funds (with or without retrocession).

**Examples in Singapore:**

- **Money market funds** hold short-term (typically under one year) fixed income instruments — bank certificates of deposit, commercial paper, Treasury bills. Because of the large denominations in direct dealing, these funds give access to competitive money market rates. Interest is earned and credited **daily**. Investors normally pay **no sales or redemption charge** but do pay a management fee.
- **Fixed income funds** invest in fixed income securities, sometimes restricted to particular types: corporate bond funds, US government bond funds, convertible bond funds, high-yield bond funds.
- **Equity funds** invest in equity securities; some specialise in particular sectors or regions.
- **Balanced funds** hold a mix of equities and bonds.
- **Asset allocation funds** shift the mix dynamically.
- **Index funds** replicate a benchmark.
- **Capital guaranteed funds** invest most capital in low-risk assets to preserve capital at maturity, with the balance in riskier assets such as options linked to equities, funds or indices, or direct equities, for upside. Maturity is determined by the expiration date of the guaranteed portion, and the guarantee usually applies **only if held to maturity**.
- **Capital preservation funds** differ from capital-guaranteed funds: they aim to provide some level of capital preservation, the language is **less onerous** for the issuer, and **no guarantor is required**.
- **Hedge funds** employ derivatives, short selling and arbitrage, focusing on **absolute returns** rather than relative performance against an index. In a **market-neutral** fund each long position is offset by an equal-value short, reducing correlation with the market. The name is a **misnomer** because hedge funds are not necessarily hedged — a manager may take directional bets and be net long or short. Managers often invest their own money and are paid a **performance fee**.
- **Quant funds** use price and market modelling based on complex mathematical algorithms. The best-known example is **Long-Term Capital Management**, whose board included **Myron S. Scholes and Robert C. Merton**, who shared the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for a "new method to determine the value of derivatives".
- **High frequency trading (HFT) funds** rely on algorithms and high-speed data transmission. Not all HFT funds are hedge funds, but many hedge funds use quantitative models and algorithms, some reporting significant profits from HFT.
- **A family of funds or umbrella fund** is a set of funds with different objectives offered under a single unit trust. Umbrella funds generally permit switching within the family at **little to no cost**, letting the investor change strategy inexpensively.

Investors in Singapore can use **CPF** funds to invest in products under the **CPF Investment Scheme (CPFIS)**. To protect CPF members, managers of CPF monies must report vital information **quarterly** to the CPF Board, and a **funds-tracking company** is appointed to publish information on performance and risks of CPFIS-included funds.

### 9.3.8 Fund Documentation — Prospectus, Product Highlights Sheets, Monthly Fund Fact Sheets, Annual Reports

With access to asset managers' websites, information on a fund is easily obtained from its **prospectus** and **product highlights sheet**. For new launches these provide details about the asset managers, the investment approach, the types of investments selected, the risk exposures, and the different types of fees and how they are calculated.

**Monthly fund fact sheets** and **annual reports** provide regular updates on performance and summary information. Summary information on the monthly fact sheet includes:

- Fund objective.
- Key information on fees.
- Performance data and ratios.
- Benchmark comparison.
- Top holdings.
- Country allocation.
- Risk statistics.
- Sector allocation.

### 9.3.9 Fund Objectives

The statement of fund objectives indicates the **target outcomes** and should also convey the **investment approach** taken to achieve them — for example a diversified portfolio across asset classes, a diversified portfolio of global equities for long-term capital growth, diversification within a single sector, or investment in a non-diversified portfolio.

### 9.3.10 Fees and Expenses

**a. Load versus no-load funds.** A **no-load fund** imposes neither an initial sales charge nor redemption charges: units are priced at NAV and the bid and offer prices are the same. A **load fund** with a **front-end load** is sold at NAV plus a sales commission. Most load funds are sold by brokers, financial planners and insurance company employees; the load often serves as commission for these distributors and is usually **1.5% to 5% of NAV**.

When holders sell, they usually receive NAV multiplied by units sold. Some funds impose a **realisation charge or redemption fee** — a **back-end load** — which may be waived if units have been held beyond a stipulated period (for example **60 days**). Its purpose is to **discourage selling right after buying**, avoiding the transaction costs of frequent securities sales to meet redemptions.

**b. Management fee.** All unit trusts charge annual management fees, typically **0.5% to 2.0% of NAV**, accrued **daily** and debited directly from the trust's asset value.

**c. Various operational costs.** Under an umbrella structure a **switching fee of about 1%** may apply when switching between sub-funds, so frequent switching is expensive. Other charges include annual **trustee, custodian and audit fees**, accrued daily, monthly or as incurred and deducted from assets — usually **0.1% to 0.15% of NAV**.

There are also set-up costs including legal and marketing, charged once or amortised over years. Beyond the front-end load, investment companies may charge an annual **distribution fee** paying for advertising, promotion and selling, charged directly against NAV. Some costs appear "waived" but end up as expenses charged on the portfolio.

**d. Performance fee.** Some funds pay an additional incentive charge for substantially exceeding benchmark targets. Measures ensure the manager does not unfairly benefit from a fund recovering after a sharp fall — a **high watermark** is set so the manager must generate a certain level of profit before the bonus can be taken from NAV.

The **total expense ratio** measures total expenses (including management fees) against NAV. It is often reviewed when comparing funds to assess a manager's **operational efficiency**.

### 9.3.11 Performances and Benchmarks

Past performance must be considered when selecting a unit trust. While "past performance is no guarantee of future performance", it remains a useful guide. A unit trust is usually managed **relative to a benchmark**, so the investor can compare the fund's return against it: if the fund's return is higher than the benchmark, the manager has done well.

Performance is typically charted against the benchmark **since inception**, with a table showing performance over the last **3 or 5 years**. Check whether returns are shown **net of management fees** — most fact sheets indicate net returns.

Where a fund has **dual currency tranches**, returns may also be reflected in the alternate currency: a USD return may differ substantially when reflected in SGD or EUR because of exchange rate movements.

For traditional funds with a **relative return** target, the benchmark is usually an index; a few use a weighted combination of indices from two or more asset classes. For funds with **absolute return** targets, an interbank rate such as **LIBOR + 1%** might be the benchmark to beat.

### 9.3.12 Net Asset Value

For funds sold OTC, the fund's value is the **NAV**:

$$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Fund securities} + \text{Other fund assets} - \text{Liabilities (including management fees)} - \text{Debt used for leveraging}}{\text{Total fund units outstanding}}$$

For open-end funds the outstanding units change with issuances and redemptions. NAV rises and falls with the value of investments and should be reflected **net of management fees**.

### 9.3.13 Bid-Offer Price

There is a difference between NAV and the price at which an investor can buy and sell. The principle is that a holder buying new units should pay a sum sufficient to purchase a proportionate amount of the underlying securities, **including the expenses necessary for that purchase**. Existing holders should not unfairly bear costs from a transaction they did not create.

To achieve equity between incoming and outgoing holders, valuations show two prices.

**Offer price** — the price at which an investor can **buy** units, representing the cost of creating new units equal in value to existing units based on market prices at the latest valuation:

- Determine NAV per unit by assessing the market value of the trust's securities.
- **Add** expenses related to purchasing securities, such as stockbrokers' commission and clearing fees.
- **Add** cash held by the trust and accrued income.
- **Add** the manager's initial charge.

**Bid price** — the price at which an investor can **sell** units, equivalent to NAV per unit if all securities were sold and proceeds distributed in cash:

- Determine NAV per unit by assessing the market value of the trust's securities.
- **Deduct** expenses related to the sale of securities.
- **Add** cash held by the trust and accrued income.

### 9.3.14 Top Holdings and Concentration Risk

Many funds do not allow a single security to exceed **5%** of total assets, but some managers allow exposure to rise to **8–9%** in an attempt to outperform. Top-holding choices significantly affect returns, concentration risk and volatility. Under- or over-weighting securities that dominate benchmark indices — Tencent, Alibaba, Apple in technology — can materially affect performance.

### 9.3.15 Allocation by Asset Class, Market and Sectors

The fund fact sheet shows how the fund is invested by asset class (for multi-asset funds), by market and by sector. Reviewing allocation shows how much exposure is favoured in one country or sector over others, which some investors prefer.

### 9.3.16 Risk-Adjusted Return and Other Risk Ratios

Beyond benchmarked returns, review risk-adjusted returns and risk using:

- **Sharpe ratio.**
- **Treynor ratio.**
- **Beta.**
- **Jensen's alpha.**
- **Information ratio.**
- **Sortino ratio** — for alternative investment funds with asymmetrical return distributions.

Generally, investors should pick funds generating **higher returns above the risk-free rate per unit of risk taken**.

**a. Investment objectives and time horizon.** Knowing the investor's objectives and horizon is essential. Generally unit trusts suit investors with **medium to long-term** horizons, the exception being certain **leveraged ETFs and highly volatile commodity ETFs**, which are meant more for traders.

The risk continuum runs from **money market funds** (least risky) to **specialised funds** (riskiest).

To meet best practice, some fund houses provide statements highlighting the type of client the product suits.

**Product suitability statements — who is the product suitable for?**

*Fund A: Conservative Fund.* Only suitable for investors who:

- Seek long-term capital growth.
- Seek regular income distribution in respect of the Distribution Class.
- Are looking for exposure to companies listed on SGX-ST and are comfortable with the volatility of a **single-country equity fund**.

*Fund B: Aggressive Fund.* Only suitable for investors who:

- Seek capital appreciation with **no need for income**.
- Seek access to dynamic emerging market countries.
- Are comfortable that the portfolio may generate potentially higher returns but comes with a **higher risk profile** than a diversified Global Equity Fund.

**b. Diversification.** A common motivation is a diversified portfolio through the fund's securities. Depending on objectives, some funds are narrowly focused: a unit trust investing in China-related large-cap companies has **less diversification** than one investing in global large-caps.

**c. Expenses.** The **expense ratio** indicates the cost and efficiency of the fund.

**d. Fund size and capacity.** A small fund can be hurt because fixed expenses have a material impact on performance. A **SGD10,000** legal expense billed to an **SGD8 million** fund is **0.125%** of the fund; billed to an **SGD1 million** fund it is **1%**.

Conversely, very large unit trusts are difficult to manage. A fund with **SGD10 billion** is huge relative to the market; the manager cannot be as nimble and may struggle to add value. When a unit trust reaches its **optimal capacity**, it typically **stops accepting fresh subscriptions**.

### 9.3.18 Retrocessions

**Retrocessions** are fees received by banks and other financial institutions for distributing third-party products to clients. When fund recommendations are made to new clients there can be a **conflict of interest**, compromising the independence of the adviser, and clients are often not fully aware of the practice. Retrocession fees paid by fund houses to banks are a **cost offset against fund performance**, negatively affecting NAV for existing clients.

While the practice has been **arrested by the courts in Switzerland**, it is still widely practised in Asia by some institutions.

Similar concerns apply to **funds of funds** selecting external funds: if the fund of fund house receives retrocessions, the objectivity of the selection process is potentially diminished. Investors also worry about the **dual layer of fees** — at fund level and again at fund-of-fund level. Retrocessions may result in significant overall effective costs, though some investors are less concerned if the overall return compensates.

## 9.4 Real Estate Investment Trusts

### 9.4.1 Overview of REITs

A **REIT** is a CIS pooling individual investors' funds to invest in real estate assets. In Singapore, REITs are governed by **Appendix 6 — Property Funds (the Property Funds Appendix)** in the Code on CIS. A REIT is a unit trust predominantly invested in real estate and real estate-related assets: the Code requires a minimum of **at least 75% of the fund** to be invested in **income-producing real estate**. A simple way to understand a REIT is as a **passive investment vehicle** engaged in real estate investment for rent collection.

In many respects a REIT is like any other unit trust, but three differences matter:

- A REIT requires a **wider range of specialists** to manage it.
- A REIT typically has a **Sponsor**, usually with a stake, that sources the properties injected into the initial portfolio and may continue to provide a **pipeline** of assets.
- **Valuations** of underlying assets are less current: unlike equities and bonds where market values are readily available, the Code on CIS requires REIT properties to be valued only **once a year**.

Unit trusts that are not REITs are typically **not listed**, whereas REITs are listed on the **SGX Mainboard**. Unlisted property trusts, while constituted the same way as listed REITs, are not publicly traded.

REITs can be **sector-specific** (retail malls) or hold investments across property sectors — healthcare, hospitality, industrial, offices, retail. REITs are **long-term investments** subject to property cycle fluctuations.

### 9.4.2 Advantages of REITs

Compared with direct investment in real estate for rental income:

- Buying into a REIT is much more **affordable** and achieves **greater diversification** than purchasing a single property.
- A REIT has significantly greater **liquidity**, trading easily and quickly on the exchange without substantial transaction costs.
- REITs distributing at least **90% of taxable income** each year enjoy **favourable tax treatment**, and many do; there is **no corresponding tax advantage** for direct investment.
- REITs come with **professional property management** without separately contracting for it.

### 9.4.3 Risks of Investing in REITs

Risks vary with the REIT's characteristics, including the geographical location of properties. Investors should consider not only the expected distribution yield but also the **quality and lease tenor** of underlying properties, **tenant quality and concentration**, and the **weighted-average lease expiry**.

| Risk | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Market risk** | REITs are listed and traded, so price depends on supply and demand. Prices reflect the outlook for specific sectors (healthcare, hospitality, industrial, office, retail), the property market, the economic outlook, management, interest rates and other factors. Investors must bear this volatility |
| **Income risk** | REITs typically distribute at least **90% of income** each year, but may be unable to distribute if they incur an operating loss — from lease expiries, lower occupancy, falling rentals or tenant contractual failure. Evaluate whether the REIT has mitigated these through lease tenor, tenant quality and tenant type |
| **Concentration risk** | REITs afford some diversification but may be vulnerable if a substantial portion of income comes from a few property assets or assets in the same industry (hospitality). Dependence on a small number of tenants also creates concentration risk |
| **Liquidity risk** | Although REIT units trade on the exchange, the **underlying properties** may not be easily liquidated because the physical real estate market is private, with bilateral dealing. REITs therefore cannot dispose of or alter investments quickly in a falling property market |
| **Leverage risk** | In Singapore REITs are subject to a **single-tier leverage limit of 45%**. Servicing acquisition debt means less income for distribution and greater volatility in equity value |
| **Refinancing risk** | Debt financing exposes the REIT to interest rate increases on refinancing. Because REITs distribute at least **90% of income** annually, they cannot easily build cash reserves for debt repayment, so they typically resort to fresh borrowing (medium term notes) or new equity (rights issue). If unable, they may be forced to liquidate investments, reducing lease income. Lacking cash reserves, REITs usually must raise capital for additional capital expenditure |

## 9.5 Business Trusts

### 9.5.1 Overview of Business Trusts

Business trusts (BTs) are governed by the **Business Trusts Act 2004**, passed in September 2004 to regulate this form of business structure. Like unit trusts, a BT is established under a **trust deed**. A BT is a **hybrid** with characteristics of both companies and trusts, but unlike a company it is **not a separate legal entity**. BTs have become a popular form of SGX listing, with participation from port infrastructure and pay television.

In Singapore, BTs are a **single responsible entity** organisation: the **trustee-manager** is vested with the roles of both trustee and manager of the trust assets. The trustee-manager has **legal ownership** of the BT's assets and is responsible for looking after unit holders' interests and managing the business. Investors subscribe for units and are paid distributions from the BT's income.

### 9.5.2 Advantages of Business Trusts

- **Limited liability** of unit holders.
- **Distributions can be paid out of operating cash flow** instead of accounting profits.
- **Investment restrictions applicable to CIS do not apply** to BTs.

The main attraction is effectiveness in **unlocking value from fixed assets that generate cash flow**. Infrastructure providers and asset-backed businesses find the structure attractive: such businesses usually have **depreciating assets that reduce accounting profits despite strong positive cash flows**, so paying distributions from operating cash flow is valuable.

Like REITs, BTs pool investors' money and give fractional ownership of underlying business assets, facilitating diversification. But unlike REITs, **BTs can actively engage in any business area**, whereas REITs are required by law to invest in real estate for rental income.

### 9.5.3 Risks in Business Trusts

BTs are subject to the same risks as unit trusts and REITs — market, liquidity, income, concentration and refinancing risk. On refinancing, note that while there is **no cap on the debt limit**, debt can still become a problem if the BT uses that freedom for **indiscriminate borrowing**, producing an unsustainable debt structure.

Given the wide range of businesses allowed, investors should analyse the **contractual details of the BT's assets** to determine the consistency and stability of income sources. For example, existing port infrastructure providers on traditional shipping routes are potentially at risk as global warming opens new, more efficient and economical routes, creating demand for new port locations.

### 9.5.4 Comparison between Listed REITs and Business Trusts

| | Listed REIT | Business Trust |
|---|---|---|
| **Entity** | Constituted by a trust deed | Constituted by a trust deed |
| **Ownership** | Unit holders do not have individual legal ownership of assets but have **beneficial interests** | Unit holders do not have individual legal ownership of assets but have **beneficial interests** |
| **Independence of trustee and manager** | Trustee and manager are **separate and independent** | **Single responsible entity** is the trustee-manager, who manages the trust assets |
| **Trust objective** | **Passive** investments | Permitted to **actively engage** in business operation |
| **Distribution** | **Mandatory distribution of a minimum of 90% of annual income** to qualify for favourable tax treatment | **No mandatory minimum.** Can distribute from operating cash flow |
| **Gearing** | **45% limit** | **No limit** |
| **Restrictions on investments** | Minimum **90%** to be generated by **rental income** | **None** |
| **Regulations** | Securities and Futures Act, Code on CIS | Business Trusts Act |
| **Taxation** | **Tax exempt** for individual unit holders | **Single-tier taxation** |

## 9.6 Stapled Securities

A **stapled security** is created when an issuer lists **two or more securities traded as a single instrument** on the exchange. The stapled security shares the same investor. For example, several Singapore REITs in the hospitality sector are stapled to a business trust for listing.

Stapled securities are popular with issuers because they combine a **passive income security** with an **active income security**, enhancing the issue's attractiveness. From the investor's perspective they provide some **diversification of business risks**.

In a stapled security the issuer is bound by the regulations specific to **each** security being stapled. If a REIT and a business trust are stapled, the issuer must pay the minimum **90% of annual income** to REIT unit holders to qualify for favourable tax treatment, but is **free from paying dividends** on the business trust. Such stapled securities **cannot be marketed as REITs**.

## 9.7 Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities

**UCITS** is a **European Union directive**, applicable in the UK since **2002**. The original objectives were to remove barriers to cross-border distribution and marketing of funds by allowing funds to invest in a wider range of financial instruments. UCITS provides the **"passport"** for funds to be sold internationally.

UCITS funds are attractive to retail investors globally because of **common standards of disclosure** and **consistent investment guidelines**. Under **UCITS III**, funds can invest in a wide range of assets beyond equities and bonds — for example using derivatives and index tracking products to limit risk or increase returns.

Since **2006**, MAS has allowed UCITS funds to be distributed and sold in Singapore if they meet local regulatory conditions. The number registered for sale in Singapore has increased significantly over the last 5–10 years, reflecting the trend in Asia.

## 9.8 Alternative Investments

Beyond REITs and business trusts, alternative investments include:

- **Hedge funds.**
- **Private equity funds.**
- **Commodity funds.**
- **Real estate funds.**

The main benefit is the ability to **further diversify** using asset classes **less correlated** with traditional equities and fixed income, potentially increasing risk-adjusted returns.

Unfortunately, during major financial crises such as 2008, alternative investments do **not necessarily provide much of a buffer**, because they are also affected by a widespread massive sell-off. Under normal conditions, however, they have been found to be less correlated with equities and bonds.

### 9.8.1 Overview of Hedge Funds

Unlike traditional funds, hedge funds can take **short positions** using derivatives. Depending on the mandate, they adopt either:

- A **single strategy** approach, following one approach.
- A **multi-strategy** approach, emphasising one approach over others given the opportunities in a particular period.

### 9.8.2 Hedge Funds versus Conventional Funds

Like conventional funds, hedge funds can take long positions to exploit positive market movements and are exposed to downturns. Unlike conventional funds, they can use **short selling or derivatives** to reduce or hedge their portfolios' **beta sensitivity** to the market.

Hedge funds often have **absolute return targets** and can rely on **leverage** to significantly augment returns and produce alpha. But some strategies require a **longer time to unwind**, and hedge funds can be **less transparent** about pricing and positions. Because unwinding takes time, the manager only knows the actual price when trades execute — hence the lack of pricing transparency.

Hedge fund managers often receive **incentive fees** on top of higher management fees.

| Hedge funds | Conventional funds |
|---|---|
| Capture **alpha** (freedom to hedge away or reduce beta) | Capture **alpha + beta** (limited ability to reduce beta risk) |
| Performance depends mostly on the manager's skills and strategies | Performance depends mostly on market factors and manager skill |
| **Absolute return** targets | **Index-based benchmark** targets |
| Can be **leveraged significantly** to augment the impact of strategy | **Limited** ability to use debt and leverage |
| Require **longer to unwind** and are **less transparent** on pricing | More transparent on investments and pricing |
| Management fees **and performance incentives** | Management fees |

### 9.8.3 Risks of Investing in Hedge Funds

- **Risky investment strategies** — speculative strategies combined with leverage can produce outsized losses. Leveraged positions are sometimes hedged with derivatives, bringing **basis risk** (the risk remaining from an imperfect hedge because a complete offsetting position may not exist) and **counterparty risk** (the other party failing to meet its contractual obligation).
- **Illiquidity** — hedge funds can invest in highly illiquid assets and may not unwind at fair prices. An unanticipated downturn can produce **margin calls** on leveraged positions and unusually large redemptions, forcing liquidation at unfavourable prices. Many hedge funds impose **lock-up periods** during which investors cannot redeem, and some have **gate provisions** letting the manager limit withdrawals on a given date to a percentage of net assets.
- **Poorer investor protection** — hedge funds are more lightly regulated and less transparent than conventional funds.

### 9.8.4 Hedge Funds — Relative Value Arbitrage Strategies

**a. Long-short equity.** A relative value approach: the manager goes long on a stock believed undervalued and short on a stock believed overvalued within the same sector. For example, going long TSMC and short Intel on the view that TSMC's premium over Intel will widen.

For such strategies it is useful to set a target for closing the positions. Positions can be rolled over, but there must be justification for further widening. If there is no additional news that would cause the premium to widen further, the spread position should be closed.

If the manager has strong conviction, they can take a **net long** position by investing more in the long leg than is short-sold — but then the strategy is **no longer market-neutral** and has partial exposure to a sector decline.

**b. Convertible arbitrage.** Also a relative value approach, but the analysis is between the **convertible securities and the common stock of the same company**. The strategy purchases convertibles while shorting the underlying stock. The rationale is that convertibles are usually **slower to reprice to fair value** than the underlying stock, creating arbitrage opportunities. The strategy is not riskless: the stock price can rise while the convertible falls.

Other, less widely used relative value strategies include **fixed income arbitrage** (exploiting yield differences between bond types), **credit long-short** and **volatility arbitrage**.

### 9.8.5 Hedge Funds — Event-Driven Strategies

Event-driven strategies emerge when specific events present themselves, and hedge funds opportunistically exploit them. Events include **financial distress** (bankruptcies, liquidation), **acquisitions**, **capital restructuring** and **consolidation**.

**a. Distressed securities.** Some hedge funds — sometimes called **vulture funds** — look selectively for opportunities in bonds priced at distressed levels. Depending on the severity of the company's crisis, distressed bonds can be priced at **10% to 99% of par**. Hedge funds buy distressed debt and borrowings they believe undervalued.

When there was a global downturn in oil and gas, the bonds of many oil-related companies, including several in Singapore, were priced at distressed levels. Some investors and banks were willing to sell at a discount, and these were bought by investors and hedge funds willing to bear the risk for potential gains.

Hedge funds typically have three options:

- **Resell** the debt at a much higher price when the credit situation improves.
- **Exchange** debt for equity and sell the equity for a profit, or combine the stake with a private equity firm and other investors to make a takeover bid.
- **Allow time** for the company to recover and pay off the full principal at maturity, yielding substantial gains above the discounted price.

Investing in distressed debt is very risky and should be managed by experts. A well-known **Swiss bank** was forced to sell its billion-dollar portfolio to a distressed-debt specialist at a huge loss in **2016** when it decided to exit the high-risk business.

**b. Corporate events arbitrage.** Event-driven funds also look for mergers, acquisitions, takeovers and liquidations. Depending on valuation and the deal process, they may:

- Buy the **target** and sell the **acquirer**, when the acquirer is overvalued.
- Buy **both** acquirer and target, when the acquisition is believed to create greater value for the merged entity through synergies.
- Buy shares of the **target** in a hostile takeover with competing offers.

Not every corporate finance deal offers mispricing opportunities, so such funds may face **dry periods**.

### 9.8.6 Hedge Funds — Global Macro Strategies

Global macro strategies take **directional views** on financial products and derivatives in anticipation of market reactions to significant economic or political changes in one or more countries. The manager decides to go long or short based on how macroeconomic trends will affect interest rates, currencies, sovereign credit, commodities, equity indices, equities and bonds.

Approaches range from very **qualitative fundamental** to **quantitative computer-based models**, or a blend. Because of the size of their positions and risks, they need to invest in fairly **liquid** assets to unwind or hedge.

**Examples.** Shorting the pound sterling and British stocks on the London Exchange during the **Brexit Referendum** was an effective global macro strategy. Conversely, a strategy that shorted US equities expecting a decline after the Republican victory in the US elections would have had to reverse quickly to avoid losses when US equities rallied shortly after. Global macro funds must be **nimble** to unwind positions and cut losses.

### 9.8.7 Hedge Funds — Directional Computer-Based Quantitative Trading Strategies

Directional quantitative strategies combine **technical analysis, market data and funds flow** to make decisions, mainly following technical price trends and momentum. As long as the trend is intact, the fund maintains its position until a reversal is observed.

**Momentum trading.** A fund following momentum continues to buy options and futures on indices in line with upward trend momentum — the philosophy that "the trend is your friend". Once the model signals a slowdown or reversal, cumulative gains are realised at once and the model awaits confirmation of the next trend. If indicators signal further correction, shorting of futures and options begins until the next reversal point, at which shorts are closed with purchases.

Such strategies benefit from **clear and persistent trends over weeks or months**. With disruptive reversals or a high frequency of reversals over very short periods, transaction costs are likely too high to make profits.

### 9.8.8 Funds of Hedge Funds — Multi-Strategies

Funds of hedge funds adopt different strategies, allocating more to some over others in a given period, because in any period some strategies outperform others due to market conditions.

The fund of hedge funds approach attempts to provide the expertise and due diligence to ensure:

- **Adequate diversification** between hedge funds, reducing concentration risk.
- That monies can be **actively reallocated** to strategies that do well under certain market conditions.

The service comes with **additional management cost**, on top of the performance and incentive fees paid to hedge fund managers. With diversification, returns typically fall to the **average** hedge fund return, which may not fit some investors' expectations.

### 9.8.9 Overview of Private Equity Funds

Private equity funds are **long-term investments sold OTC**, mainly to accredited individuals, corporate clients, institutional clients and sovereign wealth funds to complement their overall portfolio. They invest in businesses at different stages of their growth cycles.

Private equity provides businesses with capital to grow to a particular size before being **listed through an IPO** or **sold privately** to major corporations. It therefore gives founders a viable **exit option** and investors the opportunity to realise gains.

### 9.8.10 Stages of Private Equity Investment

**a. Venture capital.** Venture capitalists co-invest with an entrepreneur to provide **seed and/or start-up capital** in the early stages. Many newly founded tech and biotech companies are at the conceptual or research stage where products are not yet developed, and may go through several years of R&D and losses before potential revenues and profits. In return for the higher risk, venture capitalists expect a **higher-than-average return**.

**b. Growth capital.** Funds for companies looking to **scale up** and take advantage of high growth or expansion. Growth capital tends to focus on **later-stage** investments — companies already established but still requiring funds or co-sharing of expansion risk when scaling product range, production volume, distribution and market reach.

**c. Buyout funds.** Invest in **mature companies with stable cash flows**, established products and developed personnel. The buyout may occur **pre-IPO** for owners wanting to exit before the IPO process, or as part of the IPO process with an agreement to transfer shares between major shareholders **1–2 years after** the IPO to facilitate smooth running.

**d. Special situations.**

- **Going private** — when major shareholders believe the market undervalues the company. Buying the company and taking it private lets them benefit from a larger share of profits. Private equity vehicles can act as intermediary, or major shareholders can raise private capital among friendly parties to make an offer to remaining shareholders. When the time is ripe the business can be re-listed or consolidated with other businesses for listing.
- **Distressed businesses** — private equity firms work with private parties to take over companies facing financial distress. In downturns, good businesses may succumb to financing and liquidity issues and be forced to sell; private equity can work with creditor banks to take them over.

### 9.8.11 The Private Equity J-Curve

Private equity investments tend to have **negative initial returns and cash flows** for a relatively long period, so cash flows follow a **J-curve**.

The first **5–6 years** are the **investment period**. If an investor commits **$10 million**, it is not all invested at the outset: the manager makes several **cash calls** and draws down the capital as good opportunities arise. If many good opportunities arise early, the drawdown is faster. The **harvest period** typically begins from the **6th year**, when the fund starts making payouts.

The incentive is that the expected **internal rate of return** is typically in the double digits (**10–15%**). The downside is that funds are **locked up for several years**, and early redemption or withdrawal before the 6th year typically results in **losses**. To address suitability, it must be highlighted that private equity is **long-term** in nature: the investor must be willing to sacrifice liquidity to secure the targeted returns, the first few years involve only drawdowns, returns usually accrue only from the 6th year, and early redemptions result in losses.

### 9.8.12 Commodity Funds and Real Estate Funds

Commodity and real estate funds are largely similar to commodity ETFs and REITs. The main difference is that commodity and real estate funds are sold **OTC**, while commodity ETFs and REITs are **exchange-traded**. Some OTC funds distinguish themselves by being **more transparent** about their investment approach and types of investments.

**a. Commodity funds.** Offer indirect exposure to a portfolio of commodity-related stocks, bonds, futures and funds. They can focus on pure sectors (metals, agriculture, energy) or be a fund of funds diversified across commodity types, possibly overweighting one sector. The more diversified the fund, the less volatile it should be, though exposure to the futures market makes some volatility unavoidable.

- **Agriculture** funds suffer the same problem as their underlying commodities: successful production often outstrips demand, causing long downward cycles of overhanging supply.
- **Energy, base metal and ferrous metal** sectors are more **cyclical**: they do well when global economic activity picks up, driving underlying demand, and poorly when activity slows.
- **Precious metals** funds typically do well in times of uncertainty and volatility in traditional asset classes, and can be heavily influenced by major currency movements and interest rates.

| Agriculture funds | Energy funds | Metals funds |
|---|---|---|
| Grains | Crude oil | Precious metals |
| Soft commodities | Natural gas | Base metals |
| Livestock | Refined products | Ferrous metals |
| Dairy | Petrochemicals | |
| Biofuel | Electricity | |

**b. Real estate funds.** Not too different from REITs. OTC real estate funds can cover agricultural, industrial, residential, commercial, medical and even undeveloped land banks. Although focused on generating stable, high rental income, OTC real estate funds sold through private banks can be **less liquid**.

As with REITs, the main concern is the **mismatch between unit liquidity and the illiquidity of underlying assets**. In a property bubble, asset prices can fall very sharply once the bubble bursts, leading to redemptions. When redemptions mount, funds without deep pockets that struggle to unwind property holdings may be caught in a **liquidity crunch** and be unable to meet redemptions. Selling underlying property at market lows forces managers to realise losses, so in such situations real estate funds typically seek to **delay redemptions**.

## 9.9 Insurance

Some events are unpredictable as to when they occur but have severe financial repercussions when they do. Risk management processes must mitigate the effects of such unforeseen events.

Some insurance products also serve as investment instruments, and representatives should understand them to advise clients well. The two main types of life insurance policies that can also serve as savings or investment instruments are **whole life** and **endowment** insurance.

### 9.9.1 Whole Life Insurance Policy

Premiums for a whole life policy provide a combination of **life cover and investment**, and therefore have **cash or surrender values**. The **sum assured** is payable on the death of the life insured, whenever that occurs. Policy owners pay fixed monthly or annual premiums and beneficiaries receive fixed death benefits. Meanwhile, reserves and assets backing future liabilities earn interest and grow, building **cash values** that can be withdrawn through **surrender** (cancellation) of the policy, subject to its terms.

Alternatively, if owners do not want to surrender, they have access to accumulated policy savings: they can normally obtain **policy loans** from the insurer up to a certain percentage of the policy's cash value, the limit being insurer- and policy-specific.

### 9.9.2 Endowment Insurance Policy

Premiums also provide a combination of life cover and investment, but endowment policies usually **mature after a fixed period** — for example **10, 15 or 20 years** — and the sum assured is payable on the **maturity date or on death, whichever is earlier**. As with whole life, the sum assured is fixed.

Endowment policies carry **higher premiums** than conventional whole life and term insurance, but may be useful for special lump-sum needs such as college expenses or buying a retirement home. The **guaranteed cash values received may be less than the sum of premiums paid**, because part of the premium pays for insurance protection while the rest is invested and subject to investment risk.

### 9.9.3 Using Insurance Policies for Investments

Both whole life and endowment policies are effectively investments payable at a future date, either on death or earlier. Policies structured to provide a fixed death benefit only are **non-profit (non-participating)** policies, in contrast to **with-profits** policies.

The return of insurance policies can be linked to the insurer's investment performance either through a **with-profits** policy, where benefits are **indirectly** affected, or through an **investment-linked** policy, which has a **direct** link.

**a. With-profits policies.** Each year the insurer values the assets and liabilities of its life fund. This normally reveals a **surplus**, part of which can be allocated to the with-profits policy owner as an addition to the sum assured. This addition — a **bonus** or profit — is usually **reversionary**, meaning it is payable only at the same time as the sum assured.

Under a with-profits policy the link between benefits and the insurer's investment and operating performance (mortality experience, operating expenses) is **not direct** and depends on the annual valuation of fund assets and liabilities, where many other factors are considered.

Allocation of the surplus as a reversionary bonus ultimately depends on the **board of directors**, based in part on the appointed actuary's recommendations. Bonuses are therefore only **loosely linked** to investment and operating performance. Allowances must be made for guarantees on the basic sum assured, so the bonus system does not directly reflect the value of the underlying assets. Bonuses are also declared **yearly**, so they cannot match daily fluctuations in asset values.

**b. Investment-linked life insurance policies.** Offer policies whose values are **directly linked** to the investment performance of the underlying instruments, usually by formally linking policy values to **units in a fund** run by the insurer or by external fund managers. Such funds usually consist of a mixture of equity, fixed income, managed and other funds. The investment element varies with the underlying assets and **fluctuates daily** with their performance.

## 9.10 Annuities

An **annuity** is a series of payments guaranteed for a number of years or over the lifetime of the party receiving the payments (the **annuitant**).

### 9.10.1 Types of Annuities

- **Immediate annuity** — payments begin **as soon as it is purchased**. Always purchased with a **lump sum (single premium)**.
- **Deferred annuity** — payments begin at some **future date**, specified in the contract or at the annuitant's option. The amount received depends on contributions, the interest earned, the annuitant's **gender** and the annuitant's **age** when payments begin. May be purchased with a single premium or periodic premiums.

Examples of **non-insurance annuities** include monthly payments on home mortgages and fixed-rate coupon payments on a bond.

### 9.10.2 Annuity Calculations

**Present value of an annuity:**

$$PV_{\text{annuity}} = C \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i} \right]$$

where $$C$$ is the cash flow per period, $$i$$ the interest rate and $$n$$ the number of payments.

**Worked example.** **$1,000** paid every year for **5 years**, at an annual discount rate of **3%**:

$$PV = 1{,}000 \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.03)^{-5}}{0.03} \right] = \$4{,}579.71$$

Calculator: **PMT = −1,000**, **N = 5**, **I/Y = 3**; compute PV.

**Future value of an annuity:**

$$FV_{\text{annuity}} = C \times \left[ \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i} \right]$$

**Worked example.** **$1,000** paid every year for **8 years**, at **5%**:

$$FV = 1{,}000 \times \left[ \frac{(1.05)^8 - 1}{0.05} \right] = \$9{,}549.11$$

Calculator: **PMT = −1,000**, **N = 8**, **I/Y = 5**; compute FV.

### 9.10.3 Uses of Annuities

**a. Insurance against outliving one's income.** In its pure form a **life annuity** is a contract whereby, for a cash consideration, the insurer agrees to pay the annuitant a stipulated sum periodically **throughout life**. The consideration is fully earned by the insurer immediately on the annuitant's death. The purpose is to protect against **loss of income from excessive longevity** — exactly the opposite of life insurance, which protects against loss of income from **premature death**.

**b. Investment vehicle or savings instrument.** Most annuities are savings instruments designed to **accumulate funds and then systematically liquidate** them, usually during retirement. The annuitant can pay a lump sum or regular premiums; at the annuity starting date they receive a guaranteed income for life, the amount depending on the cash value and the payment option selected.

Annuities are attractive for someone who has not yet accumulated an estate but wants financial independence in old age. **Professional entertainers and athletes**, who enjoy very large income for a limited period, find annuities particularly attractive as a savings medium, purchasable through flexible periodic premiums or a single premium when large sums arrive.

**c. Hedge against adverse financial developments.** Wealthy individuals who have accumulated an estate purchase annuities as a hedge against adverse financial developments. Large estates can be wrecked through unwise investments, and individuals once wealthy can depend on annuity payments purchased when they were more affluent.

**d. Comparing life insurance and annuities.** Life insurance guards against living **too short** a life; life annuities guard against living **longer than expected**, supporting post-retirement living expenses for as long as the annuitant lives. To provide sufficient retirement income, life annuity products are often structured as **savings and investment products before payments begin**. The savings and asset-building phase is crucial to retirement planning and an integral part of many life annuity products, where regular premiums are paid into the retirement savings fund for investment and growth.

## 9.11 Risk Management for Retirement Funds

For investment of **CPF** funds ultimately meant for retirement, the CPF Board has provided guidance aligned with risk management best practice:

- Investors should be made aware that funds with **higher equity proportions** are generally riskier and more volatile.
- Investment providers should highlight **focused or concentration risk** — geographical, sector or specific investment-related.
- For a fund to be included under **CPFIS** and eligible to take CPF monies, it must meet the **CPFIS funds admission criteria**, including being in the **top quartile of the global peer group** and benchmarking **total expense ratios to the median of CPFIS funds** in the respective risk class.

**Diversification and dollar-cost averaging** are recommended to spread risk and potentially improve overall returns.

### 9.11.1 Dollar Cost Averaging Versus Market Timing

**a. Market timing.** The technique of shifting investments in and out of risky asset classes to:

- Capture the upside for risky asset classes during bull markets.
- Exit before downturns, avoiding the downside during bear markets.

Market timing is an **active** investment approach.

**b. Dollar cost averaging.** A practice for investors who do not want to undertake market-timing risk: invest **steady, equal amounts over a period of time**. Because equal dollar amounts are invested, **fewer shares are accumulated when the price is high and more when the price is low**. Executing it accurately will likely leave the investor with **odd lots**.

**Worked example.** An investor with **$60,000** is considering a security currently priced at **$5**.

- Invest all **$60,000** at the start: **60,000 / 5.00 = 12,000 units**.
- Buy **$5,000** worth evenly over the next 12 months.

| Month | Price | Quantity | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5.00 | 1,000 | 5,000.00 |
| 2 | 4.80 | 1,042 | 5,000.00 |
| 3 | 4.60 | 1,087 | 5,000.00 |
| 4 | 4.60 | 1,087 | 5,000.00 |
| 5 | 4.70 | 1,064 | 5,000.00 |
| 6 | 4.50 | 1,111 | 5,000.00 |
| 7 | 4.50 | 1,111 | 5,000.00 |
| 8 | 4.50 | 1,111 | 5,000.00 |
| 9 | 4.30 | 1,163 | 5,000.00 |
| 10 | 4.30 | 1,163 | 5,000.00 |
| 11 | 5.00 | 1,000 | 5,000.00 |
| 12 | 4.80 | 1,042 | 5,000.00 |
| **Total** | **4.622** | **12,980** | **60,000.00** |

Investing all at the start gives an average price of **$60,000 / 12,000 = $5**. Dollar cost averaging gives **$60,000 / 12,980 = $4.622**. This is also always mathematically lower than the simple average of the twelve monthly prices (**$4.633**), but that does not mean much: what matters is whether the average cost is below the **final** price at the end of the 12 months, which depends on the price trend.

**c. Missing the best days.** Advocates of passive investing cite empirical evidence that most investors fail to use market timing to make a positive impact sustainably. Not only are the odds against them, the "punishments" are devastating if the best trading days are missed.

Consider the best **25 days** of the S&P from **1970 to 2016** (47 years). An investor who started at 18 and retired 47 years later in 2016, but **missed the best 25 days**, would see the return fall from **1,910% to just 371%** — from **6.7% to 3.0% annually**. That is even lower than the return on a **1-month US T-bill** rolled over regularly, which would generate an average yield of **4.9%** over the period. Passive buy-and-hold would have done much better.

Two further considerations:

- The best trading days usually occur **immediately after some of the worst** trading days, so the chance of missing the best days is high because many investors sell in panic.
- Best trading days tend to occur in **close proximity** to each other, further increasing the risk of missing most of them.

It is almost impossible to predict the best and worst trading days, because daily movements are affected by many highly unpredictable events; they are usually identified only in hindsight. Rather than trying to identify good and bad days, a better strategy may be **dollar cost averaging**.

**d. Missing the best and worst days.** Market timers may miss some of the best days, but they may also miss some of the worst. Removing the **worst 25 days** of S&P 500 performance over the same period, the return rebounds to **2,750% (7.6% annualised)** — better than the **1,910% (6.7% annualised)** earned by the buy-and-hold investor.

These results do **not** necessarily show that market timing is superior to passive management. Because market timers are not always invested, their non-participation means they are likely to miss some of the best **and** some of the worst days. Direct evidence that market timers can beat a passive strategy is needed.

Unfortunately, most working adults lack the time, expertise or emotional temperament to be disciplined active managers of their own portfolios, and **70–80% of active fund managers** have been found to underperform the market each year. Dollar cost averaging as a passive form of investing for retirement may therefore be a good approach.

## 9.12 Summary

- In Singapore, pooled investments are CIS or unit trusts, governed by the Code on CIS. The VCC structure, introduced by the Variable Capital Companies Act in **October 2018**, allows free issuance and redemption of shares, dividends paid from capital, and an umbrella structure with sub-funds.
- ETFs track indices or fixed baskets and involve no active management. They trade during exchange hours, unlike unit trusts which transact at the daily closing NAV.
- Physical ETFs use full replication or representative sampling; synthetic ETFs use swaps and derivatives, reducing tracking error but adding counterparty risk.
- ETF advantages: diversification, small capital outlay, low costs, liquidity, transparency. Risks: tracking error, counterparty, market and sector, FX, and taxation.
- Smart beta ETFs select and weight securities on performance criteria rather than market capitalisation.
- VIX ETFs are for very short-term trading; leveraged and inverse ETFs are generally unsuitable for retail investors.
- A unit trust pools funds under an independent trustee. Open-end trusts issue units continuously at NAV; closed-end trusts have a fixed number of units priced by market demand and supply.
- Unit trust advantages: diversification, small outlay, professional management, flexibility, liquidity, lower transaction costs, security. Disadvantages: sales charges and management fees.
- Fees include front-end load (**1.5–5%**), back-end load, management fee (**0.5–2.0%** of NAV), operational costs (**0.1–0.15%** of NAV), switching fee (about **1%**) and performance fee subject to a high watermark.
- The offer price adds purchase expenses, cash and accrued income and the manager's initial charge; the bid price deducts sale expenses and adds cash and accrued income.
- Retrocessions create conflicts of interest and reduce NAV.
- A REIT must invest at least **75%** in income-producing real estate, distributes at least **90%** of taxable income, and is subject to a **45%** leverage limit. Properties are valued only **once a year**.
- Business trusts are governed by the Business Trusts Act 2004, are not separate legal entities, use a single trustee-manager, may distribute from operating cash flow, have **no gearing limit** and **no investment restrictions**.
- Stapled securities combine two or more securities traded as one instrument, and cannot be marketed as REITs.
- UCITS is an EU directive providing a passport for cross-border fund sales; MAS has allowed UCITS distribution in Singapore since **2006**.
- Alternative investments diversify because they are less correlated with equities and bonds, but do not necessarily buffer during crises.
- Hedge fund strategies include relative value (long-short equity, convertible arbitrage), event-driven (distressed securities, corporate events), global macro, and directional quantitative trading.
- Private equity stages: venture capital, growth capital, buyout, and special situations (going private, distressed). Cash flows follow a **J-curve**, with the investment period of **5–6 years** and harvest from year 6.
- Whole life insurance provides life cover plus cash value; endowment insurance matures after a fixed period. With-profits policies link benefits indirectly; investment-linked policies link directly.
- Immediate annuities begin payments at purchase; deferred annuities begin later. Annuities protect against longevity risk, the opposite of life insurance.
- CPFIS admission requires top-quartile performance and total expense ratios benchmarked to the CPFIS median.
- Dollar cost averaging invests equal amounts over time, accumulating more units when prices are low.

## 9.13 Additional Reference Materials

### 9.13.1 Key Features of Variable Capital Companies

The **Variable Capital Company (VCC)** is a legal entity structure for investment funds introduced to offer asset managers **greater flexibility and lower costs**, encouraging fund managers to co-locate fund domiciliation with their substantive fund management activities in Singapore and deepen the asset servicing ecosystem.

Key features:

- A VCC can be used for both **traditional and alternative funds**, and for both **open- and closed-end** funds.
- A VCC can house a **standalone fund or a collection of sub-funds** under an umbrella structure, with **segregation of assets and liabilities** of the sub-fund. An umbrella structure lets asset managers achieve cost efficiencies by consolidating administrative functions at the umbrella level.
- Shares of the VCC can be **redeemed without shareholders' approval**, and **dividends are payable from capital**, offering greater flexibility than existing investment companies. Shares are redeemed and issued at **NAV**.
- A VCC must be managed by a fund manager that is **registered, licensed or exempted by MAS**.
- For taxation, a VCC is a **company and a single tax entity**.
- A VCC **does not need to publicly disclose its shareholder register**, offering investor privacy.

### 9.13.2 CPF Investment Risk Classification System

The CPF Board has classified unit trusts included under the **CPF Investment Scheme (CPFIS)** under different risk categories. The **Risk Classification System** splits the investment risk of a unit trust into **two axes**: **equity risk** and **focus risk**.

**i. Equity risk** relates to the proportion of "riskier" investments in the unit trust. The greater the proportion invested in stocks, the higher the equity risk; the greater the proportion in bonds and cash, the lower the equity risk. Four categories:

| Category | Fund type |
|---|---|
| **Higher risk** | Equity funds |
| **Medium to high risk** | Balanced funds |
| **Low to medium risk** | Bond funds |
| **Lower risk** | Cash funds |

**ii. Focus risk** reflects how focused the investments are in one particular geographical region, country, industry or individual company. Two categories:

- **Broadly diversified** — investments spread across relatively more regions, countries, industries and companies.
- **Narrowly focused** — investments concentrated in particular regions, countries, industries or companies.

In general, a unit trust has a **higher** level of focus risk when it is relatively **less diversified**. A unit trust investing in shares of companies worldwide has **lower** focus risk than one investing only in Japanese corporations; the Japan-only trust has higher focus risk because it invests in one foreign country only.

Within the focus risk category, CPFIS-included unit trusts are further divided into sub-groups:

- **Regional** — regional exposure, for example Europe, Asia or Emerging Markets.
- **Sector** — specific industry exposure, for example healthcare and biotechnology.
- **Country** — country-specific, for example Japan or China.

These sub-groups make it easier for CPF members to compare unit trusts that are roughly similar in geographic focus.

The system works alongside the CPFIS admission criteria: a fund must be in the **top quartile of its global peer group** and have its **total expense ratio benchmarked to the median of CPFIS funds** in the respective risk class before it can accept CPF monies.

## Exam cues

- **VCC Act passed October 2018.** Two features: free issue and redemption of shares with dividends from capital, and an umbrella structure with sub-funds.
- **SPDR STI ETF:** priced at about **1/100th** of the STI level; board lot **100 units**; dividends **twice a year**; annual costs **0.3%**; creation and redemption only through participating dealers in **100,000-unit** blocks.
- **ETF arbitrage:** traded price **above** NAV → sell ETF, buy the basket; **below** NAV → sell the basket, buy the ETF.
- **Physical vs synthetic:** physical uses full replication or representative sampling; synthetic uses swaps and derivatives. **Synthetic has lower tracking error but higher counterparty risk.**
- **ETF advantages:** diversification, small outlay, low cost (**under 1%** vs **1–2%** for active unit trusts), liquidity, transparency.
- **ETF risks:** tracking error (greater in **physical**), counterparty, market and sector, FX, and **US withholding tax** for non-US investors in US ETFs.
- **Securities lending** creates counterparty risk; collateral is typically about **105%** of the lent stock's value.
- **Smart beta** weights on performance criteria rather than market capitalisation.
- **VIX ETFs are for trading, not investing.** VXX returned **−56.85%** while SVXY (short VIX) returned **+35.26%** in the same period.
- **Leveraged ETFs** are **2x or 3x**, with normal daily moves of **2–4%** and possible **±20–25%** in a session. **Not suitable for retail investors.** Issuers use **reverse splits** to reset prices.
- **Unit trust terminology:** US = mutual fund; UK = unit trust; Germany = investment fund; Luxembourg = **Sicaf** (closed-end) and **Sicav** (open-end). Most offshore funds are domiciled in **Luxembourg**.
- **Open-end vs closed-end:** open-end issues units continuously at **NAV**; closed-end has a **fixed** number of units priced by **market demand and supply**.
- **Trustee holds the assets and issues or redeems units**; the manager does neither.
- **Unit trust advantages:** diversification and divisibility, small outlay (**$1,000–$5,000**), professional management, flexibility (switching within a family of funds), liquidity, lower transaction costs, security.
- **Unit trust disadvantages:** sales charges and management fees paid regardless of performance.
- **Front-end load 1.5–5% of NAV; management fee 0.5–2.0% of NAV; operational costs 0.1–0.15% of NAV; switching fee about 1%; back-end load often waived after 60 days.**
- **High watermark** prevents a performance fee being paid twice on the same recovered value.
- **Offer price** = NAV + purchase expenses + cash and accrued income + manager's initial charge. **Bid price** = NAV − sale expenses + cash and accrued income.
- **Concentration:** many funds cap a single security at **5%**; some allow **8–9%**.
- **Fund size:** a **SGD10,000** expense is **0.125%** of an **SGD8m** fund but **1%** of an **SGD1m** fund. Very large funds lose nimbleness and stop accepting subscriptions at **optimal capacity**.
- **Retrocessions** create conflicts of interest, reduce NAV, and are **banned by the courts in Switzerland** but still practised in Asia.
- **REIT:** at least **75%** in income-producing real estate; distributes at least **90%** of taxable income; **45%** leverage limit; properties valued **once a year**; listed on **SGX Mainboard**.
- **REIT advantages over direct property:** affordability, diversification, liquidity, **90% distribution tax advantage**, professional management.
- **REIT risks:** market, income, concentration, liquidity, leverage, refinancing.
- **Business trust:** Business Trusts Act **2004**; **not a separate legal entity**; **single trustee-manager**; distributions from **operating cash flow**; **no gearing limit**; **no investment restrictions**; **single-tier taxation**.
- **REIT vs BT:** REIT has separate trustee and manager, passive investments, mandatory **90%** distribution, **45%** gearing cap, **90%** rental income requirement, tax-exempt for individuals. BT has a single trustee-manager, active business, no minimum distribution, no gearing cap, no investment restriction, single-tier tax.
- **Stapled securities** combine a passive and an active income security; the issuer must still meet the REIT's **90%** distribution on the REIT component; **cannot be marketed as a REIT**.
- **UCITS:** EU directive, in the UK since **2002**; **UCITS III** allows derivatives and index tracking; MAS permitted distribution in Singapore since **2006**.
- **Hedge funds vs conventional:** alpha vs alpha+beta, absolute vs index targets, leverage permitted vs limited, less transparent and slower to unwind, performance fees.
- **Hedge fund risks:** risky strategies (basis and counterparty risk), illiquidity (lock-ups and gates), poorer investor protection.
- **Hedge fund strategies:** long-short equity, convertible arbitrage, fixed income arbitrage, credit long-short, volatility arbitrage; event-driven (distressed, corporate events); global macro; directional quantitative (momentum).
- **Distressed debt** can be priced at **10–99% of par**. A Swiss bank sold a billion-dollar portfolio at a huge loss in **2016**.
- **Private equity J-curve:** investment period **5–6 years**, harvest from **year 6**, expected IRR **10–15%**, early redemption typically results in losses.
- **Private equity stages:** venture capital, growth capital, buyout, special situations (going private, distressed).
- **Commodity funds:** agriculture suffers overhanging supply; energy and metals are cyclical; precious metals do well in uncertainty.
- **Whole life** pays on death whenever it occurs and builds cash value; **endowment** matures after a fixed period (**10, 15 or 20 years**) and pays on maturity or death, whichever is earlier. Endowment premiums are **higher**, and guaranteed cash values may be **less than premiums paid**.
- **With-profits** links benefits **indirectly** through annual valuation and a **reversionary bonus** decided by the board; **investment-linked** links **directly** to unit values and fluctuates daily.
- **Immediate annuity** begins payments at purchase with a **single premium**; **deferred annuity** begins later and depends on contributions, interest, **gender** and **age**.
- **Annuity formulas:** $$PV = C[1 - (1+i)^{-n}]/i$$; $$FV = C[(1+i)^n - 1]/i$$. Worked: $1,000 for 5 years at 3% → **$4,579.71**; $1,000 for 8 years at 5% → **$9,549.11**.
- **Annuities protect against longevity risk; life insurance protects against premature death** — exact opposites.
- **CPFIS admission:** top quartile of the global peer group, total expense ratio benchmarked to the CPFIS median in the risk class.
- **Dollar cost averaging worked example:** $60,000 over 12 months at prices averaging $4.633 gives an average cost of **$4.622** and **12,980 units**, against **12,000 units** at **$5** if invested at the start.
- **Trap:** a **REIT** must distribute **90%** of income, which is why it cannot easily build cash reserves — the source of its **refinancing risk**.
- **Trap:** a **business trust** has **no gearing limit**, but that is not a licence for indiscriminate borrowing.
- **Trap:** **capital guaranteed** funds guarantee only if held to **maturity**; **capital preservation** funds have no guarantor and use less onerous language.
- **Trap:** a **hedge fund** is not necessarily hedged — it may be net long or short.
- **Trap:** **retrocessions** are paid by the fund house to the distributor and reduce the fund's NAV, so they harm existing investors as well as creating a conflict.
