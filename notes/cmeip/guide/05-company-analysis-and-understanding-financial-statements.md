# Chapter 5 — Company Analysis and Understanding Financial Statements

Company analysis serves both equity and credit work: any security whose payments depend on an issuer's continuing viability is analysed through the same three statements. This chapter covers the qualitative advantages that precede the numbers, the structure of the balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement, the per-share calculations, and the four families of ratio the examination tests.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Explain company analysis in the context of fundamental analysis.
- Read and interpret the three major financial statements — the balance sheet, the income statement and the statement of changes in cash flow.
- Analyse a company's financial performance using financial ratios.
- Explain the liquidity of a company using the current, quick and cash ratios.
- Explain financial risk and leverage using the debt-to-equity ratio, total debt to total assets ratio, interest coverage and total fixed charge coverage.
- Analyse operating efficiency using total asset turnover, inventory turnover, days to sell inventory, receivables turnover and the collection period for accounts receivables.
- Analyse profitability in relation to sales or capital using gross profit margin, operating profit margin, net profit margin, return on assets, return on equity and earnings per share.
- Understand the use of valuation ratios in estimating the value of a share.
- Understand what good quality of financial statements and good quality of earnings mean.
- Explain the importance of comparative and trend analysis.
- Identify the limitations in the use of financial ratios.

## 5.1 Introduction

Company analysis does not relate only to equity analysis. It is equally relevant to **credit analysis** of bond investments and of any other security whose cash flow payments depend on the ongoing viability of the issuing company.

Company analysis divides into two parts:

- **Qualitative analysis.**
- **Quantitative analysis** (financial statement analysis).

Three principles govern the work:

- **Asset prices move on future prospects.** Historical statements show recent profitability and how the company has been managed. Historical numbers, ratios and relationships are used to make projections given the outlook. Prices move when expectations about future performance change.
- **Qualitative factors drive performance.** Price movements are first driven by news and information bearing on prospects. It matters more to focus on the qualitative conditions that allow success than to rely on ratios alone, because those conditions drive and precede the quantitative outcomes.
- **Excellent companies can look expensive and still be good investments.** Companies with strong business models and excellent earnings growth may see price run ahead of earnings in the near term. If the positive qualitative factors persist and steady, sustainable earnings growth is delivered, earnings catch up with valuations and prices rise. Buying weaker or loss-making companies that have been heavily sold down brings, at best, short-term trading opportunities; at worst it traps investors as prospects keep deteriorating.

## 5.2 Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative analysis identifies the characteristics that let a company outperform its peers. Eight competitive advantages are examinable.

| # | Competitive advantage | Companies exhibiting it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dominance and control of key technology, research and patents | Apple, Nvidia, Pfizer, Johnson & Johnson, Novartis |
| 2 | Control over key resource supply | De Beers, Bayer-Monsanto |
| 3 | Superior brand differentiation | Apple, Coca-Cola, Microsoft, BMW, Mercedes, Samsung, Toyota, Facebook, SIA, Disney, Google, Nestlé, Nike |
| 4 | Control of distribution channels | Alibaba, Amazon, Tencent |
| 5 | Cost efficiency, lowest-cost producer | Xiaomi, TSMC, McDonald's, IKEA |
| 6 | Successful focus on niche clients | Harley Davidson, Swiss private banks (UBS, Credit Suisse, Julius Baer), LVMH, Prada |
| 7 | Superior management, talent, connections | Apple, Alibaba, Microsoft, Berkshire Hathaway, Cheung Kong-Hutchinson Holdings |
| 8 | Diversified core businesses with shared costs | General Electric, 3M, Honeywell |

**Technology, research and patents.** Control over technology lets a company maintain higher prices and margins while growing revenue. Patented technology can give an almost monopolistic influence as the sole supplier in the high-end range, establishing significant price premiums. Apple continues to command a premium over other smartphone producers; Nvidia, the top-end chipmaker designing GPUs for gaming, mobile computing and automotive, enjoys higher margins than competitors because it focuses on top-end chips.

**Dominance over supply.** Substantial control over raw materials and resources is an advantage. Despite giving up almost monopolistic control of diamond supply decades ago, **De Beers still controls about 30–35% of world diamond supply**. Monsanto, a leading producer of genetically modified seeds, was acquired by Bayer, another leading producer; the combined group should hold more than half the GMO seed crop market.

**Superior brand differentiation.** A strong brand lets products command higher pricing, better sales and better margins. Luxury companies are especially conscious of brand image. Some brands are known for precision, quality and dependability (Mercedes, BMW, Honda, Toyota); others for superior service standards (Singapore Airlines).

**Controlling the distribution channel.** Dominating the sales channel is a critical advantage. The need for costly physical infrastructure is now less of a barrier because of the internet, which has redefined how companies market, communicate, collect payments and deliver. Traditional brick-and-mortar retailers have been forced into a blended approach. Companies with a first-mover advantage on internet platforms (Alibaba, Amazon, Tencent) take a larger share of sales commissions and advertising revenue on top of selling their own products.

**Cost efficiency, lowest-cost producer.** Some companies compete by being the most efficient producers, driving volume on very low margins through efficiency and economies of scale. Some have become outsourcing partners for manufacturers that no longer want to bear manufacturing cost and retooling risk. These partners have lower cost and scalability, operate on thin margins, and offer economies by aggregating orders from different companies into high volumes. The infrastructure cost needed for scale becomes a natural barrier to entry. Examples: **Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing, Foxconn and Samsung**, major subcontractors for other technology companies.

**Successful focus on niche clients or products.** Specialising in a particular client group often commands premium fees and pricing. Harley Davidson caters to a high-end niche of motorcycle enthusiasts; Swiss private banks and luxury goods manufacturers have long focused on high net worth clients.

**Superior management, talent and relationships.** Strong management and founders with foresight, expertise or relationships are hard to emulate. Entrepreneurial acumen and creative genius cannot easily be transferred, letting some companies dominate their fields. Others benefit from management's relationships with markets and governments. Examples: Steve Jobs and Apple, Bill Gates and Microsoft, Warren Buffett and Berkshire Hathaway, Jack Ma and Alibaba, Li Ka-shing and Cheung Kong Hutchinson Holdings.

**Diversified core businesses with shared costs.** Some companies regularly identify new core businesses, typically holding **3–4 core businesses** where they have a competitive advantage and lead market share. Resources focus on the cores, with a smaller portion for new business development. Sharing common costs (administrative, marketing, human resources) lets businesses compete more aggressively on pricing and leverage shared capabilities. When one business hits a cyclical downturn, the others support earnings until it recovers. Typically one or two cores become **cash cows** funding new growth. As margins narrow with industry maturity and product life cycles reach their tail end, promising peripheral businesses are converted into new cores. Examples: General Electric, Honeywell, 3M.

## 5.3 Quantitative Analysis (Financial Statements Analysis)

Financial statements show the resources available to management, how those resources are financed, and what the company has accomplished with them. They are prepared for many parties: creditors, shareholders and investors, tax authorities, industry regulators, senior managers, the board and employees.

Three statements are analysed:

- The **balance sheet**.
- The **income statement**.
- The **cash flow statement**.

### 5.3.1 Historical Financial Statements

A company's current value depends on potential earnings and future cash flow, not historical data. Why then examine history?

For most companies the most recent historical performance is still the best reference point for near-term forecasts. Although "history is not necessarily a good predictor of the future", the odds tilt in the investor's favour when investing in companies with a track record of solid earnings and financials. Companies that have faced difficulties recently are generally more likely to keep encountering problems unless conditions change significantly.

Analysing past growth and ratios helps identify leading companies with the right strategies and competitive advantages. Analysts combine that with research on future industry demand and supply to project earnings better.

### 5.3.2 Presenting Financial Statements

Financial statements must be **audited**. The investment community expects auditors to make qualifying comments where statements are not well prepared. **Qualified reports** are issued when auditors are not completely satisfied with how the statements are prepared.

| Market | Reporting frequency | Accounting standard |
|---|---|---|
| **United States** | Quarterly statements to shareholders and the investment community is the norm | **US GAAP**, adopted by the US Securities and Exchange Commission |
| **Europe** | Semi-annual and annual statements are the minimum in some markets | **IFRS** for all listed European companies |
| **Singapore** | Most companies currently provide **quarterly** reporting in the interest of transparency | **IFRS** |

Because US GAAP and IFRS differ, **adjustments and restatements** are needed to compare companies across markets properly.

### 5.3.3 Reviewing Financial Statements

Four steps:

- Examine key ratios over time and explain the reasons behind the company's performance and financial health.
- Forecast likely future performance from the research outlook on the economy and industry, key performance drivers, and interviews with management and industry experts.
- Compare financial health and projected performance with other companies in the same industry or league.
- Estimate fair value given future earnings potential relative to similar companies and general market conditions.

## 5.4 The Balance Sheet

The balance sheet shows the financial condition of a company **at a point in time**, typically at the financial year-end. It has three parts:

- **Assets** — the resources owned: cash, accounts receivables, inventories, motor vehicles, property, plant and equipment.
- **Liabilities** — amounts owed to banks, suppliers, vendors and other non-bank parties: accounts payables, bank loans, overdrafts.
- **Shareholders' equity** — total assets minus total liabilities; the amount financed using ordinary and preferred shares. It equals **share capital plus retained earnings minus treasury shares**.

| Jurisdiction | Presentation | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **US** | Assets on one side; liabilities and shareholders' equity summing to the same total on the other | $$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' Equity}$$ |
| **British** | Assets net of liabilities (net assets) on one side, equating to shareholders' equity on the other | $$\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} = \text{Shareholders' Equity (net assets)}$$ |

**Worked illustration — Singapore Airlines.** SIA's financial year runs **1 April to 31 March**. The statements used are for FY2020/2021, FY2019/2020 and FY2018/2019; competitor airlines use a calendar year. The analysis spans the COVID pandemic, which came to global prominence in **Q1 2020** and hit the airline industry severely. Government intervention differed across airlines, which complicates trend analysis and peer comparison.

For SIA in 2021: property, plant and equipment **62.5%** of total assets; borrowings (long-term) **28.1%**; cash and bank balances **20.7%**; total equity **43.3%**.

When examining a balance sheet, focus on:

- The **largest items** on both sides.
- The **relationship between assets and liabilities**.
- **Significant trends** in the accounts over time.

## 5.5 Assets

Assets convertible to cash within a year are **current assets**; the rest are **long-term or non-current assets**. The amount of current assets relative to current liabilities reflects short-term liquidity. Ratios compare current assets against current liabilities, against overall liabilities, and as a proportion of total assets.

The questions of interest are how assets are allocated for productive use to generate earnings, whether asset values will change over time, and what mix of debt and equity funds them.

### 5.5.1 Cash and Bank Balances

Some companies hold a large share of earnings in cash because of the liquidity, risk and operational needs of their industry. In cyclical downturns a large cash balance accumulated from past earnings lets a company keep funding the business and meet short-term obligations. Excess cash also lets it buy raw materials cheaply and invest directly in technology, property, equipment and businesses without extra debt or asset sales.

| Airline | Cash / total assets (December 2020) |
|---|---|
| IAG | 19.1% |
| Cathay Pacific | 9.5% |
| American Airlines | 0.4% |
| **SIA (March 2021)** | **20.7%** |

SIA maintained a cash war chest much higher than most airlines for several years. More recently, proceeds from capital raised at the height of the pandemic in mid-2020 (rights issue and mandatory convertible bonds) were kept mostly in cash, presumably as a war chest to conserve liquidity should the downturn be protracted, or for future investment in plant and machinery when the market recovers.

Note that IAG's "high" cash ratio results from a **reduced balance sheet** (denominator), driven largely by a decline in shareholders' equity from losses and reduced equity reserves during the pandemic.

**Disadvantages of high cash.** Cash not invested in the business is an **underutilised asset**, and a perpetually large balance sacrifices returns available from other financial assets. In some countries large cash balances make companies targets for acquisition. High cash and cash generation also suggest potential for high dividend payouts: management may set cash aside for larger payouts if it sees no need for additional investment. Cash-based businesses such as gaming and mature industries such as tobacco (Genting, Las Vegas Sands; Altria, BATS, Swedish Match) naturally run high cash balances and pay high dividends.

### 5.5.2 Inventories

For retailers, a warning signal is a **sudden build-up in inventories amid falling sales**. If products stay longer on the shelves, the company may be struggling to sell.

An inventory build-up can also be positive:

- An increase in finished goods in anticipation of greater demand next period.
- An increase in raw material inventory ahead of rising prices, or an opportunity to buy at very low prices.
- A change in the mix of products manufactured.

Investigate the underlying reason rather than concluding from the number alone. During downturns, rapid technological advance and changes in taste can make older inventory obsolete and hard to sell; excess inventory may require major price discounts and write-downs.

### 5.5.3 Account Receivables

Accounts receivables are amounts owed by customers for products or services delivered on credit. A **cash-based business** such as a supermarket or restaurant should not carry high receivables. Airline passenger ticket sales are largely cash-based — usually **95% or more** — so receivables are typically **less than 5%** of an airline's assets. Manufacturing companies selling on credit must watch receivables for bad debts.

Pay attention to receivables quality in downturns. Where a company depends on a few buyers, receivables may unexpectedly become bad debts if a major buyer becomes insolvent. Knowing the main buyers, the share of sales to them, and tracking their performance can give early warning. **Falling sales with a build-up in receivables during a recession** signals collection problems and likely bad debts in later quarters.

**Rising receivables alongside rising sales** may mean the company is offering more credit terms to distributors and retailers so its goods gain an advantage over competing products. Low cash sales suggest more credit and hire purchase sales, which generate additional interest earnings and can be lucrative (Courts in Singapore; car dealers offering credit alongside banks).

A **reduction** in receivables, for example by shortening the collection period, raises liquidity. That may indicate liquidity challenges, or simply a more efficient collection system.

### 5.5.4 Investments

Some companies hold sizeable investments. Investment holding companies are in the business of managing investments — Berkshire Hathaway is the prominent example. When businesses generate strong cash flows, managers may invest excess cash in financial assets rather than reinvesting entirely, or park cash in short-term securities until a better use appears. Over time the portfolio can become large; SIA's short- and long-term investments combined were no more than **S$500 million** in each of the three years.

For some companies investment income exceeds income from the core business. When investments become too large they should be outsourced to different fund managers for diversification, professional monitoring and accountability. Self-management is acceptable provided there are sufficient checks and balances.

Low-risk, yield-oriented investments give a steady income stream; riskier holdings can produce significant **mark-to-market (MTM) losses** when markets turn. Accounting best practice is to reflect investments at **market value**, but some countries allow latitude on unrealised gains and losses — for example, permitting reclassification of short-term investments into long-term investments at cost or book with no immediate MTM requirement. Representatives should be aware of this potential manipulation.

### 5.5.5 Plant, Property and Equipment

SIA's largest balance-sheet item is Aircraft, Parts and Engines. Together with Plant, Property and Other Equipment it accounts for **62.5%** of total assets. Other airlines may classify both under plant, property and equipment (PPE). Large PPE balances are characteristic of **capital-intensive industries**: shipping, airlines, railways, steel manufacturing, plantations and mining.

| Airline | PPE / total assets (December 2020) |
|---|---|
| IAG | 57.9% |
| Cathay Pacific | 64.5% |
| American Airlines | 51.1% |
| **SIA (March 2021)** | **62.5%** |

Four considerations when reviewing PPE:

- The **age** of plants, equipment, aircraft and vessels.
- Whether the **technology** is obsolete.
- The estimated **cost of upgrading**.
- How resources are deployed to **fund upgrades**.

SIA funds fleet and equipment renewal largely from equity capital and reserves, and renews diligently as part of its branding and value proposition. For manufacturing, the concern is that large plant commitments can become obsolete quickly given the pace of technological change. Where competing technologies exist and eventual victors emerge, obsolescence must be managed: choosing a technology that becomes obsolete too quickly can mean massive write-downs and the cost of replacement, plus staff retraining.

Some companies pass obsolescence risk to subcontractors, keeping PPE spending manageable. In **Apple's 2015/16 accounts, PPE was only 7–8% of overall assets**.

### 5.5.6 Property

In Singapore, Hong Kong and most of Asia, property companies — developers and owners of commercial and retail property, and REITs — are blue-chip investments. Property holdings form the bulk of their value, typically **55–70%**, functioning both as an earnings generator and a long-term store of value. Including holdings through associates and joint ventures, the proportion is even larger, **70–85%**.

| Property company | Investment property, landbank and development property | Current property | Total property holdings |
|---|---|---|---|
| City Developments | 37.8% | 26.3% | 64.1% |
| CapitaLand | 43.2% | 10.6% | 53.8% |
| UOL | 47.3% | 10.2% | 57.5% |
| GuocoLand | 41.1% | 31.2% | 72.3% |

Properties developed and completed for sale in the current period are **inventory** for a property company and sit in current assets. Undeveloped land bank, developments beyond a year, and investment property held for rental income are classified as **long-term assets**.

Property companies are significantly affected by economic slowdowns when inventory and asset values are revised down. Write-downs have occurred in major downturns, though upward revisions are more common because of re-zoning, reclassification of land use and general land price appreciation in Asia. Companies in Singapore and Malaysia that acquired industrial and agricultural property in the ordinary course of business later redeveloped it for residential use in the 1990s. **Singapore land values rose about 14 times, or about 6.8% a year, over the 40 years from 1976 to 2016.**

Because significant value is locked in physical property, lowering the productive use of capital, many property companies unlock it by injecting non-core holdings into REITs for listing, freeing capital for higher-return investments.

In downturns with high unemployment, watch the funding support for property holdings with large development commitments. Residential property under development may stay on the books much longer, incurring more interest and charges. Small developers face higher liquidity risk, and bank credit lines tighten or become costlier. With insufficient cash reserves relative to commitments, over-commitment can produce a liquidity crunch; in previous downturns several small developers were forced to liquidate choice properties at the bottom of the cycle or were acquired.

### 5.5.7 Intangible Assets

Intangible assets of value include **goodwill, patents, copyrights, trademarks and licences**, which have a long predetermined legal life. Many intangibles — goodwill is the notable exception — are **amortised** over time.

For companies that have made acquisitions, the common intangible is **goodwill**: the premium paid above the book value of the target. The acquirer pays a premium for brand value, proprietary technology or superior management. Goodwill represents a value premium over the fair market value of tangible assets, so large write-downs are common. Energy companies wrote down more goodwill than most industries because of the sharp oil price drop caused by COVID-19; the largest impairment in 2020 was **Baker Hughes' USD14.77 billion write-down**, associated with an oil-field services and equipment business largely acquired in its 2017 merger with GE's oil and gas division.

Many companies write down goodwill even without urgent need. A goodwill write-down is typically charged directly against **equity reserves**. Unproductive asset value leaves total assets while nothing changes operationally, so **ROA and ROE in subsequent years are higher** because the denominators are lower. Bank CEOs remunerated on ROA and ROE improvement can therefore deliver "better performance" over their tenure that reflects systematic write-downs rather than operating improvement.

## 5.6 Liabilities

### 5.6.1 Debt and Bank Borrowings

On the liabilities side, pay particular attention to bank borrowings and other debt financing such as bond issues. Four questions:

- The **size** of debt used to finance assets and relative to equity.
- The **funding mix** for the debt obligations.
- The **currency denomination** of the debt.
- The **security and terms and conditions** of the debt.

**a. Size of debt and bank borrowing.** The higher the debt funding the asset base, the greater the financial risk. Over-reliance on debt in a low-rate environment causes difficulty servicing it if rates rise significantly. There should be an appropriate debt-equity mix, compared against the industry norm.

Companies with larger debt tend to be those that:

- Are regularly engaged in major infrastructure or property projects (construction and engineering, property developers).
- Require large asset commitments (steel and iron foundries, power generation, ships and aircraft, mining, oil drilling).
- Are involved in leveraged mergers and acquisitions.

Perpetual refinancing is used by some, but can suddenly become difficult and costly in downturns or after project overruns. Having sufficient cash flow for immediate obligations and the ability to mobilise liquid assets to retire debt matter especially to bond investors.

**b. Funding mix.** Companies should maintain an appropriate mix between:

- Short-term and long-term financing.
- Funding from different banks and the capital markets.
- Funding from shareholders.

Excessive funding of long-term assets with short-term financing creates liquidity problems because short-term assets may not cover short-term obligations. In downturns, long-term borrowings can suddenly become short-term if banks deny credit extensions — documentation often contains clauses letting banks shorten the credit term. Hence the need for a mix of funding sources rather than dependence on one or two banks. For companies large enough, bond issues during low-rate cycles provide cheap financing beyond bank borrowings.

**c. Currency denomination of debt.** An unhedged foreign currency obligation is precarious. Companies borrowing in a foreign currency because of low rates (Japan) risk the obligation growing if that currency appreciates.

In **1997**, many Southeast Asian companies financed operations with **USD-denominated Eurobonds**, which offered lower rates than much higher local Asian rates — a carry trade of borrowing in USD and investing in local debt. When the **Rupiah and Baht fell by more than 75%** during the Asian Currency Crisis, the USD Eurobond borrowings **quadrupled in local currency terms**, and many companies found local currency cash flows and liquid assets could not cover the USD debt. Several declared bankruptcy.

### 5.6.2 Accounts Payables

Accounts payables show short-term credit extended by suppliers for goods and services. The longer the payment period, the longer the effective financing received. To improve operating cash flow, a company can stretch payables while shortening the receivables collection period.

A **build-up of payables** in quarterly statements may suggest the company is trying to increase liquidity. A negative reading is that the company is struggling to pay, signalled by a build-up of payables each quarter **accompanied by falling sales**.

### 5.6.3 Deferred Taxes

Deferred tax expected to be paid in the current period is a **current liability**; portions payable in future years are **long-term liabilities**. When earnings rise consistently year on year and the company defers part of its tax, deferred taxes tend to rise. Constant deferral provides a form of **free capital**. If earnings fall sharply or turn negative, deferred taxes fall.

### 5.6.4 Pension Obligations

Less common in Singapore, pension obligations feature in countries without a national retirement fund, where a pension is set aside as a retirement benefit for long-serving employees. Some international companies, especially those with US exposure, hold pension funds for international staff. In markets such as the US, where pension obligations are monitored under the **Employee Retirement Income Security Act**, pension obligations can become a major problem if poorly managed and underfunded.

## 5.7 Shareholders' Equity

Shareholders' equity reflects the accumulated net worth, or accounting book value, of a company: what shareholders own after all liabilities are paid. It consists of:

- Share capital.
- Reserves.
- Provisions.
- Foreign exchange adjustments.

### 5.7.1 Share Capital

Share capital is the original capital provided by common and preferred shareholders. **Preferred shareholders** receive a fixed dividend and are paid before common shareholders, but do not benefit from future earnings growth. **Common shareholders'** dividends can increase with earnings over time but are paid only after preferred holders receive their fixed payments. Common shareholders are therefore **residual owners**, earning what remains after all other obligations. Share capital is the amount issued out of the total authorised share capital.

### 5.7.2 Treasury Stock

Treasury stock is the portion bought back and "retired" by the company. When a company buys back stock, remaining shareholders own a larger share of earnings and dividends. Buybacks create a treasury stock account and benefit the remaining common shareholders.

### 5.7.3 Reserves and Hidden Reserves

Accumulated net earnings or losses over the years, after dividends, are reported in **reserves** or capital reserves. Write-downs in the value of long-term assets or investments, and provisions for losses, are sometimes charged **directly against reserves** rather than through profit and loss, because they are not seen as part of normal operating performance.

Banks make large provisions for loan losses in downturns, but the losses may not materialise. When economies recover and customers repay what was classified as bad, some banks are slow to reverse provisioning, creating **hidden reserves**. Hidden reserves also arise from excessive write-downs of assets such as real estate and from provisions for extraordinary losses.

Having reserves means a company is probably worth more than its accounts state. Some companies write back provisions under profits or extraordinary profits to smooth or lift earnings in later years.

## 5.8 The Income Statement

The income statement, or profit and loss statement, is the main focus for most investors because it reflects sales and earnings performance, and it is accompanied by comments on the outlook. It can be recast as a **common size statement** based on sales revenue.

Four inferences follow:

- Revenue fell **76%** in FY2020/2021 against FY2019/2020. Costs are more inelastic, so the **S$9.5 billion** cut in operating expenses did not offset the **S$12 billion** revenue loss, producing an operating loss of about **S$2.5 billion**.
- Fuel, a variable cost, fell more than **75%** to roughly **S$1 billion**, but still accounted for **26%** of a reduced revenue base.
- The operating loss was S$2.5 billion but the pre-tax loss was **S$4.9 billion**, because of non-operating items: impairment of aircraft **S$1.7 billion**, impairment of goodwill **S$170 million**, losses of associated companies **S$127 million** and others **S$128 million**.
- The common size statement shows a **volume-driven business with small margins**: operating profit was **6.5%** of sales in FY2018/2019, a good pre-pandemic year, and net margin before tax **5.3%**. Changes in fuel prices or fare competition dramatically affect profitability.

Beyond the statements, qualitative comments at press and analyst briefings reveal management's outlook and the key variables they watch. Together with other research, these feed a forecast of future sales and earnings.

### 5.8.1 Sales and Revenue

Break sales revenue into two parts:

- **Unit sales growth.**
- **Pricing changes.**

Raising either increases revenue, so forecasting revenue means watching the factors that drive each.

| Unit sales growth drivers | Pricing change drivers |
|---|---|
| New geographical markets | Competition (direct and indirect) |
| New target customer segment | Brand value development |
| New uses | Cost of production |
| New range or version of product | Buyers' financial health |
| Production capacity | Government pricing restrictions |
| Competitors' market share | |
| Limits to competition | |

For an airline, securing new lucrative routes adds ticket sales (more units); successful service quality and branding allows premium pricing. Conversely, new players with deep financial pockets create strong competition and reduce revenue.

**Segmental breakdown.** Breaking revenue and profit down by line of business clarifies the contribution of each. Tracking news on the sales and performance of key businesses and new products lets the analyst anticipate the impact on overall growth. Companies also provide segmental breakdowns of assets to show resource allocation.

### 5.8.2 Interest and Investment Income

Cash-rich companies with strong cash flows over several years accumulate financial investments, producing large interest and dividend flows that may not be part of core activity. SIA's interest income was **S$35 million** and dividend income **S$8.4 million** in FY2020/2021.

Some companies have made speculative investments that wiped out core profits. Review the accounting notes to understand the type of investments made and the likely income volatility.

### 5.8.3 Costs and Expenses

Watch for changes in major cost items measured as a percentage of sales.

**a. Major cost items.** For manufacturers, production costs — labour, raw materials, utilities — sit in cost of goods sold, with raw materials typically significant. If raw material prices fall while finished goods prices and sales hold, margins rise. Some manufacturers separate production costs from marketing, distribution and administration, splitting costs into cost of goods sold and selling and administrative costs. Research and development is another significant cost not going directly into production. Distribution and service companies reflect cost of sales or operating cost before operating profit.

Three things to note:

- Major cost items as a percentage of sales.
- Significant rising or falling trends in cost (raw materials, fuel).
- Cost items that are **non-cash** accounting costs, which do not affect cash flow.

Major cost items differ by industry. In service industries such as banking and hospitality, staff costs dominate. In transportation, fuel, staff and depreciation dominate.

For SIA, staff costs were **30.4%** of revenue in FY2020/2021 and **16%** in FY2019/2020; fuel costs were **26.6%** and **29%** respectively.

Jet fuel prices are highly volatile. After the precipitous fall in H1 2020, the recovery was equally striking. Because higher fuel costs are hard to pass on through ticket prices, higher fuel prices cut airline profitability. Airlines hedge fuel with forwards and futures, and hedging brings its own problems: airlines that bought fuel futures for H1 2020 delivery became **over-hedged** when demand collapsed, and the unused long hedges had to be unwound at a loss when prices fell. Even without a demand collapse, airlines that hedged at forward prices become uncompetitive against unhedged airlines when fuel prices fall unexpectedly.

**b. Depreciation.** For capital-intensive companies depreciation can be significant. SIA's depreciation was more than **50% of sales** in FY2020/2021 under atypical conditions, and roughly **8%** in FY2018/2019. Depreciation is not a real cash outflow but an accounting method. For transport companies making large ongoing commitments to aircraft or ships, depreciation will always be large.

Depreciation can be abused. A cosmetic approach when performance is poor is to stretch depreciation over more years: a taxi fleet operator depreciating taxis over **10 years instead of 5** dramatically lowers depreciation expense and improves reported net profit, while nothing changes in cash flow.

## 5.9 The Cash Flow Statement

For capital-intensive industries, net earnings may not accurately show the ability to support short-term liabilities and borrowings. The cash flow statement adds back depreciation and other non-cash items, revealing the real cash earnings from operations and the level of liquidity.

It has three parts:

- Cash flow from **operating** activities.
- Cash flow from **investing** activities.
- Cash flow from **financing** activities.

Depreciation accounted for **63% (about S$2 billion)** of the negative operating cash flow in FY2020/2021; adding it back to the pre-tax loss greatly reduced the negative operating cash flow. Under the more benign conditions of FY2018/2019, profit before tax was **S$868 million** while operating cash flow was **S$2.8 billion** — more than three times larger.

The negative operating cash flow in FY2020/2021 was largely funded by the rights issue and mandatory convertible bonds totalling **S$8.8 billion**. Investing cash flow shows that large capital investments such as aircraft purchases were **deferred**. No dividends were paid. With reduced investing and additional capital raised, SIA ended FY2020/2021 with net cash and cash equivalents of **S$7.8 billion**.

**Why cash flow matters.** For most investors, and especially bondholders, the major concern is **consistently negative operating cash flow**. A poor overall net cash position can be temporarily masked by selling assets and businesses, creating positive investing cash flow. This often begins with selling peripheral and poorly performing businesses and excess assets, but a desperate company starts selling assets that would fuel future growth, or assets it can temporarily do without. Without recovery in the core business, these efforts come to nothing.

## 5.10 Per Share Calculations — BVPS, DPS, EPS and Price

The share count used depends on what is being measured.

| Measure | Formula | Share count |
|---|---|---|
| **Book value per share (BVPS)** | Shareholders' equity / total number of shares outstanding | Shares outstanding **at the financial year-end**, because the balance sheet is a snapshot |
| **Earnings per share (EPS)** | Net earnings / **weighted average** number of shares | Weighted average, because earnings were generated over the whole year and the share count fluctuates with issuance and buybacks |
| **Fully diluted EPS** | Net earnings / fully diluted number of shares | Assumes all derivatives and convertibles outstanding are exercised and converted |
| **Dividend per share (DPS)** | Dividends paid / total number of shares outstanding | Shares outstanding **as at the ex-dividend date** |

EPS should also be computed on a **fully diluted** basis, which is particularly relevant for EPS projections. To ensure comparability, all future EPS forecasts should be calculated on a fully diluted basis.

The share price is already per-share and is frequently compared with these computed figures. The **forward price-earnings (PE) ratio** is the price paid for future EPS and is a popular comparison method; the **price-to-book** ratio is another. Both are revisited in Chapter 6.

## 5.11 Ratio Analysis

Ratio analysis is an established method for interpreting financial accounts: it evaluates financial strength, the quality of earnings and performance using simple mathematical ratios.

**Used in isolation, ratios are meaningless.** An interest coverage ratio of two times may be low for some industries and very high for others. Findings must be compared and benchmarked against companies in the same industry. Properly applied, ratios diagnose financial health.

| Question | Ratios |
|---|---|
| How profitable is the company? | Return on assets, return on equity, gross profit margin, net profit margin, earnings per share, industry analysis |
| How solvent is the company? | Debt-to-equity, debt-to-total assets, interest coverage, current ratio, quick ratio |
| How efficiently does the company use its assets? | Total asset turnover, inventory turnover, receivables turnover |

Four categories are examined: **liquidity, leverage, operating efficiency/activity, and profitability**.

### 5.11.1 Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity is the availability of funds at any given time and at a reasonable cost. Liquidity ratios indicate the ability to meet short-term financial obligations. Banks are governed by liquidity ratio levels specified by their local authorities. One lesson of the 2008 Global Financial Crisis is the importance of liquidity, not just for banks but for all companies. Maintaining strong liquidity can be expensive when interest rates are high.

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Current ratio** | Current assets / current liabilities |
| **Quick ratio** | (Cash + marketable securities + accounts receivable) / current liabilities |
| **Cash ratio** | (Cash + near-cash) / current liabilities |
| **Cash flow ratio** | Cash flow from operating activities / current liabilities |

**a. Current ratio.** The most common liquidity ratio, measuring the degree to which current assets cover current liabilities. Conventional wisdom suggests **2 or more**, but the cost of too much liquidity is unproductive use of assets, since current assets tend to generate low returns. Companies now manage working capital more efficiently, producing generally lower current ratios.

**b. Quick ratio.** Also called the **acid test ratio**. Same denominator as the current ratio, but the numerator **excludes inventories** because they are less liquid. It indicates highly liquid assets available to cover current liabilities: cash, marketable securities and accounts receivables.

Quick ratios vary by industry. A supermarket's quick ratio may be as low as **0.2** because it holds substantial inventory, but that is not a liquidity concern: its current assets are fast-moving consumer goods financed by short-term supplier credit. Quick ratios are less meaningful for non-retail and non-merchandising companies such as banks and other financial institutions, which hold no inventory.

**c. Cash ratio.** A more stringent test, looking at cash and cash equivalents. The numerator typically includes only cash and short-term marketable securities convertible quickly to cash; assets that cannot immediately be converted are omitted.

**d. Operating cash flow ratio.** Shows the cash flow from operating activities available to cover current liabilities. Some companies have a weak current ratio, with barely enough current assets to cover current liabilities, yet generate cash flow well in excess of them — typical of **cash-based service businesses** that cover short-term liabilities by rendering services for cash rather than selling inventory on credit.

Conversely, a company may have a fine current ratio but a worrying operating cash flow ratio. A company in dire straits, overcommitted to loss-making projects, may liquidate peripheral long-term assets to shore up cash for near-term obligations. If operating cash flow and income stay negative, the strategy is unsustainable and default follows later. Most defaulting Singapore corporate bond and perpetual issuers showed this pattern.

**Worked illustration — SIA liquidity.** Liquidity ratios rose significantly in FY2020/2021 except the operating cash flow ratio, because most funding raised through the share issue and mandatory convertible bonds in 2020 was kept as cash (numerator) while a significant portion of short-term borrowings was repaid, and sales in advance of carriage fell sharply with the downturn, reducing total current liabilities (denominator). The operating cash flow ratio turned from **0.25** in FY2019/2020 to **−0.58** in FY2020/2021 because of the trading loss.

| Current ratio | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Cathay Pacific | 0.59 | 0.48 |
| IAG | 0.68 | 0.89 |
| American Airlines | 0.67 | 0.45 |
| **SIA (March 2021/2020)** | **1.69** | **0.44** |

**Window dressing.** Current and quick ratios at reporting dates are susceptible to manipulation. Toward year-end, companies can collect receivables earlier, reduce inventory below normal levels and delay purchases; proceeds from faster collection can pay off current liabilities.

**An arithmetic trap.** An equal dollar increase in both current assets and current liabilities does **not** leave the current ratio unchanged: it **lowers** the ratio if it was previously above 1, and **raises** it if it was previously below 1.

### 5.11.2 Leverage Ratios — Debt and Solvency Ratios

Leverage ratios show the financing mix between debt and equity, the amount of financial risk, and the capacity to meet principal and interest. They are of primary interest to **bondholders**, whose main risk is default. For other stakeholders they are more useful analysed alongside other ratios.

A principal consideration in capital structure is the extent of leverage. Higher leverage increases return on equity and reduces potential dilution of earnings, but may lower the credit rating and raise the cost of debt.

| Ratio | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Debt-to-equity (gearing)** | Total debt / total shareholders' equity | Debt includes all short- and long-term borrowings, including subordinated convertible bonds. Deferred taxes may or may not be included. Operating leases, especially if extensive, must be included as an estimate of the present value of lease payments |
| **Debt-to-total assets** | Total debt / total assets | Debt includes all borrowings, long- and short-term; assets include intangibles. Debts are carried at **face value** and not revalued |
| **Total liabilities-to-total assets** | Total liabilities / total assets | Shows how much of the asset base is funded by liabilities |

The greater the debt, the higher the leverage, the greater the impact on ROE, and the harder it is to generate sufficient income to repay. When reviewing debt-to-equity or debt-to-assets, **check for foreign currency-denominated debt**: if the foreign currency appreciates against the local currency, the market value of the debt becomes much larger. Companies with foreign currency bonds in countries with weak currencies are especially susceptible to ballooning debt.

**Worked illustration — SIA leverage.**

| Ratio | 2021 | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt/equity (gearing) | **0.70** | 1.01 | 0.49 |
| Debt/assets | **0.31** | 0.29 | 0.22 |
| Liabilities/assets | **0.57** | 0.71 | 0.55 |

SIA's gearing improved in FY2020/2021 principally because shareholders' equity (the denominator) rose through the rights issue and mandatory convertible bonds. The company has always been conservative with borrowings, and its gearing is the **lowest among airlines**.

| Gearing ratio | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Cathay Pacific | 1.27 | 1.55 |
| IAG | 11.91 | 2.09 |
| United Airlines | 4.49 | 1.26 |
| **SIA (March 2021/2020)** | **0.70** | **1.01** |

### 5.11.3 Coverage Ratios

**a. Interest coverage.** The times-interest-earned ratio indicates how many times fixed interest charges are covered by earnings. A higher ratio of earnings to interest indicates lower financial risk.

$$\text{Interest Coverage} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}}$$

SIA's coverage ratios were negative in FY2020/2021 because of trading losses. A higher positive ratio is good (lower financial risk); a negative ratio indicates the opposite — the loss as a multiple of financing charges. **The size of a negative ratio is also determined by the absolute amount of interest charges.** A firm with a coverage ratio of **−1** does not necessarily have lower financial risk than one at **−3**: both are incurring trading losses, but the −1 firm has **higher financing charges** (the denominator), which is not a good thing even though both made the same trading loss.

All airlines in the comparison incurred net losses in 2020 and therefore show negative coverage. Do not over-interpret negative coverage ratios when assessing financial risk — the denominator has a direct effect, so look for the reasons behind the ratio.

**b. Total fixed charge coverage.** Measures how well earnings cover total fixed financial charges, including fixed lease payments and preferred dividends paid out of after-tax earnings.

$$\text{Fixed Charge Coverage} = \frac{\text{Earnings Before Interest, Taxes and Lease Payments}}{\text{Interest Expense} + \text{Lease Payments} + \dfrac{\text{Preferred Dividend}}{1 - \text{Tax Rate}}}$$

### 5.11.4 Operating Efficiency / Activity Ratios

Efficiency ratios measure how productively management uses assets and capital to generate sales. Some measure the speed of converting inventory to sales and receivables to cash, and feed into the **cash conversion cycle** — the period between paying suppliers for inventory and collecting from customers for the inventory sold, which is the period for which short-term financing is needed.

| Ratio | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Total asset turnover** | Sales / **average** total assets | Higher means greater efficiency in using assets to generate sales. Use average assets because sales are generated by possibly different asset amounts over the period |
| **Inventory turnover** | Sales / **average** inventory | Measures how many times a year inventory is sold and restocked. Some analysts prefer cost of goods sold, since inventory is at cost while sales include profit; COGS is often unavailable, so sales is used |
| **Days to sell inventory** | 365 / inventory turnover, or (average inventory) / (sales / 365) | Another way to express inventory turnover |
| **Receivables turnover** | Sales / **average** accounts receivables | How many times, on average, a firm collects its receivables |
| **Collection period** | 365 / receivables turnover, or (average accounts receivables) / (sales / 365) | Average days to collect receivables |

**Total asset turnover** varies substantially across industries: from about **1 for capital-intensive industries** to **over 10 for some retailing operations**. It can be affected by factors other than efficiency: a high ratio relative to the industry might imply too few assets for the potential business (foregone sales), or the use of outdated, fully depreciated assets.

**Worked illustration — SIA total asset turnover.**

| | 2021 | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 3,816 | 15,976 | 16,323 |
| Average total assets | (37,582 + 33,713)/2 | (33,713 + 30,505)/2 | (30,505 + 25,893)/2 |
| **Asset turnover** | **0.11** | **0.50** | **0.58** |

| Sales/assets | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Cathay Pacific | 0.22 | 0.53 |
| IAG | 0.24 | 0.80 |
| United Airlines | 0.27 | 0.85 |
| **SIA (March 2021/2020)** | **0.11** | **0.50** |

The pandemic left many airliners idle rather than generating revenue, so turnover fell across the industry. SIA's turnover is lower than competitors', suggesting room to improve asset use — though keeping older fleets longer would raise turnover and should not be done at the expense of safety and customer experience.

**Inventory turnover** is more applicable to manufacturing and retail and less useful in service industries. A high ratio normally indicates efficiency, but can also indicate inadequate inventory to meet demand, leading to shortages, back orders and eventually lost sales. Too low a ratio relative to the industry may mean excess inventory and possibly obsolete stock.

**Collection period** should be compared with the firm's credit policy and the industry average. If the industry average is **60 days**, a collection period of **70 days** indicates slow-paying customers, tying up capital and raising bad debt risk. A figure substantially below the norm may indicate overly stringent credit terms relative to competitors, which could hurt sales.

### 5.11.5 Profitability Ratios

Profitability is analysed in relation to **sales** or **capital**.

| Ratio | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross profit margin** | Gross profit / sales | Gross profit is profit after deducting cost of goods sold but before selling, general and administrative expenses. Useful for comparing manufacturers, distributorships, supermarkets and other companies with goods for sale |
| **Operating profit margin** | EBIT / sales | Operating profit is gross profit minus selling, general and administrative expenses — earnings before the financing decision and income taxes. Its variability over time indicates **business risk**, the uncertainty of income caused by the industry |
| **Net profit margin** | Net income / sales | Net income is profit after tax but before dividends on preference and ordinary shares |
| **Return on equity (ROE)** | Net income / **average** common equity | The rate of return on shareholders' equity capital |
| **Return on assets (ROA)** | (Pre-tax income + interest) / **average** total assets | Measures overall efficiency in managing assets to generate profits. Normally expressed before interest, to separate operating from financing decisions and allow comparison across different capital structures |

**EBITDA margin.** Some investors add back depreciation and compute an **EBITDA profit margin**, reflecting cash earnings since depreciation and amortisation are non-cash. This can give insight into heavy manufacturing firms with large depreciation charges.

**Worked illustration — SIA profitability.**

| | 2021 | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating profit margin | (4,957 + 268)/3,816 = **−122.89%** | (220 + 221)/15,976 = **0.00%** | (869 + 116)/16,323 = **6.03%** |
| Return on equity | −4,283 / ((16,278 + 9,733)/2) = **−32.94%** | −169 / ((9,733 + 13,683)/2) = **−1.45%** | 722 / ((13,683 + 13,228)/2) = **5.36%** |
| Pre-tax ROA | (4,957 + 268) / ((37,582 + 33,713)/2) = **−13.15%** | (220 + 221) / ((33,713 + 30,505)/2) = **0.00%** | (869 + 116) / ((30,505 + 25,893)/2) = **3.49%** |
| Basic EPS | (115.6) cents | (11.2) cents | 57.7 cents |
| Fully diluted EPS | (115.6) cents | (11.3) cents | 57.4 cents |

**The ROE trap.** ROE numbers must be reviewed carefully to see whether strong performance reflects better earnings or a **depletion of the equity base** in prior years.

| ROE | 2020 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Cathay Pacific | −37.5% | 2.7% |
| IAG | −170.2% | 25.3% |
| United Airlines | −80.8% | 27.9% |
| **SIA (March 2021/2020)** | **−33.9%** | **−1.9%** |

IAG, the merger of British Airways and Iberia, substantially reduced shareholders' equity in **2012** with losses of **EUR0.9 billion** charged to reserves; in **2013** it charged **EUR2 billion** for restatement of merger value to reserves; in **2014** it charged **EUR1.2 billion** in losses under net value in changes of hedges. United Airlines charged its losses to retained earnings in **2011 and 2012**, substantially reducing its equity base. Subsequent ROE for both looks high because equity was reduced by prior losses. Interpreting two years of ROE without reviewing the past gives an incomplete picture.

### 5.11.6 Quality of Financial Statements

The quality of financial statements and earnings depends on good accounting practice, conservative revenue recognition, limited reliance on external debt, understating rather than overstating asset values, and inclusion of all financial liabilities including off-balance-sheet items. Most telling is not how management reports when business is rosy, but the level of transparency and treatment of accounts and losses when conditions are poor and the industry is at its cyclical trough.

Signs of strong, high-quality financial statements:

- **Limited or lower use of debt** relative to the industry, reducing financial risk; lower debt also implies sufficient credit lines to draw on if needed.
- **Higher liquidity** than competitors and the industry.
- **Assets carried below market values.**
- A preference **not to reflect intangible assets** such as patents, goodwill, trademarks or valuable proprietary research, with costs expensed rather than deferred into amortisation schedules.
- **Funds set aside for replacement and upgrades** of plant and factories, with scheduled renewal and technological upgrade plans. Companies using obsolete or inferior technology overstate asset values, because market values are likely below original cost.
- **Conservative revenue recognition** that does not change just to reflect higher sales, conservative accounting of unrealised income by deferring recognition until realised, and adequate provisioning for losses and expenses.
- **Absence of off-balance-sheet activities.** Where such liabilities exist, account for them when estimating the true financial position.
- **Discipline in keeping to core business** and avoiding speculative financial activities.
- **Realistic recognition of bad debts** and adequate provisioning during downturns. A rising average collection period usually means bad debts may increase. A bank with an overly optimistic view of credit quality understates non-performing loans.
- **Steady, sustainable revenue and earnings growth** from existing businesses. Good-quality earnings are realistic and repeatable.

**Worked illustration — SIA's response to the pandemic.** The statements show decisive action to build a more resilient business:

- Raised significant equity capital (**S$8.8 billion**) through a rights issue and mandatory convertible bonds.
- Conserved cash flow by withholding dividends and deferring major capital investment.
- Used part of the capital to reduce borrowings, lowering leverage.
- Kept most of the funding in cash as a war chest against a protracted downturn.
- Ended with a highly liquid and solvent balance sheet, reduced leverage and a substantial war chest for the recovery.
- Achieved this financial resilience at the expense of short-term profitability and asset efficiency.

### 5.11.7 Making Comparisons and Trend Analysis

Beyond industry averages, **trend analysis** — studying past performance over time — gives valuable insight into financial health.

Business cycles affect all companies. To understand performance properly, cover **at least one full cycle** and track performance across it; otherwise the analysis may mislead, showing a company doing well when it is simply at the crest of a cycle about to turn down. Most companies provide a **5-year or 10-year** summary of key statistics in their annual reporting, and analysts also use key operating statistics to forecast and assess performance.

### 5.11.8 Limitations of Ratio Analysis

- **Different accounting policies** across companies require adjustments to make figures comparable.
- **Consolidated accounts** cover groups, making it difficult to determine the performance of one company within the group.
- **Figures may not be representative** of the rest of the year — year-end inventory may be drawn down to abnormally low levels, giving a misleading impression of strength.
- **Special situations** may depress profitability, such as research and development costs for new products.
- **Inflation and general economic conditions** affect trend analysis.
- **Read the footnotes**, which contain additional information on the financial situation.
- **Comparisons require similar companies** in industry sector and size.
- **Ratios can be manipulated** — high ROE from reducing equity in prior years. Look at prior years' accounts to understand the genesis of the numbers.
- **Ratios are only meaningful over time and against peers or benchmarks.** Companies in emerging sectors (emerging technology or business models) do not lend themselves easily to ratio or financial analysis; the lack of comparable, reliable information greatly limits it.
- **Asset-light business models** with little or no hard balance sheet of operating assets, where value rests on brand perception or the founder, are especially hard to analyse by ratio.

## 5.12 Summary

- Company analysis divides into qualitative analysis and quantitative analysis (financial statement analysis).
- Companies establish competitive advantage by dominating and controlling key technology, research and patents; controlling or dominating key resource supply; becoming very cost efficient and the lowest-cost producer; establishing superior brand differentiation; controlling the distribution channel; focusing successfully on niche clients; developing superior management, talent and relationships; and establishing diversified core businesses with shared costs.
- Current asset values and future price movements are driven by expected future sales and earnings, not historical data.
- A company's performance is driven by qualitative factors.
- Excellent companies with sustainable profit growth potential can appear expensive in the near term but usually remain good investments for the longer term.
- The current value of a company depends on future earnings and cash flow, but historical data is still used because recent performance is the best predictor of near-term performance and provides a foundation for forecasts.
- The three major statements are the balance sheet (financial condition at a point in time), the income statement (revenue and expenses over a period) and the statement of changes in cash flow (changes in cash position over a year).
- When examining a balance sheet, focus on the largest items on both sides, the relationship between assets and liabilities, and significant trends over time.
- Some companies hold large cash balances for liquidity, risk and operational needs, but cash not reinvested represents underutilised assets and sacrifices potential returns.
- In downturns, older inventory may become obsolete and hard to sell; excess inventory may require major discounts and write-downs. A steady build-up of inventories with falling sales may indicate a problem.
- For manufacturing, large commitments to plant and factories carry the risk of rapid obsolescence.
- Some technology companies outsource manufacturing to subcontractors while retaining ownership of software, intellectual property and research.
- Falling sales with a build-up in receivables during recessions may signal collection problems and future bad debts.
- Rising receivables alongside rising sales may mean more credit terms offered to distributors and retailers, or more credit and hire purchase sales, which generate additional interest earnings.
- Best practice is to reflect both short- and long-term financial investments at market value.
- For property holdings with large development commitments, examine the funding supporting them; in downturns, properties under development may stay on the books a long time and drain liquidity.
- Significant value locked in physical property can lower the productive use of capital; injecting non-core holdings into REITs is one way to unlock it.
- Goodwill represents a value premium over the fair market value of tangible assets; write-downs hit equity directly and can artificially improve ROE in later periods.
- On the liabilities side, pay attention to the size of debt relative to assets and equity, the funding mix, the currency denomination, and the security and terms.
- Companies with larger debt tend to be those engaged in major infrastructure or property projects, those requiring large asset commitments, and those involved in leveraged mergers and acquisitions.
- Break sales growth into unit sales growth and pricing changes.
- Unit sales growth is driven by new geographical markets, new customer segments, new uses, new product ranges, production capacity, competitors' market share and limits to competition.
- Pricing changes are driven by competition, brand value development, cost of production, buyers' financial health and government pricing restrictions.
- Segmental breakdowns and model forecasts highlight the main sources of revenue and income.
- When reviewing costs, note major cost items as a percentage of sales, significant trends, and non-cash accounting costs.
- The cash flow statement shows real cash earnings from operations and the level of liquidity, in three parts: operating, financing and investing.
- Financial ratios fall into four categories: liquidity, leverage, operating efficiency and profitability.
- The quality of financial statements and earnings depends on good accounting practice, conservative revenue recognition, limited reliance on external debt, understating rather than overstating asset values, and inclusion of all financial liabilities including off-balance-sheet items.
- Financial statement analysis should always use data over several years, say five, so underlying trends can be identified.
- Ratios can be artificially manipulated; analysts should look at prior years' accounts to understand the genesis of the numbers.

## Exam cues

- **Two halves of company analysis:** qualitative and quantitative. Qualitative factors **precede** the numbers.
- **Eight competitive advantages:** technology/patents, resource supply, brand, distribution, cost efficiency, niche focus, management/talent, diversified cores with shared costs.
- **Balance sheet is a snapshot; income statement and cash flow cover a period.**
- **Two balance-sheet equations:** US — assets = liabilities + equity; British — assets − liabilities = equity (net assets).
- **Common size statement** expresses every item as a percentage of total assets (balance sheet) or sales (income statement).
- **Three things to examine on a balance sheet:** largest items on both sides, the asset-liability relationship, and trends over time.
- **Cash:** high cash is a war chest in a downturn but an underutilised asset otherwise. SIA **20.7%** of total assets; American Airlines **0.4%**; IAG's high ratio reflects a shrunken denominator.
- **Receivables:** airlines are **95%+ cash-based**, so receivables are **under 5%** of assets. Falling sales + rising receivables = collection problem.
- **PPE:** capital-intensive industries run **50–65%** of assets. SIA **62.5%**; Apple only **7–8%** because it outsources manufacturing.
- **Property companies:** property is **55–70%** of assets, or **70–85%** including associates and joint ventures. Singapore land rose about **14x** (about **6.8% a year**) from 1976 to 2016.
- **Goodwill write-downs** are charged to equity reserves, lowering the denominators and **raising ROE and ROA** in later years. Baker Hughes wrote down **USD14.77 billion** in 2020.
- **1997 Asian crisis:** USD Eurobonds quadrupled in local currency terms when the Rupiah and Baht fell **more than 75%**.
- **Per-share share counts:** BVPS uses **year-end** shares; EPS uses the **weighted average**; DPS uses shares at the **ex-dividend date**; diluted EPS assumes all convertibles and derivatives are exercised.
- **Liquidity ratios:** current = CA/CL; quick = (cash + marketable securities + AR)/CL; cash = (cash + near-cash)/CL; cash flow = operating cash flow/CL.
- **Current ratio of 2** is the conventional benchmark, but lower ratios are now normal because working capital is managed better.
- **Quick ratio excludes inventory.** A supermarket at **0.2** is fine because its inventory is fast-moving and supplier-financed.
- **Window dressing:** current and quick ratios at reporting dates can be manipulated by collecting receivables early, cutting inventory and delaying purchases.
- **Ratio arithmetic trap:** an equal dollar increase in current assets and current liabilities **lowers** a ratio above 1 and **raises** one below 1.
- **Leverage ratios:** debt/equity, debt/total assets, total liabilities/total assets. Debt is carried at **face value**. Always check for **foreign currency debt**.
- **SIA gearing 0.70** in 2021 versus IAG **11.91** — the lowest in the industry, improved by raising equity, not by repaying debt.
- **Interest coverage = EBIT / interest.** A **negative** ratio's magnitude depends on the interest charge: **−1 is not better than −3** if the −1 firm has higher financing charges.
- **Fixed charge coverage** adds lease payments and preferred dividends (grossed up by dividing by 1 − tax rate) to the denominator.
- **Efficiency ratios use AVERAGE balances** in the denominator because the numerator covers a period.
- **Total asset turnover** ranges from about **1** for capital-intensive industries to **over 10** for some retailers. SIA **0.11** in 2021, **0.50** in 2020.
- **Collection period benchmark:** industry average **60 days**; **70 days** means slow payers.
- **Profitability ratios:** gross margin, operating margin (EBIT/sales), net margin, ROE (net income/average common equity), ROA ((pre-tax income + interest)/average total assets).
- **ROE trap:** high ROE can come from a **depleted equity base**, not better earnings. IAG charged EUR0.9bn (2012), EUR2bn (2013) and EUR1.2bn (2014) to reserves; United charged losses to retained earnings in 2011–2012.
- **Quality of earnings — ten signs:** low debt, high liquidity, assets below market value, few intangibles, funded replacement, conservative revenue recognition, no off-balance-sheet items, core-business discipline, realistic bad-debt provisioning, sustainable growth.
- **Cash flow has three parts:** operating, investing, financing. Depreciation is added back because it is non-cash — it was **63%** of SIA's negative operating cash flow in 2021.
- **Consistently negative operating cash flow** is the bondholder's central concern; asset sales can mask it temporarily.
- **Trend analysis must cover at least one full business cycle**, using the 5- or 10-year summary.
- **Ratio limitations:** differing accounting policies, consolidated accounts, unrepresentative year-end figures, special situations, inflation, footnotes, comparability of size and sector, manipulation, emerging sectors, asset-light models.
- **Trap:** a **higher** current ratio is not automatically better — excess current assets earn low returns.
- **Trap:** **ROA is normally computed before interest** to separate operating from financing decisions; using after-interest ROA across different capital structures is not comparable.
- **Trap:** **depreciation is not a cash outflow.** Stretching the depreciation period (5 years to 10) raises reported profit and changes nothing in cash flow.
