# Chapter 7 — Deposits and Fixed-Income Securities

This is the largest chapter in the syllabus and the most calculation-heavy after Chapter 2. It runs from cash and deposit insurance through money market instruments, bond types and features, credit ratings and spreads, the full set of fixed-income risks, bond pricing, duration and convexity, yield measures, the term structure, and convertible bond analysis.

- Describe various basic money market instruments.
- Describe the characteristics of a fixed-income instrument such as a bond.
- Define and explain the terminology used in fixed-income securities.
- Explain the credit risk in holding bonds.
- Describe the issuers and their motivation in issuing bonds.
- Describe the primary and secondary bond markets.
- Explain how prices are quoted in bond trading.
- Discuss various special features in bond issuance and explain their significance to investors.
- Describe high-yield bonds and their implied bond rating.
- Discuss asset-backed fixed-income instruments.
- Describe features of the internal and external bond markets, and the Eurobond market.
- Explain the differences among the various grades of debt rating by rating agencies.
- State the various risks a fixed-income investor is exposed to.
- Calculate the value of a bond.
- Calculate the bond equivalent yield for bonds paying interest more often than once a year.
- Explain the relationship between coupon rate, market yield, required yield and bond price.
- Explain why bond prices converge to par on maturity.
- Explain duration and convexity and their implications for a bond's price risk.
- Understand price value of a basis point and how it monitors price risk in a bond portfolio.

- Explain reinvestment risk and how it is affected by coupon rate and maturity.

## 7.1 Introduction

This chapter covers the basic interest-bearing asset classes:

- **Cash.**
- **Money market instruments.**
- **Fixed income instruments.**

Cash is interesting because physical cash as a medium of exchange is gradually being replaced by digital forms of storage and disbursement. It is often classified as a non-volatile, safe asset class — and for that reason pays the **lowest return**. Keeping excessive cash from **2008 to 2017** was non-optimal because of opportunity cost and loss of purchasing power.

Beyond cash, money market instruments provide better returns, followed by fixed income instruments of various credit risks and tenures. Most money market instruments are short-term, usually **3–6 months**. For a wider range of tenures, risks, returns and payoff profiles, investors use fixed income instruments — from sovereign notes and bonds to corporate bonds, and from plain vanilla bonds to bonds with embedded options and structured elements.

## 7.2 Cash Deposits and Cash-Equivalents

All investments begin with, and can be liquidated to, cash. During downturns and crises, cash deposit balances rise as other financial assets are sold down. Cash may be in local or foreign currency, and deposits generally provide interest through savings or term deposits, making them the most basic form of fixed income investment.

Because cash provides the lowest return, institutional investors and fund managers generally minimise cash balances, holding only what is needed for redemptions. But if a major downturn is anticipated they may **increase** cash holdings, giving them funds to take advantage of opportunities. Corporate entities must also manage excess cash and liquidity: excess cash needs to be invested to optimise returns, and for very large conglomerates even the short-term cash float must generate a reasonable return before remittance. For some banks, cash management services for corporates have become a lucrative activity.

For individuals, income and monies received fall into four categories:

- Money for **consumption**.
- **Reinvestment** into the business (for entrepreneurs).
- **Reserves** set aside for family needs, life's risks and unfortunate eventualities.
- **Excess cash**.

Reserves and excess cash can be invested in personal assets such as property and in financial assets, or held as cash and cash equivalents for liquidity.

### 7.2.1 Currency Deposits

**a. Local currency deposits.** The most widely known cash-equivalent investments are savings and time deposits at banks and finance companies. **Time deposits** are held for a specified period — 3, 6, 12 months or longer, up to 36 months. The longer the period, the higher the interest, but a portion of interest may be forfeited as a penalty for premature withdrawal. Although low risk, the disadvantage is **low yield**: from **July 2009 to July 2017**, money in savings or fixed deposits accumulated returns of at most **5–6%**, while the **S&P 500 appreciated more than 133%** over the same period, and average Asian housing prices rose significantly.

Time deposits therefore do not provide a good inflation hedge and can be "costly" by forgoing other investments.

**b. Foreign currency deposits.** Foreign currency deposits with Singapore banks can offer potentially higher interest but carry exchange rate risk. In Singapore, foreign currency deposit accounts are **not covered** by the Deposit Insurance Scheme.

Local currency deposits reduce direct exposure to currency fluctuations, but an investor may be better off in foreign currencies when the local currency continually depreciates. If the domestic economy has a poor outlook with weak GDP growth and worsening political stability, local currency weakness can be expected. To mitigate sustained depreciation, investors can hold a portion of deposits in a stable, relatively stronger currency. For growing companies and wealthy individuals, holding part of assets in foreign currency has become integral to diversification and risk management.

**c. Deposit insurance in Singapore.** The DI Scheme was introduced on **1 April 2006** and reviewed and enhanced by MAS and the Singapore Deposit Insurance Corporation (SDIC) in **May 2011**. All full banks and finance companies in Singapore are members, except those exempted by MAS. The scheme protects small depositors if a member fails.

| Coverage limit | Effective |
|---|---|
| S$50,000 | Original |
| **S$75,000** | **1 April 2019** |
| **S$100,000** | **1 April 2024** |

Coverage extends to individuals and other non-bank depositors — sole proprietorships, partnerships, companies, and other unincorporated entities such as charities, associations and societies. It applies **only to Singapore dollar-denominated deposits** with a DI member, including:

- Savings account deposits.
- Fixed deposit account deposits.
- Current account deposits.
- Monies under the CPF Investment Scheme (CPFIS).
- Monies under the CPF Retirement Sum Scheme (CPFRS).
- Monies under the CPF Supplementary Retirement Scheme.
- Murabaha, as prescribed by MAS.

**Not insured** by SDIC:

- Foreign currency deposits.
- Structured deposits.
- Investment products such as unit trusts, shares and other securities.

Monies under CPFIS and CPFRS are aggregated and **separately** insured up to **S$100,000**.

A payout may be made on MAS's decision where a court order has been made to wind up a DI member, or where MAS determines a member is insolvent, unable or likely to become unable to meet its obligations, or about to suspend payments.

**Worked example 1 — individual accounts, one bank.** An individual places **S$650,000** across accounts, all with a single bank.

| Account | Balance (S$) | Insured | Not insured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual current account | 10,000 | | |
| Individual savings account | 10,000 | | |
| Individual fixed deposit account | 280,000 | | |
| **Subtotal, S$ deposits** | **300,000** | **100,000** | **200,000** |
| CPF deposits (CPFIS) | 100,000 | **100,000** | — |
| Individual USD currency investment | 250,000 | — | **250,000** |
| **Total** | **650,000** | **200,000** | **450,000** |

For accounts with a single bank, the scheme covers only up to **S$100,000** of the aggregate balance across the different accounts. CPF deposits invested with the bank receive separate cover of up to **S$100,000**. So although the individual has S$650,000 of deposits, only **S$200,000** is insured.

For deposits above S$100,000, the scheme allows some benefit from **joint accounts** and **multiple accounts**. Partners in a joint account are separately insured for their share.

**Worked example 2 — different banks and joint accounts.** A man and his wife hold accounts with three banks. At Bank A he has **USD250,000** in deposits, **S$30,000** in an individual current account, **S$40,000** in an individual savings account, and **S$80,000** in a joint account with his wife. At Bank B he has **S$100,000** in a joint fixed deposit with his wife and **S$50,000** in a CPFIS deposit. At Bank C he has another **S$100,000** in an individual account. All three banks are SDIC members.

The insured amount is computed **per bank** for S$ deposits, with joint account balances split between the partners, and CPF monies insured separately.

## 7.3 Fixed-Income Securities

Fixed income securities are debt instruments providing periodic payments at an interest rate known in advance, with eventual return of principal at maturity.

| Money market instruments (≤ 1 year) | Capital market instruments (> 1 year) |
|---|---|
| Treasury bills | Government bonds |
| Commercial paper | Agency debts |
| Negotiable certificates of deposit | Municipal and provincial government debts |
| Banker's acceptances | Corporate bonds |
| Repurchase agreements | Convertible bonds |
| Trade bills | Other securities |
| | Asset-backed securities — auto loans, student loans, credit-card debts |
| | Collateralised debt obligations (CDOs) |

One major classification is by **credit risk**, splitting securities into **investment-grade** and **high-yield** bonds. Investors in short-term money market instruments look for low risk, so the majority of those instruments are investment grade.

## 7.4 Money Market Instruments

Money market instruments are debt instruments issued by governments, financial institutions and corporations with maturities of **less than one year**. Because of the short maturity they carry relatively low risk, but the **minimum denomination is usually large — S$250,000 and above**. Examples include Treasury bills, negotiable certificates of deposit or banker's acceptances, commercial paper, repurchase agreements and bills of exchange.

For institutional portfolios, a portion of funds may be placed in money market instruments for **liquidity** — the ability to convert an investment into cash quickly with little or no loss in value. As near-cash instruments, their primary function is to provide a pool of reserves for emergencies or to accumulate funds for specific purposes.

Investment in money market instruments generally **increases** when investors are uncertain about other asset classes, or when stocks and bonds are expected to perform poorly. Holdings fall when a bull market is expected.

Professional fund managers avoid allocating too much beyond liquidity requirements because of the low yield. Although money market instruments carry almost no risk of principal loss, they are subject to **reinvestment risk** — the risk that proceeds cannot be reinvested at equivalent rates, possibly because rates have fallen. Short-term instruments carry **more** reinvestment risk because proceeds must be reinvested each time the instrument matures.

### 7.4.1 Treasury Bills

Short-term government securities with maturities of **one year or less**, issued to the investing public to fund government expenditure. Buying a T-bill is equivalent to lending to a sovereign government.

T-bills issued by a creditworthy government are the safest investments and generally considered **risk-free**. "Risk-free" here means no risk of defaulting on interest or principal when due.

T-bills are sold on a **discount basis**: investors pay less than face value and receive face value at maturity. The difference is the interest earned and is used to calculate the yield. The T-bill yield is usually the benchmark **risk-free rate**. T-bills can be readily sold and converted to cash before maturity at modest cost.

### 7.4.2 Banker's Acceptances and Certificates of Deposit

Both are money market instruments issued by financial institutions.

A **banker's acceptance** facilitates international commercial trade: it represents a claim on the issuing bank for a specific amount on a specific date. It is a **negotiable** security issued on a **discounted** basis.

A **certificate of deposit (CD)** is issued by a bank indicating that a specific sum has been deposited. It carries a maturity date and a specific interest rate, generally on a **compounding** basis, with interest payable at maturity.

### 7.4.3 Commercial Papers

Short-dated **unsecured** promissory notes issued by a corporation. Because they are unsecured, only corporations with **very strong credit ratings** can issue them. Like T-bills and banker's acceptances, commercial paper is issued at a **discount** to face value.

### 7.4.4 Repurchase Agreements

Secondary trading in money market instruments can take place through a **repurchase agreement (repo)**: the sale of a money market instrument with a commitment by the seller to buy it back at a specified price on a future date. Effectively it is a **collateralised short-term loan**, with the money market instrument as collateral. The yield is the difference between the sale and purchase prices.

### 7.4.5 Bills of Exchange

Widely used to finance international trade, important because of the time lag in trade transactions and the distance between buyers and sellers. A bill may be drawn payable **on sight or demand**, or payable after a period — commonly **91 days** — in which case it is a **term bill**. Bills of exchange are **negotiable** and generally transferable by endorsement and delivery.

## 7.5 Bonds

A **coupon bond** is a debt security on which the issuer is obligated to make regular interest payments (coupons) on specified dates and repay principal at maturity. The coupon rate is usually fixed for the life of the bond, giving rise to the term "fixed income".

The debt securities market divides into three maturity segments by original maturity:

- **Money market instruments** — short-term, maturing within one year.
- **Medium-term notes** — more than one year and up to **10 years**.
- **Long-term bonds** — longer than **10 years**.

### 7.5.1 Benefits and Drawbacks of Investing in Bonds

**Benefits.** Bonds suit investors interested in:

- **Principal preservation.**
- **A steady stream of periodic income.**

Fixed income securities have long been regarded as good vehicles for current income: under normal circumstances the investor receives cash flows with reasonable certainty. For capital gains, bond values can appreciate from improvements in the issuer's credit quality or when market yields fall. Active bond traders look for undervalued bonds likely to enjoy credit rating improvements and upgrades.

**Drawbacks.** A major disadvantage of fixed coupons and principal repayment is that these securities **do not adjust for inflation**. Inflation erodes the purchasing power of the cash flows and can lead to swings in interest rates, producing price fluctuations and substantial capital losses if the securities are not held to maturity.

A second disadvantage is the **lack of activity and liquidity** in smaller secondary markets — those other than the major bond markets of the US, Europe and Japan. Investors must be confident of holding relatively illiquid bonds until maturity.

### 7.5.2 Terminology

**Par value (face value) or principal** is the redemption value — the amount repaid at maturity. For most bonds the par value is **$1,000**. Par value is **not** the same as market value, which rises or falls with factors affecting required return, such as interest rate movements.

The **coupon** is the periodic interest payment over the life of the bond. The **coupon rate** multiplied by the principal gives the dollar coupon payment. Coupons are typically paid **annually or semi-annually**.

In Singapore, Government bonds are **scripless**: ownership of Singapore Government Securities is reflected as electronic book entries. Otherwise bonds may be **bearer** or **registered**, but since most markets have moved to electronic trading and settlement, few bearer bonds are issued today, given the high security risk.

**Term-to-maturity** is the number of years remaining to maturity. Two types exist:

- **Term bonds** — a single maturity date; most corporate bonds.
- **Serial bonds** — a series of maturity dates; essentially bundles of bonds issued at the same time with different maturities.

**The longer the maturity, the more volatile the bond price.** Conservative investors choose shorter maturities to reduce interest rate and default risk. Price volatility is not a concern if the bond is held to maturity, but it is if the investor is forced to sell early.

### 7.5.3 Issuers

Bond issues are classified as:

- **Government.**
- **Government-related bodies.**
- **Corporations.**

Governments issue bonds to pay for projects such as infrastructure and essential services like healthcare and education. In some countries, such as the US, government agencies are active issuers. In Singapore, **government-linked corporations** tap the bond market rather than sourcing directly from the government.

Corporations raise funds through bonds as well as equity and bank loans. The corporate sector provides the most diverse issues in type and quality.

## 7.6 Price Quotations

For pricing, fixed income securities are classified as **discount** or **coupon** securities. Securities with maturities of **1 year or less** — T-bills, commercial paper — are discount securities; those with maturities **over one year** are coupon securities.

### 7.6.1 Discount Basis

T-bills are quoted on a **bank discount basis**:

$$Y_d = \frac{D}{F} \times \frac{360}{t}$$

where $$Y_d$$ is the annualised yield on a bank discount basis, $$D$$ is the dollar discount (face value minus price), $$F$$ is the face value, and $$t$$ is the number of days remaining to maturity.

**Worked example.** A T-bill with **90 days** to maturity, face value **$100,000**, selling for **$98,000**:

$$D = 100{,}000 - 98{,}000 = 2{,}000 \qquad Y_d = \frac{2{,}000}{100{,}000} \times \frac{360}{90} = 8.0\%$$

Although widely used, the bank discount yield is **not a meaningful measure of return** from holding a T-bill, because it is calculated on **face value** rather than the actual amount invested.

### 7.6.2 Price Basis

Prices of fixed income securities with maturities over one year are quoted as a **percentage of par value**. With a par value of $1,000, a quote of **98.50** represents **$985**, and **95¾** represents **$957.50**.

US Treasury bonds and notes are quoted in **thirty-seconds (1/32)** of a percentage point, while corporate bonds are quoted in **eighths (1/8)**. A Treasury quote of **90-24** means **90 24/32**.

All fixed income securities trade on an **accrued interest basis**: the buyer pays the price plus interest earned since the last coupon date. For an **8%** bond paying semi-annual interest on 1 June and 1 December, sold on **1 September**, the seller is entitled to accrued interest of **$20** ($80 × 3/12) for the three months held.

## 7.7 Bond Markets

The bond market divides into the **internal bond market** and the **external bond market** (popularly the Eurobond market). The internal market further divides into the **domestic** and **foreign** bond markets.

In the Singapore context, the domestic bond market comprises **Singapore Government Securities (SGS)** and corporate bonds issued by Singapore companies. The foreign bond market refers to foreign issuers who have issued bonds in the Singapore market.

Global bond portfolios invest **40% or more** of assets in foreign bonds. Some follow a conservative approach favouring high-quality developed-market bonds; others own lower-quality bonds from developed or emerging markets. Some invest exclusively outside the US; others invest in both.

### 7.7.1 The Singapore Bond Market

The market comprises Government securities (T-bills and bonds) plus corporate bonds issued by quasi-government statutory organisations, local companies and foreign entities.

**a. Singapore Government Securities.** SGS were initially issued to meet banks' need for a risk-free asset in their liquid asset portfolios. The Singapore Government does not need to finance expenditure through bonds, because it operates a **balanced budget policy** and often enjoys surpluses. SGS issuance therefore serves primarily to:

- Build a **liquid SGS market** to establish a **benchmark yield curve** for pricing Singapore dollar private debt securities.
- Encourage an active **secondary market**, for both cash transactions and derivatives, for risk management.
- Attract domestic and international issuers and investors to the Singapore bond market.

SGS auctions of T-bills and bonds are conducted regularly according to an annual calendar. MAS introduced **"mini-auctions"** of SGS bonds in **2015** as a regular feature to address unexpected instances of strong demand.

**b. Singapore corporate bond market.** Despite challenges in the offshore energy market, which triggered some defaults, and the global slowdown, overall corporate bond issuance rose in **2017**. Corporate debt issued totalled **SGD259 billion**, while total debt outstanding rose to **SGD386 billion**. Several new issues came from **green bonds**, which fund projects delivering environmental benefits.

### 7.7.2 Eurobond Market

The Eurobond market is an international bond market. A **Eurobond** is a bond that is:

- Underwritten by an **international syndicate**.
- Offered at issuance **simultaneously to investors in a number of countries**.
- Issued **outside the jurisdiction of any single country**.

Eurobonds derive their name from originally being sold in Europe, typically London. The market is divided by the currency of denomination: USD-denominated Eurobonds are **Eurodollar bonds**; yen-denominated are **Euroyen bonds**.

### 7.7.3 Primary and Secondary Bond Markets

**a. Primary market.** Bonds are issued to the public in the primary market. SGS are sold at **auctions through Primary Dealers**. A company raising funds appoints an investment bank to help.

**b. Secondary market.** Once issued, bonds trade in the secondary market — either the exchange where the bond is listed (for retail bond issues) or the **OTC market**. Beyond being a place to buy and sell, the secondary market performs the important function of **benchmarking market values and yields** of traded bonds. That information is needed to value bond portfolios and to establish benchmark yield curves for bonds of different credit ratings. Of particular interest is the **spread over the yield on Government issues of similar maturity** — the difference between the corporate bond yield and the Government bond yield.

## 7.8 Types of Bonds

### 7.8.1 Floating Rate Bonds

Instead of a coupon fixed for the bond's life, the coupon on a floating rate security is **periodically reset** against a predetermined benchmark such as **SIBOR**. For example, the coupon may reset every six months to a spread of **100 basis points** over the six-month Treasury bill rate. Floating rate securities appeal to some institutional investors because the income stream more closely matches the floating nature of some of their liabilities.

### 7.8.2 Zero-Coupon Bonds

Zero-coupon bonds make **no periodic coupon payments**. They are sold at prices far below par — at **deep discounts** — and the investor receives par at maturity. The difference between maturity value and purchase price generates the effective interest rate of return.

The most attractive feature is that there is **no reinvestment risk** within the bond's life, because there is no coupon to reinvest, so the rate of return is locked in when held to maturity.

Disadvantages:

- **Call features** potentially limit capital appreciation when rates decline, because issuers are likely to redeem.
- **High price volatility** — a rise in rates hits a zero-coupon bond much harder than a coupon bond. Interim volatility is not a concern if held to maturity.
- **Liquidity may be limited.**

### 7.8.3 Convertible Bonds

Bonds convertible, at the holder's option, into ordinary shares of the issuing company within a specified period. Yields are generally **lower** than comparable straight bonds because of the conversion privilege, which the holder effectively pays for through the lower yield. There is potential capital appreciation from a rise in the underlying share price, a fall in interest rates, or an improvement in the issuer's credit rating.

Convertibles benefit the issuer because they allow **refinancing at a lower rate** and **defer potential dilution**. At issue, the conversion rate is set so the effective price paid per share on conversion is **above** the current share price, making conversion unattractive. If the share price appreciates, conversion may become worthwhile, and the amount converted determines the extent of dilution.

### 7.8.4 Covered Bonds

A covered bond is a **highly rated** paper backed by **bank assets**, typically costing less to issue than unsecured bonds. Covered bonds have a long history in Europe dating to **1769**; though many banks have come and gone, these bonds have **never experienced a default**. Partly for that appeal, and in response to the Global Financial Crisis of 2007–2008, covered bonds are now issued in many countries. MAS released guidelines in **December 2013** allowing local banks to sell covered bonds. A bank in acquisition mode might consider a covered bond offering as an additional, more attractive way to raise funds.

### 7.8.5 High-Yield Bonds

Bonds rated **below BBB** are **high-yield** or **junk** bonds. They trade primarily on their **creditworthiness** rather than the level of interest rates. Their high yields reflect factors such as the small size of the company or a lack of credit history.

Complex junk structures exist, particularly for **leveraged buyout (LBO)** financing and recapitalisations. To reduce heavy interest payments, firms issue bonds with **deferred coupon structures** that let the issuer avoid cash interest for **three to seven years**.

### 7.8.6 Deferred-Coupon Bonds

Four structures:

| Structure | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Deferred-interest bonds** | The most common deferred-coupon structure. Sell at a **deep discount** and pay no interest for an initial period, typically **three to seven years** |
| **Step-up bonds** | Pay coupon interest, but the rate is **low initially** and then steps up to a higher rate |
| **Payment-in-kind (PIK) bonds** | Give the issuer the option to pay cash at a coupon date or give the holder a **similar bond** (same coupon, par equal to the coupon payment). The choice period varies from **five to ten years** |
| **Perpetual bonds** | **No maturity.** Pay a higher coupon to compensate for the indefinite holding period and higher interest rate risk. In a rising rate environment they suffer among the **largest** declines in value. Issuers are not legally obliged to redeem, but there is usually an option to redeem on specific dates, typically **three to five years** after issue. Perpetual bonds rank **before equities** in the order of discharge on default |

## 7.9 Bond Features

Unlike a plain vanilla or straight bond — a basic fixed income security with no special features — some issues carry features that **augment risk**.

### 7.9.1 Call Provisions

A **call provision** gives the **issuer** the right to retire the debt, fully or partially, before maturity. A non-callable bond gives no such right.

The call is attractive to issuers because it gives flexibility to control financing costs if rates fall, and can help circumvent restrictions in the indenture.

It is unattractive to investors, who risk losing a high-coupon bond when rates decline. To protect the investor there may be a **call protection period** after issue. The prospect of a call also **limits upside price potential** in a falling-rate environment. Because of these disadvantages, callable bonds carry **higher yields** than equivalent non-callable bonds.

The **call price** is normally **above par**. The difference between call price and par is the **call premium**, which tends to be **higher early in the bond's life** and declines gradually toward par as maturity approaches.

### 7.9.2 Sinking-Fund Provision

Corporate indentures may require the issuer to retire a certain amount of debt each year. The issuer may buy the bonds in the open market if the price is below par, or pay the trustee, who calls bonds for redemption by **lottery**. The purpose is to **reduce credit risk**.

Some sinking-fund provisions embed options letting the issuer accelerate repayment. The **doubling option** grants the right to retire **twice** the amount the sinking fund requires, supposedly to reduce credit risk.

### 7.9.3 Put Provisions

**Puttable bonds** give the **bondholder** the right to sell the bonds back to the issuer **at par** on designated dates. A put provision **limits downside risk**: when rates rise and the price falls, the investor can put the bond back at par. As with a convertible, the investor has effectively bought a put option from the issuer and pays for it with a **lower yield**.

### 7.9.4 Security for Bonds

The issuer's promises and the investors' rights are specified in a **bond trust deed**. A **trustee** is legally appointed to look after bondholders' interests and ensure all provisions are met, including timely payment of interest and principal.

Failure to pay principal or interest is **legal default**, and court proceedings can enforce the contract. Bondholders, as creditors, have a **priority claim** over ordinary and preferred shareholders on both income and assets.

| Bond type | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Secured (senior) bonds** | Backed by a legal claim on specified property in the event of default — collateral, as for a loan. Bondholders have **first rights** over the asset. **Mortgage bonds** are secured by real estate; others by a dedicated revenue stream such as tolls or utility fees |
| **Debenture** | Any debenture stock, bond, note or other debt security within the meaning of the Securities and Futures Act 2001 |
| **Subordinated (junior) bonds** | Claim on income and assets **subordinated** to secured debt and after main creditors |
| **Income bonds** | Junior debt: the issuer can **omit or delay** interest if earnings are too low |

When an asset is pledged, the **title** is held by the lender until the loan is paid; the borrower still owns and can use the asset, but the documents of ownership are held by the lender.

### 7.9.5 Guarantees and Credit Insurance

Loans and bonds issued by subsidiaries may carry **guarantees from the parent**, adding to credit standing. Where that is lacking, the company can improve credit quality by securing **credit insurance** for a fee.

## 7.10 Asset and Mortgage-Backed Securities

Asset and mortgage-backed securities are created for sale to investors by lenders pooling and securitising illiquid asset cash flows. The primary difference between **asset-backed securities (ABS)** and **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** is the underlying asset: MBS are created from pooling **mortgages**, while ABS evolved from MBS and are created from pooling **non-mortgage** assets.

ABS represent an interest in pooled assets including:

- Auto loans and leases.
- Consumer loans.
- Credit card receivables.
- Royalty payments.

**The sub-prime crisis.** Before the 2007 crisis, the **Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation (Freddie Mac)** and the **Federal National Mortgage Association (Fannie Mae)** issued large amounts of MBS. Numerous mortgages were promoted and approved despite poor credit quality — **sub-prime** loans — because loan officers were rewarded on commission. This lending occurred amid a property bubble building from **2001 to 2007**. Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae purchased and invested in huge numbers of mortgages and MBS with their funding advantage, having **lower capital requirements** than other regulated institutions.

When the bubble burst, mortgage defaults began and MBS fell in value. Several institutions that purchased these loans suffered massive losses and became insolvent. Their default and demise triggered further losses and defaults in other investments, putting banks globally at risk. Defaulting MBS were at the root of the sub-prime crisis in 2007 and the global financial crisis that followed.

**Singapore.** The asset securitisation market was active from its commencement in **2006** but became very quiet after the 2007 crisis. Examples include commercial MBS structured and sold by **DBS Bank**, dealing with pre-sales of properties under construction, where progress payments were securitised to provide construction finance. Another deal, structured and lead-managed by **HSBC for Courts Asia**, used two distinct **special purpose vehicles (SPVs)** — one in Malaysia and one in Singapore — for a multi-currency, multi-jurisdiction transaction, with Courts' loan receivables as the SPV assets.

### 7.10.1 Collateralised Debt Obligations (CDOs)

A CDO is structured by an investment manager or bank that pools cash flow-generating assets (bonds or loans), which are repackaged and sold to investors. Investors purchase the CDOs, typically issued by an **SPV** and secured and backed by the acquired assets.

| CDO type | Backed by |
|---|---|
| **Collateralised bond obligation (CBO)** | A pool of **bonds** |
| **Collateralised loan obligation (CLO)** | A pool of **loans** |

CDOs are effectively a type of asset-backed security. A fund manager looks after a portfolio of assets, and the proceeds from issuing the CDOs finance the acquisition. A CDO is therefore a vehicle for an investment manager to gather assets to generate management fees, or to arbitrage — earning the yield between the return on the assets and the rate paid to CDO investors.

During the sub-prime crisis, **40–50%** of US CDOs were made up of MBS. Those MBS were mixed with other investment grade securities and, with insurance purchased on them, re-sold as CDOs with **higher credit ratings**.

Some banks selling CDOs included **credit default swaps (CDS)** to protect buyers against default. An insurance company found the CDS premiums attractive and began selling CDS to different parties **even for the same MBS** — guaranteeing payment to several parties if the same MBS defaulted. As long as the CDOs and MBS did not default, the insurer earned premiums from multiple investors on the same asset. On default, it was obligated to pay all the CDS buyers, and when defaults occurred in 2007/8 it had to pay **many times over**.

## 7.11 Credit Risk

**Credit risk** or **default risk** is the risk that the issuer cannot make timely principal and interest payments as agreed. Most corporate bonds pay a higher yield than comparable US Treasury securities, which are regarded as free of credit risk.

### 7.11.2 Credit Spreads

A **credit spread** is the rate difference, usually in basis points, between two bonds of the **same maturity** but **different credit quality**. It directly measures the cost of risk — the extra yield investors demand to take on a given default risk. **As the issuer's default risk increases, its bond price falls and its spread widens.**

**Worked example.** A 10-year Treasury at **2%** and a 10-year corporate bond at **4%** means the corporate offers a **200 basis point** spread over the Treasury. A **AAA** investment grade industrial bond traded at roughly a **60 basis point** spread over the 10-year USD Treasury Note.

Comparisons can be made between any bond type and a benchmark yield, with the spread monitored over time. A US Financials bond rated **AA** offered a **higher** yield than a US Industrial bond rated **A** — because since the Global Financial Crisis and the European Sovereign Debt Crisis, financial institutions' credit ratings remain weak. **Credit spreads widen against the benchmark during crises and narrow when conditions and risk appetite improve.**

### 7.11.3 Non-Rated/Unrated Bonds

Some Asian companies choose not to be rated, believing the main investors know their business and history — an Indonesian corporate seeking funding mainly from local institutions familiar with the company, for example. An unrated company is not necessarily of poor credit or afraid of being rated; it is simply **not rated**.

Regulators are generally concerned about unrated debt because of lower transparency and higher investor risk. Local and regional investors are attracted by the **higher yields**, which reflect the higher risk. Some rely on the company's brand or its association with the government; others depend on the credit analysis of the institutions underwriting and marketing the bonds. Those institutions must carry out **due diligence** and ensure no conflicts of interest.

### 7.11.4 Mitigating Poor Credit Quality of the Bonds

Poor credit quality raises the cost of financing. Companies can improve it by:

- Creating a dedicated **sinking fund** setting aside income to sustain interest payments and eventually repay the debt.
- Offering specified assets, such as property, as **collateral or security**.
- Offering **parental guarantees**, backing the issue with the parent's or major equity holder's credit standing.
- Working with a financial institution to **structure and repackage** lower-quality bonds with higher-quality bonds, pooling them as the underlying for a higher-rated security (a CDO).
- Getting a financial institution to offer **credit insurance or credit default swaps**, guaranteeing to cover losses if the issuer defaults.

### 7.11.5 Credit Default Swaps

A CDS is the most commonly used form of credit derivative. In simple terms it is a **contract of insurance** guaranteeing a buyer of debt protection against default. CDS let investors exchange the risk of default for a premium.

- The **buyer** pays a premium to be insured against default.
- The **seller** receives regular payments and must make a **lump-sum payment** (the principal) if default occurs.

CDS were conceived to let financial institutions transfer credit exposure and free up regulatory capital. Besides protecting against default of high-risk bonds, sovereign and corporate debt, investors use CDS to hedge the credit risk of MBS and CDOs. In the bond context, CDS mitigate risk by transferring it from one party to another **without transferring the underlying bond**.

The derivatives market was largely **unregulated and lacked transparency until 2009**, after the Global Financial Crisis. Issuers of CDS saw the market's lucrativeness given the absence of regulation ensuring the seller had the money to pay if the bond defaulted, and there was no clarity on which sellers ultimately held the exposure. Many institutions, including insurers, sold CDS excessively for premiums while heavily **undercapitalised and unhedged**. CDS gave buyers a **false sense of security**, so they bought riskier debt believing they were protected. The system worked while there were no defaults and no expectation that all debts would come due at once.

When US mortgagees began defaulting, MBS could not make payments and defaulted. Institutions that purchased the securities came under distress and some went bankrupt — **Lehman Brothers**. Insurers that sold multiple CDS on the same MBS could not provide the protection they sold — **the bailout of AIG**. The CDS market fell apart overnight.

Today CDS are the engine of the credit derivatives market, with enhanced regulatory structure, letting investors hedge or gain credit exposure and arbitrage.

### 7.11.6 Red Flags for Credit Analysis and Distressed Debts

Financial statements are often released too late and only confirm perceptions. Fluctuations in the value of credit assets tend to precede the release of financials, leaving investors reactive. To manage a debt portfolio proactively, focus on variables that affect current and future revenues and costs **before** they appear in the accounting numbers.

| Category | Red flags |
|---|---|
| **Projected revenue falls** | Sudden, significant and sustained falls in sales or prices from oversupply or structural industry change (crude prices, freight rates); sudden or gradual fall in demand from technological disruption where new competitors lead (Kodak, Nokia); deferment of major contracts, especially where one industry's downturn hits another harder (oil affecting rig building); a non-diversified revenue stream with the loss of major clients |
| **Projected cost rises or stickiness** | Key cost items rise and operating costs spike; revenues fall from industry change but the company cannot cut costs quickly because of inefficiency or inertia |
| **Regulatory restrictions** | New regulation restricts old forms of revenue creation; new regulation drastically raises the cost of doing business, forcing some to exit |
| **Projected operating cash flow falls** | If current and projected operating cash flow stays negative, the only ways to service debt are refinancing or asset sales. Quarterly cash flow tracking should reveal challenges, but investors should act on projections if significantly negative. Cash flow liquidity is the immediate focus for most bondholders |
| **Departure of key officers** | Regular changes in financial controllers, accountants, CEO, COO, CFO, CIO and board members can signal disagreement or concern about how accounts are reflected, or about performance |

Window-dressing of financials is another red flag, but it typically does not allow timely mitigation. A "wait-and-see" approach until the company has difficulty meeting debt and interest obligations is too risky. The primary objective should be to **minimise risk** while still achieving the desired return.

### 7.11.7 Investing in Distressed Debts

A company facing distress may not default. With credit deterioration the debt may be priced below par, but that does not mean the company will fail as a going concern.

At that point, re-examine projected operating cash flow and asset disposals to assess whether the company can meet immediate obligations and whether assets are available to fulfil maturing debt. If it can, with sufficient buffer, the debt may be a worthwhile investment because it is priced at a discount, giving a higher yield.

Some hedge funds, private equity firms and higher-risk-threshold investors see distressed debt as an opportunity. There is potential for recovery and substantial gains, especially where the challenges relate to a **short-term credit squeeze or cyclical downturn** with recovery when key variables normalise. A distressed bond priced at **$0.30–$0.40 on the dollar** may recover a significant portion of value.

If, however, the likelihood of revenue recovery is low — structural industry change — and peripheral asset values are unavailable or insufficient to support the debt, and there is insufficient cash flow to tide over, investors should salvage what they can. Several major institutions sustained massive losses from distressed debt during the Global Financial Crisis in 2008.

## 7.12 Other Sources of Risk

### 7.12.1 Interest Rate Risk

Bond prices are most vulnerable to interest rate changes. There is an **inverse relationship**: as rates rise, bond prices fall, and vice versa. This is also called **delta risk**. For an investor holding to maturity, intermediate volatility is not a concern; for one who plans to sell before maturity, a rate rise means capital loss.

### 7.12.2 Reinvestment Risk

Interest income is usually reinvested, but in a changing rate environment the reinvestment rate is uncertain. There is a risk that the rate at which interim cash flows can be reinvested will **decline**, lowering the eventual return. Reinvestment risk is **greater for higher coupon bonds and longer holding periods**.

Interest rate risk and reinvestment risk make **opposite** predictions. A rate rise pushes the bond price down (the price must fall to give the higher yield, since the coupon is fixed), while the higher rate at which coupons can be reinvested pushes the eventual return up. These are **not fully offsetting**: generally the reinvestment effect only slightly mitigates the interest rate effect.

### 7.12.3 Timing or Call Risk

Callable bonds carry the risk of redemption before maturity. Three disadvantages for the investor:

- Issuers are likely to redeem when rates decline, exposing the investor to **reinvestment risk** at lower yields.
- **Capital appreciation is limited**, because the price will not rise much above the call price.
- The promised **cash flow pattern is not known with certainty**.

### 7.12.4 Inflation or Purchasing Power Risk

Because promised cash flows do not change, rising general prices reduce their real value. Inflation risk is the uncertainty about how much lower the real return will be than the nominal return. It concerns **unanticipated** inflation: if inflation is anticipated and factored into the price, investors are adequately compensated.

### 7.12.5 Marketability or Liquidity Risk

The ease with which a security can be sold at or near fair value in the secondary market. An indication is the **size of the bid-offer spread**: the greater the spread, the greater the marketability risk. For an investor holding to maturity, marketability risk matters less.

### 7.12.6 Exchange Rate or Currency Risk

The SGD cash flows of a foreign currency bond depend on prevailing exchange rates when payments are received. If the foreign currency **depreciates** against the SGD, fewer SGD are received — currency risk. If it **appreciates**, the investor enjoys currency gains.

### 7.12.7 Volatility Risk

The price of a bond with an embedded option depends on the level of interest rates and other factors influencing the option's value, one of which is the **expected volatility of interest rates** — **vega risk**. The value of an option rises when expected rate volatility increases. The risk that a change in volatility adversely affects the price is volatility risk.

### 7.12.8 Political Risk

A government may declare withholding taxes on a bond, declare a tax-exempt bond taxable, or impose capital controls on remittance of funds. These actions can adversely affect value. The possibility of political or legal actions adversely affecting a security's value is **political or legal risk**.

### 7.12.9 Event Risk

Occasionally the issuer's ability to pay interest and principal is seriously impaired by a natural catastrophe, regulatory change, takeover or corporate restructuring. These are **event risks** and result in a rating downgrade.

### 7.12.10 Sector Risk

Sector risk applies only to a particular industry or sector: the stocks of many companies in one sector fall at the same time because of an event affecting the whole industry. Investors can reduce sector risk by **diversifying**.

## 7.13 Bond Valuation

The value of a bond equals the **present value of its expected cash flows** — the periodic interest payments plus the principal repayment at maturity — discounted at the required rate of return.

For annual coupons:

$$P = \frac{C_1}{1+k} + \frac{C_2}{(1+k)^2} + \frac{C_3}{(1+k)^3} + \dots + \frac{C_n}{(1+k)^n} + \frac{M}{(1+k)^n} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+k)^t} + \frac{M}{(1+k)^n}$$

For semi-annual coupons:

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{2n} \frac{C_t}{(1 + k/2)^t} + \frac{M}{(1 + k/2)^{2n}}$$

where $$P$$ is the present value today, $$C_t$$ the coupon payments for period $$t$$, $$M$$ the maturity value, $$n$$ the number of years to maturity, and $$k$$ the required market yield — the yield currently available for a similar security of this maturity.

The discount rate $$k$$ the investor wants is the **required rate of return**. The **required yield** is the yield offered by comparable bonds of the same maturity and credit quality.

**Worked example.** A **10-year** bond with par **$1,000** and coupon rate **8%**, with a required market yield of **6%**.

**a. Annual coupons.**

$$\text{Price} = \frac{80}{1.06} + \frac{80}{1.06^2} + \frac{80}{1.06^3} + \dots + \frac{80}{1.06^{10}} + \frac{1{,}000}{1.06^{10}} = \$1{,}147.20$$

Calculator inputs: **N = 10**, **PMT = 80**, **FV = 1,000**, **I/Y = 6**; compute PV.

**b. Semi-annual coupons.**

$$\text{Price} = \frac{40}{1.03} + \frac{40}{1.03^2} + \dots + \frac{40}{1.03^{20}} + \frac{1{,}000}{1.03^{20}} = \$1{,}148.77$$

The number of periods is **doubled** and the discount rate **halved**.

### 7.13.1 Valuation of a Zero-Coupon Bond

A zero-coupon bond has only the maturity value as a cash flow, so its price is the present value of the maturity amount:

$$P = \frac{M}{(1 + k/2)^{2n}}$$

**Worked example.** A zero-coupon bond maturing in **15 years** with par **$1,000**, at a required return of **9%**:

$$P = \frac{1{,}000}{(1.045)^{30}} = \$267.00$$

Even without coupons, the semi-annual rate of **4.5%** and the half-year period are used, to stay consistent with semi-annual coupon bonds. Calculator inputs: **FV = 1,000**, **N = 30**, **I/Y = 4.5**; compute PV.

## 7.14 Relationship between Bond Price and Market Yield

There is an **inverse relationship**: bond prices move opposite to market yields. The price is the present value of the cash flows, so as yields rise the present value falls with the higher discount rate. Given expectations of a series of yield increases through monetary policy such as Fed hikes, existing bond prices and portfolios fall in value as yields rise. The reverse also holds.

| Required yield | Bond value (% of par) |
|---|---|
| **10%** | **112.46%** |
| **12%** | **100.00%** |
| **14%** | **89.41%** |

This is for a **10-year, 12% semi-annual coupon** bond. As the required yield rises, the bond value declines.

## 7.15 Relationship between Coupon Rate, Market Yield and Price

When a bond is issued, the coupon rate is set at approximately the prevailing market yield. When the market yield **equals** the coupon rate, the price is **par** (100%). If yields rise above the coupon rate, the investor must be compensated through a **lower price** — the bond sells at a **discount**. If yields fall below the coupon rate, the price rises — the bond sells at a **premium**.

- Coupon rate **equals** market yield → bond trades at **par**.
- Coupon rate **lower** than market yield → bond trades at a **discount**.
- Coupon rate **higher** than market yield → bond trades at a **premium**.

## 7.16 Relationship between Market Yield and Other Bond Features

### 7.16.1 Yield and Maturity

The longer the maturity, the greater the impact of a rate change on price, because longer-term cash flows are more sensitive. When yields rise, longer maturities suffer greater price declines; when yields fall, longer maturities rise more.

An investor whose horizon matches the bond's maturity, and who is unconcerned about marking price fluctuations to market, would be **indifferent** to interest rate risk, and would also have the opportunity for capital gains should yields fall. For bond fund managers and traders, however, portfolios reflect gains or losses each year with yield changes, so active management entails adjusting maturity and duration.

### 7.16.2 Yield and Coupons

**Current yield** is the rate of return given the current price; the **coupon rate** is the rate based on par value, usually the price at first issue.

A bond with **smaller coupons** has a larger percentage of its value attributable to the long-term principal repayment. Since longer-term cash flows are more sensitive to rate changes, **bonds with smaller coupons are more severely affected by a rise in yield** than bonds with larger coupons. For two bonds of equal maturity, the one with the **larger coupon** is less affected by rising yields and priced slightly higher.

### 7.16.3 Yield and Callable Features

A call feature gives the issuer the right to redeem when cheaper refinancing is available. When rates fall, the call benefits the issuer, which can refinance at the lower rate without paying market price to call. For the buyer it is a disadvantage, because the buyer is exposed to **reinvestment risk** if the bond is called when rates fall.

## 7.17 Duration and Convexity

### 7.17.1 Duration

Duration and convexity are central to bond portfolio risk management. Duration is affected by **coupon rate, yield and remaining time to maturity**. It is a **linear** measure of the approximate price sensitivity of a bond to **small** interest rate changes — not more than **1% or 100 basis points**.

All else equal, duration is **higher** for bonds with **lower coupons, lower yields and longer maturities**.

**Modified duration** calculates price changes from small yield changes. A portfolio's overall duration is calculated to monitor profit and loss sensitivity to small yield changes. **Portfolio duration is the weighted-average duration** of all bonds in the portfolio.

### 7.17.2 Convexity

Duration is an imperfect measure because it approximates price sensitivity linearly, whereas the price-yield relationship is **convex**.

- With **positive convexity**, duration increases as yields fall. For large rate changes, price **appreciation exceeds** price depreciation. This favours the investor, so a higher-convexity bond has a **higher price** than a lower-convexity bond.
- With **negative convexity**, price appreciation is **less** than price depreciation for large rate changes.

Convexity is directly related to the coupon rate and remaining term: **the lower the coupon, the higher the convexity**. **Zero-coupon bonds have the highest convexity.**

### 7.17.3 Duration and Convexity: The Price-Yield Relationship

Duration and convexity are the two metrics used to assess how a bond's price responds to rate changes, and are the most prevalent measures for quantifying market risk across rate environments.

**Duration** matters when capitalising on rate sensitivity. A higher duration implies greater price movement up or down. Anticipating **falling** rates, a portfolio's duration should be **increased** to maximise sensitivity. Anticipating **rising** rates, duration should be **decreased** to minimise sensitivity.

**Convexity** is the **second derivative** of the price function, measuring the rate of change in duration — the curvature of the price-yield relationship. **Regardless of whether rates are rising or falling, investors should seek greater convexity.**

| Interest rate | More duration | More convexity |
|---|---|---|
| **Rising** | Price falls **more** | Price falls **slower** |
| **Falling** | Price rises **more** | Price rises **faster** |

A more convex bond outperforms a less convex bond **both** when rates rise and when they fall.

### 7.17.4 Present Value of a Basis Point (PVBP or PV01)

PVBP, or PV01, is a risk management tool: the **change in present value of the entire bond portfolio for a one basis point movement in interest rates**. Typically a limit is set for the total PVBP effect, given parallel rate movements of **100, 200 and 300 basis points**.

## 7.18 Time Path of a Bond

Holding all else constant, a bond's price must **converge to par** as maturity approaches, because it will be redeemed at par. Over time the price of a **discount** bond **increases**, and the price of a **premium** bond **declines**. A bond selling at par stays at par as it approaches maturity.

## 7.19 Yield Measures

The most common measures quoted by bond dealers are **current yield** and **yield-to-maturity**.

### 7.19.1 Current Yield

The ratio of the interest payment to the current price:

$$\text{Current Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Interest Payment}}{\text{Bond Price}}$$

**Worked example.** A **10-year, 6%** coupon bond with par **$1,000** priced at **115%** (that is, $1,150):

$$\text{Current Yield} = \frac{60}{1{,}150} = 5.22\%$$

Current yield considers only the annual interest payment and **does not account for capital gain or loss**.

### 7.19.2 Yield-to-Maturity (YTM)

YTM is the rate of return earned if the bond is purchased at a given price and held to maturity. It is the **discount rate that makes the present value of the bond's cash flows equal to its current market value**.

**Worked example.** A price of **$1,148.80** for a **10-year** bond with par **$1,000** and coupon **8%**, paid semi-annually:

$$1{,}148.80 = \frac{40}{1 + k/2} + \frac{40}{(1 + k/2)^2} + \dots + \frac{40}{(1 + k/2)^{20}} + \frac{1{,}000}{(1 + k/2)^{20}}$$

Solved by trial and error, or with a calculator: **N = 20**, **PV = −1,148.80**, **PMT = 40**, **FV = 1,000**; compute I/Y = **3.00%** semi-annually.

Market convention annualises by **doubling** the semi-annual yield, giving **6%** — the **bond-equivalent yield**.

The proper way to annualise is the effective annual yield:

$$\text{Effective annual yield} = (1 + \text{semi-annual rate})^2 - 1 = (1.03)^2 - 1 = 6.09\%$$

**Approximation formula.** Relates net annual effective cash flow to the average amount invested, assuming the price gradually converges to par:

$$\text{Approximate YTM} = \frac{\text{Annual Interest Payment} \pm \text{Amortised Gain or Loss}}{(\text{Current Market Price} + \text{Par Value})/2}$$

For the bond above, the amortised loss per year is (1,148.80 − 1,000)/10 = **$14.88**:

$$\text{Approximate YTM} = \frac{80 - 14.88}{(1{,}148.80 + 1{,}000)/2} = \frac{65.12}{1{,}074.40} = 6.06\%$$

**Zero-coupon YTM.** Substituting zero coupons into the present value model and solving for $$k$$:

$$\frac{k}{2} = \left[ \frac{\text{Maturity Value}}{\text{Price}} \right]^{1/2n} - 1$$

**Worked example.** A **15-year** zero-coupon bond with maturity value **$1,000** selling for **$300**:

$$\frac{k}{2} = \left[ \frac{1{,}000}{300} \right]^{1/30} - 1 = 0.0409 = 4.1\%$$

The number of periods is **30 semi-annual periods**, double the years. The **bond-equivalent yield is 8.2%**.

**YTM versus current yield.** YTM is more meaningful because it considers both coupon income and any capital gain or loss realised on holding to maturity, and it accounts for the timing of cash flows.

The investor realises the stated YTM only if:

- Coupon payments can be **consistently reinvested at the YTM**.
- The bond is **held to maturity**.

The risk that future reinvestment rates differ from the YTM at purchase is **reinvestment risk**. If the bond is not held to maturity, it may be sold at a price different from par and at a different time, producing a return different from the YTM. The risk of selling at a loss because yields have risen is **interest rate risk** or **price risk**.

### 7.19.3 Reinvestment Risk

Maturity and coupon rate affect the degree of reinvestment risk.

- For a given coupon rate, **the longer the maturity, the greater the reinvestment risk**, because more of the total dollar return depends on compounding to realise the YTM.
- For a given maturity, **the higher the coupon rate, the greater the reinvestment risk**, because there is more to reinvest.

A **zero-coupon bond has no reinvestment risk** if held to maturity, because there is nothing to reinvest.

### 7.19.4 Taxable Equivalent Yield (TEY)

Some bonds are tax-exempt, so their stated rate is lower than comparable taxable bonds. To compare, compute the TEY:

$$\text{Taxable Equivalent Yield} = \frac{\text{Tax-Exempt Yield}}{1 - \text{Marginal Tax Rate}}$$

**Worked example.** An investor in the **28%** marginal bracket holds a 5-year tax-exempt bond with a YTM of **7%**:

$$\text{TEY} = \frac{0.07}{1 - 0.28} = 9.7\%$$

## 7.20 Term Structure of Interest Rates

The relationship between yield-to-maturity and term to maturity is the **maturity structure** or **term structure** of interest rates — the **yield curve**.

### 7.20.1 Types of Yield Curves

| Shape | Description | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Rising** (most common) | Yields on short-term issues are low and rise consistently with maturity | Short-term rates are expected to **rise**; the whole curve will move upward |
| **Declining** | Short-term yields are **higher** than long-term yields | Generally indicates **lower** short-term rates in future |
| **Humped** | Intermediate-term yields are higher than short-term, and long-term rates decline below short-term before levelling out | — |
| **Flat** | Short-term and long-term yields are the **same** | — |

### 7.20.2 Theories of Interest Rates

Three theories explain the different yield curve shapes.

**a. Expectations theory.** The shape is determined by investors' **interest rate expectations**. When the market expects rates to rise, lenders prefer to lend short, forcing short-term rates down, while borrowers prefer to borrow long, forcing long-term rates up — creating an **upward-sloping** curve. If the market expects rates to fall, lenders prefer the long end (forcing long rates down) and borrowers the short end (forcing short rates up), producing a **downward-sloping** curve. When the market is ambivalent, the curve tends to be **flatter**.

**Worked example.** A 1-year T-bill yields **6%**, and after one year a new 1-year T-bill is expected to yield **8%**. An investor with a 2-year horizon has two options:

- Invest in 1-year T-bills on a rolling basis, giving an average yield of **7%**: $$[(1.06)(1.08)]^{1/2} - 1 = 0.07$$.
- Invest in a 2-year security, requiring a yield of **7%** — the average of the 6% realised in year one and the 8% expected in year two.

**b. Liquidity preference theory.** Argues the yield curve should **always be upward sloping**, and any other shape is a temporary aberration. Because long-maturity securities are more price-volatile, investors require a **higher return** to hold them. Short-term securities are more easily converted to cash without large price movements, so investors pay a higher price and receive a lower yield. From the lender's perspective, higher yields are required to induce long-term lending because liquidity is sacrificed for longer. This again justifies an upward slope.

**c. Market segmentation theory.** Presumes investors have their own **maturity preferences dictated by the nature of their liabilities**. Life insurance companies with long-term liabilities prefer long-term bonds; banks prefer short-term liquid securities to match deposits. The yield for each maturity is determined **solely by supply and demand in that segment**. Long-term borrowers' and lenders' activities set long-term rates; short-term trades set short-term rates.

## 7.21 Analysis of Convertible Bonds

A **convertible bond** gives the holder the right to convert into a predetermined number of ordinary shares of the **issuer** within a specified period. **Exchangeable bonds** grant the right to exchange for ordinary shares of a firm **other than the issuer**. A major reason for adding the convertible feature is to increase the issue's attractiveness, ensuring a market for it.

Convertibles are issued as part of fundraising. The feature is a **sweetener**, letting the company issue at **lower coupon rates** than comparable straight bonds, lowering financing costs.

**Worked example — Genesis convertible bond.**

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Maturity | 10 years |
| Coupon rate | 4%, paid semi-annually |
| Conversion ratio | **40** (1 bond converts to 40 shares) |
| Par value | $1,000 |
| Market price of the convertible | **$1,100** |
| Market price of the common share | **$24** |
| Annual dividend | **$0.35** per share |

### 7.21.1 Minimum Value of a Convertible Bond

A convertible is a "double" security: a **straight bond** plus a **call option** on the shares. Two values must be considered.

**a. Investment value.** The bond value, also called the **straight value**, is the value without the conversion feature, calculated from the cash flows as if there were no option. It requires the market yield on a comparable straight bond. With a comparable yield of **8%**:

$$\text{Bond Value} = \sum_{t=1}^{20} \frac{C_t}{(1 + k/2)^t} + \frac{\text{Par Value}}{(1 + k/2)^{20}} = \$728.19$$

**b. Conversion value.** The total market value of the shares the bond can be exchanged for:

$$\text{Conversion value} = \text{Conversion Ratio} \times \text{Market Share Price} = 40 \times 24 = \$960$$

**The minimum value of a convertible bond is the greater of its conversion value and its investment value.** With a conversion value of **$960** and an investment value of **$728**, the minimum value is **$960**.

If the bond sold below $960 — say at $728 — arbitrageurs would buy it, convert, and sell the shares at $24, making **$232** per bond. If the investment value were higher than the conversion value, the bond would trade at its investment value; if it traded below, the yield would exceed comparable straight bonds, and investors would bid the price up until yields equalised.

### 7.21.2 Conversion Ratio and Conversion Price

$$\text{Conversion Ratio} = \frac{\text{Par Value of CB}}{\text{Conversion Price}}$$

For Genesis the ratio is **40**, meaning a $1,000 par convertible exchanges for 40 ordinary shares.

$$\text{Conversion Price} = \frac{\text{Par Value of CB}}{\text{Conversion Ratio}} = \frac{1{,}000}{40} = \$25$$

### 7.21.3 Conversion Parity Price

What the investor effectively pays per ordinary share if the convertible is purchased and converted:

$$\text{Conversion Parity Price} = \frac{\text{Market Price of CB}}{\text{Conversion Ratio}} = \frac{1{,}100}{40} = \$27.50$$

The conversion parity price can be considered a **share price breakeven point**. Any further increase in the share price beyond this point produces the same percentage increase in the convertible's value.

### 7.21.4 Conversion Premium

An investor buying the convertible instead of the share pays a premium of **$3.50** ($27.50 − $24), or **14.6%**, over the current share price. The conversion premium is the difference between the convertible's market price and its conversion value, expressed in absolute or percentage terms.

$$\text{Conversion premium (\$)} = \text{Market Value of CB} - \text{Conversion Value} = 1{,}100 - 960 = \$140$$

$$\text{Conversion Premium (\%)} = \frac{\text{Market Value of CB} - \text{Conversion Value}}{\text{Conversion Value}} = \frac{140}{960} = 14.6\%$$

Normally a convertible also trades at a premium to its **straight value**, because it embeds an option equivalent to a call on the share at a strike of **$25** (one $1,000 bond converts to 40 shares). The price differential between the convertible and its straight value is effectively the value of the **embedded call option**. The investor pays the higher price for the potential capital gain if the share appreciates, while downside risk is limited to the straight value.

### 7.21.5 Breakeven Time

Convertibles are often issued with a **current yield higher than the dividend yield** on the shares. Investors compare attractiveness by how quickly the conversion premium is recovered from the income advantage of holding the convertible.

$$\text{Breakeven Time} = \frac{\text{Conversion Premium}}{\text{Bond Income} - \text{Dividend Income}}$$

For Genesis, the conversion premium is **$140**.

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Bond income | 0.04 × $1,000 | **$40** |
| Dividend income | 40 × $0.35 | **$14** |
| Differential | 40 − 14 | **$26** |
| **Breakeven time** | 140 / 26 | **5.38 years** |

Breakeven time ignores the time value of money, but is a useful indicator of relative attractiveness.

### 7.21.6 Downside Risk

The investment value is usually the **floor price** of a convertible. Downside risk is measured by comparing the market price with the investment value:

$$\text{Premium Over Straight Value (\%)} = \frac{\text{Market Price of CB}}{\text{Investment Value}} - 1$$

For Genesis: $1,100 / $728 − 1 = **51%**.

Despite its use in practice, this measure is **flawed** because the straight value changes as the discount rate changes.

### 7.21.7 Investing in Convertible Bonds

Convertibles offer a unique combination of an income stream from a fixed income security and participation in the capital appreciation of the underlying share — **upside potential and downside protection**. The yield is usually **higher than the dividend yield** of the underlying share but **lower than a comparable plain vanilla bond**.

When the share price rises above the conversion strike, the convertible's price is mainly influenced by the share. If the share price in the example rose to **$50**, the bond would have a minimum value of **$2,000**, and conversion would be clearly profitable. When trading at a reasonable conversion premium, the convertible's value is influenced by interest rates, the share price and the share's price volatility. When trading near investment value, the embedded option is almost worthless because the share price is substantially below the strike, and the convertible trades like a straight bond.

## 7.22 Summary

- For individuals, income falls into four categories: consumption, business reinvestment, reserves for family needs and life's risks, and excess cash.
- All full banks and finance companies in Singapore are DI Scheme members except those exempted by MAS. Effective **April 2024** the coverage limit is **S$100,000**, covering individuals, charities, sole proprietorships, partnerships, companies and other unincorporated entities.
- Money market instruments are debt instruments issued by governments, financial institutions and corporations with maturities under one year: T-bills, negotiable CDs or banker's acceptances, commercial paper, repos and bills of exchange.
- A fixed income security provides periodic payments at a rate known in advance, with eventual return of principal at maturity.
- A bond is a fixed income security on which the issuer makes regular interest payments on specified dates and repays principal at maturity.
- The bond market divides into the internal and external bond markets.
- In Singapore, the domestic bond market comprises SGS and corporate bonds issued by Singapore companies; the foreign bond market refers to foreign issuers issuing in Singapore.
- The Eurobond market is divided by the currency of denomination; USD-denominated issues are Eurodollar bonds.
- The secondary market benchmarks market values and yields of traded bonds.
- Special bond types include callable, puttable, convertible, sinking-fund, zero-coupon, floating rate, deferred-coupon and perpetual bonds.
- Convertibles benefit issuers by allowing financing at a lower rate and deferring potential dilution.
- Bonds rated below **BBB** are high-yield or junk bonds.
- ABS may be secured against auto loans and leases, consumer loans, commercial assets and credit card receivables.
- A CDO is structured by an investment manager or bank pooling cash flow-generating assets, repackaged and sold to investors.
- Credit risk is rated primarily by S&P Global, Fitch and Moody's, from **AAA** to **D**.
- Deferred-interest, step-up and payment-in-kind bonds have deferred-coupon structures.
- The credit spread measures the cost of risk; as default risk increases, bond price falls and spread widens.
- A CDS guarantees against bond default and transfers credit exposure. The buyer pays a premium and receives a lump sum on default; the seller receives premiums and pays the insured amount on default.
- Beyond financial ratios, rely on swings in projected revenues, costs and cash flows, regulatory policy and changes in key management.
- Bonds are most vulnerable to credit and interest rate risk. Other risks include reinvestment, call, inflation, liquidity, currency, volatility, political, event and sector risk.
- The value of a bond is the present value of its future cash flows: coupons plus principal for a coupon bond, principal only for a zero-coupon bond.
- There is an inverse relationship between bond prices and required market yield.

## Exam cues

- **Cash pays the lowest return.** From **July 2009 to July 2017**, deposits returned at most **5–6%** while the **S&P 500 rose more than 133%**.
- **Deposit insurance:** introduced **1 April 2006**, enhanced **May 2011**; limit **S$50,000 → S$75,000 (1 April 2019) → S$100,000 (1 April 2024)**. Covers **S$ deposits only** — not foreign currency deposits, structured deposits, unit trusts, shares or other securities. CPFIS and CPFRS monies are aggregated and **separately** insured up to S$100,000.
- **DI worked example:** S$650,000 across accounts at one bank → only **S$200,000** insured (S$100,000 for S$ deposits plus S$100,000 for CPF).
- **Money market instruments:** maturity **under 1 year**, minimum denomination **S$250,000 and above**, low risk but **high reinvestment risk** because of the short maturity.
- **Discount instruments:** T-bills, commercial paper, banker's acceptances. **Interest-bearing:** certificates of deposit.
- **Commercial paper is unsecured**, so only very strong credits can issue it.
- **Repo = collateralised short-term loan.** The yield is the difference between sale and purchase prices.
- **Bill of exchange:** payable on sight or demand, or after a period — commonly **91 days** (a term bill). Negotiable by endorsement and delivery.
- **Maturity segments:** money market **≤ 1 year**; medium-term notes **> 1 to 10 years**; long-term bonds **> 10 years**.
- **Par value is usually $1,000** and is not market value. **Term bonds** have one maturity date; **serial bonds** have several.
- **Bank discount yield:** $$Y_d = (D/F)(360/t)$$. Worked: 90 days, face 100,000, price 98,000 → **8.0%**. It is **not a true return** because it uses face value, not the amount invested.
- **Price quotes:** percentage of par. **98.50 = $985**; **95¾ = $957.50**. Treasuries in **1/32** (90-24 = 90 24/32); corporates in **1/8**.
- **Accrued interest:** an 8% semi-annual bond sold 3 months after the last coupon owes **$20** ($80 × 3/12).
- **SGS exist to build a benchmark yield curve**, not to fund the government, which runs a balanced budget. **Mini-auctions** began in **2015**. Corporate debt issued **SGD259 billion**, outstanding **SGD386 billion** in 2017.
- **Eurobond:** underwritten by an international syndicate, offered simultaneously in several countries, issued outside any single jurisdiction.
- **Zero-coupon bonds have no reinvestment risk** but the **highest price volatility** and **highest convexity**.
- **High-yield = below BBB.** Deferred-coupon structures: deferred-interest (**3–7 years**), step-up, PIK (**5–10 years** choice), perpetual (no maturity, redeemable typically **3–5 years** after issue, ranks **before equities**).
- **Call provision:** issuer's right; call price above par; call premium **higher early**, declining to par. Callable bonds carry **higher yields**.
- **Put provision:** holder's right to sell back **at par**; lowers the yield because the investor has bought a put.
- **Sinking fund** reduces credit risk; the **doubling option** lets the issuer retire twice the required amount.
- **Claim order:** secured (senior) → subordinated (junior) → income bonds; bondholders rank above preference and ordinary shareholders.
- **ABS vs MBS:** MBS pool **mortgages**; ABS pool **non-mortgage** assets. **CBO** = bonds; **CLO** = loans. **40–50%** of US CDOs were MBS before the crisis.
- **Rating scale:** AAA/Aaa down to D. **Investment grade is BBB−/Baa3 and above**; below that is high-yield. **SD** = selective default; **R** = under regulatory supervision; **WR** = withdrawn.
- **S&P grace period rule:** any stated grace period longer than **five business days** is treated as five business days.
- **Credit spread:** same maturity, different credit quality. 10-year Treasury at **2%** vs corporate at **4%** = **200bp**. Spreads **widen in crises** and **narrow** when risk appetite improves.
- **CDS:** buyer pays premium, receives lump sum on default; seller receives premiums, pays on default. **Lehman** failed as a CDS buyer; **AIG** was bailed out as a CDS seller.
- **Distressed debt:** a bond at **$0.30–$0.40 on the dollar** may recover substantially if the problem is a short-term squeeze rather than structural decline.
- **Ten fixed-income risks:** interest rate (delta), reinvestment, timing/call, inflation, marketability/liquidity, currency, volatility (vega), political/legal, event, sector.
- **Interest rate risk and reinvestment risk move in opposite directions** and are **not fully offsetting** — reinvestment only slightly mitigates rate risk.
- **Bond pricing:** $$P = \sum C_t/(1+k)^t + M/(1+k)^n$$. Worked: 10-year, 8% coupon, 6% yield → **$1,147.20** annual, **$1,148.77** semi-annual.
- **Zero-coupon pricing:** $$P = M/(1 + k/2)^{2n}$$. Worked: 15 years, 9%, par 1,000 → **$267.00**.
- **Price-yield table:** 10-year 12% semi-annual bond at 10% yield = **112.46%**; at 12% = **100.00%**; at 14% = **89.41%**.
- **Coupon vs yield vs price:** coupon = yield → **par**; coupon < yield → **discount**; coupon > yield → **premium**.
- **Duration:** higher for **lower coupons, lower yields, longer maturities**. Linear approximation valid only for changes up to **100 basis points**. Portfolio duration is the **weighted average**.
- **Convexity:** lower coupon → higher convexity; **zero-coupon has the highest**. Positive convexity means appreciation exceeds depreciation. **Always prefer more convexity**, whether rates rise or fall.
- **Duration vs convexity table:** rising rates — more duration falls more, more convexity falls slower; falling rates — more duration rises more, more convexity rises faster.
- **PVBP/PV01:** change in portfolio present value for a **one basis point** move; limits set at **100, 200 and 300bp** parallel shifts.
- **Time path:** discount bonds **rise** to par; premium bonds **fall** to par; par bonds stay at par.
- **Current yield:** annual interest / price. Worked: 6% coupon at 115% → **5.22%**. It ignores capital gain or loss.
- **YTM:** the discount rate equating present value to price. Worked: price 1,148.80, 8% semi-annual, 10 years → semi-annual **3.00%**, bond-equivalent **6%**, effective annual **6.09%**.
- **Approximate YTM:** (annual interest ± amortised gain/loss) / ((price + par)/2). Worked: (80 − 14.88)/1,074.40 = **6.06%**.
- **Zero-coupon YTM:** (1,000/300)^(1/30) − 1 = **4.1%** semi-annual, bond-equivalent **8.2%**.
- **YTM is realised only if** coupons are reinvested at the YTM **and** the bond is held to maturity.
- **Reinvestment risk rises with maturity and with coupon.** A zero-coupon bond has **none**.
- **TEY:** tax-exempt yield / (1 − marginal rate). Worked: 7% at 28% → **9.7%**.
- **Yield curve shapes:** rising (most common), declining, humped, flat.
- **Three theories:** expectations (shape reflects rate expectations), liquidity preference (always upward sloping, premium for long maturities), market segmentation (each maturity set by its own supply and demand).
- **Expectations worked example:** 6% now and 8% expected next year → 2-year yield **7%**.
- **Convertible worked example (Genesis):** conversion value **$960**; investment value **$728**; minimum value **$960**; conversion price **$25**; conversion parity price **$27.50**; conversion premium **$140 or 14.6%**; breakeven time **5.38 years**; premium over straight value **51%**.
- **Trap:** the **bank discount yield** overstates the true return because it divides the discount by **face value** rather than the price paid.
- **Trap:** **current yield** ignores capital gain or loss, so it understates the return on a discount bond and overstates it on a premium bond.
- **Trap:** a **negative** convexity bond underperforms in both directions; a **positive** convexity bond outperforms in both.
- **Trap:** the **premium over straight value** is a flawed downside measure because the straight value itself moves with the discount rate.
