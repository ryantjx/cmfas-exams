# Chapter 4 — Foreign Exchange

Foreign exchange is the one asset class every other investment is exposed to, whether or not the investor intends it. This chapter covers what moves exchange rates, how rates are quoted and read, how forward rates are derived from interest differentials, and the four risks an FX position carries.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Describe the features of the foreign exchange market.
- Understand how foreign exchange rates are quoted.
- Understand the difference between direct and indirect quotations and cross quotations.
- Calculate cross rates from indirect quotes in USD against other currencies.
- Understand the bid-offer spread.
- Distinguish between a spot and a forward transaction.
- Explain how forward rates are determined and calculate forward points.
- Explain interest rate parity and covered interest arbitrage.
- Describe non-deliverable forwards and their use.
- Identify the risks in foreign exchange and the methods of managing them.

## 4.1 Introduction

Investments in foreign currencies carry **foreign exchange risk**. Currency values fluctuate daily and can affect international investments significantly: fluctuations may enhance returns through exchange gains or depress them through exchange losses. A representative must therefore understand how exchange rates are determined, how the FX market operates, and how to use it in financial transactions.

Investing in a foreign currency also forces a second decision: **which currency funds the investment?** The investor may borrow in a foreign currency or convert funds from the base currency, and the two choices produce different FX and interest-rate risk exposures.

### 4.1.1 Definition and Terminology

A foreign exchange transaction is the purchase of one currency against the sale of another for settlement or delivery on a specified date. The **exchange rate** is the price per unit of one currency expressed in units of another.

Most FX transactions are **spot** deals, made for delivery in **two working days** (value spot). There is also a market for same-day delivery (**value today**) and next-day delivery (**value tomorrow**, or value tom).

**Base and counter currency.** Currencies are quoted in pairs, for example USD/SGD 1.2520. The first listed currency is the **base currency**; the second is the **counter currency** or **term currency**. In that example USD is the base and SGD the counter, and the quotation means USD 1 = SGD 1.2520.

For an investor, the base currency is the **operating or reference currency**: all profits and losses are measured in it. Holding investments in any other currency is a source of FX risk that must be managed.

**Long and short positions.** FX risk positions are described as long or short.

- **Long** means buying with the expectation that the purchase will rise in value — betting the base currency will strengthen against the term currency.
- **Short** means selling the base currency in the hope its price falls.

If an investor borrowed USD to fund a SGD investment, they sold USD spot for SGD, so the position is **short USD and long SGD**. To close it, they enter the opposite transaction — sell spot SGD and buy USD — and are said to have **squared off** the position, leaving no FX exposure.

## 4.2 Factors Influencing Exchange Rates

Besides interest rates and inflation, the exchange rate is one of the most important determinants of a country's overall economic health. Six leading factors explain exchange rate fluctuations:

- Interest rates.
- Inflation rates.
- Political stability.
- Foreign currency reserves.
- Trade account balances and terms of trade.
- Government or public debt.

### 4.2.1 Interest Rates

Interest rates, inflation and exchange rates are highly correlated. Governments and central banks vary interest rates to manage inflation and currency values. **Higher interest rates offer lenders a higher return relative to other countries, attracting foreign capital inflow, which causes the exchange rate to rise.** The impact may be moderated by higher inflation in that country relative to others, or by other economic and political factors.

### 4.2.2 Inflation Rates

A country with a consistently **lower** inflation rate than others will typically see its currency **appreciate**. High inflation tends to produce **depreciation**.

Inflation can have a severe impact on currency value. IMF data for 2017–2018 showed **Venezuela, South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo** with the three highest inflation rates, all experiencing hyperinflation in 2018. The IMF indicated Venezuela's inflation would have hit **1,000,000% in 2018**. Venezuelans had already converted to USD, gold and even bitcoin as better stores of value, and the Venezuelan Bolivar was considered virtually worthless. Despite launching the New Venezuelan Bolivar in late August 2018, the new currency **fell 95% in value on the day of launch**.

In developed economies, inflation was negligible for most of the decade after the Global Financial Crisis of 2007/2008; several major economies were more concerned about deflation in the years immediately after. With a decade of monetary and fiscal stimulus in the US, Europe and elsewhere, and the recovery of oil prices, inflation returned to the fore.

### 4.2.3 Political Stability

Investors seek stable countries with strong economies. Countries with stable governments executing sound financial and trade policies attract investment, and currencies tend to appreciate when there are strong capital inflows and demand for domestic investments. The opposite happens with corruption, social unrest, frequent changes in government and inconsistent policies, which lead to lost investor confidence and lower currency demand. Political uncertainty and social unrest also drive outflows from residents who feel it safer to hold wealth in more stable foreign currencies.

### 4.2.4 Foreign Currency Reserves

A larger foreign currency reserve does not necessarily lead to appreciation, because some governments do not want their currency to strengthen. But countries with strong reserves are generally better positioned to defend and manage their currency levels than those with weak reserves. During the Asian Currency Crisis, hedge funds and other speculators exploited the vulnerability of Asian currencies with weaker reserves, while countries with strong reserves — **Hong Kong, Singapore and Taiwan** — successfully defended their currencies.

### 4.2.5 Trade Account Balance and Terms of Trade

A **trade deficit** means the country spends more on imports than it receives from exports, so it must acquire more foreign currency to pay for imports, raising demand for foreign currency. A **trade surplus** means export receipts exceed import expenditure, producing a net increase in demand for the domestic currency. All else equal, a trade deficit should weaken demand for the local currency and a trade surplus should strengthen it.

**Terms of trade** is the ratio of export prices to import prices. Terms of trade **improve** when export prices rise faster than import prices, implying higher demand for the currency and appreciation. If export prices rise proportionally less than import prices, demand for the domestic currency falls and the currency depreciates.

Export-oriented countries may find upward pressure on the local currency difficult to manage, and a massive inflow from export-driven surpluses can encourage domestic inflation. To offset this, a government can take money out of the system by investing abroad, moderating the impact on its currency. **China** has invested in US Treasuries and invested abroad so the CNY could strengthen more gradually. **India**, which has run a trade deficit for 20 years, saw its deficit widen further in 2018, and the INR fell about **14% in the first nine months of 2018**.

**US–China trade and foreign exchange.** In 2018 the US government accused major trading partners, particularly China, of enjoying hundreds of billions of dollars of surpluses over the previous decade. The argument was that by keeping the CNY low and not erecting tariff barriers, China artificially made its exports more competitive while limiting imports. To protect US jobs, the US began imposing larger tariff barriers on a wider range of Chinese goods.

### 4.2.6 Government Debt

Government debt affects both interest rates and currency value. Nations with excessive public debt relative to their income-generating capacity and GDP are usually less attractive to foreign investors. Large debt is often needed to increase liquidity and stimulate the economy when tax and other income is insufficient.

When government debt is denominated in **USD**, a fall in the local currency makes the debt harder to repay. If public debt is repaid by **printing money**, the country can experience massive inflation that further devalues the currency, making further local-currency financing even harder.

Sometimes printing money is not even an option. The **Greek public debt crisis** began after the Prime Minister admitted in **2009** that budget deficit figures had been understated for years and could be double those originally reported, triggering rating downgrades. Because Greece is a Eurozone member and had no monetary policy independence, it could not inflate its way out of debt. With default appearing imminent, many foreign investors sold Greek bonds. Foreign investors are less willing to acquire securities in a country whose government is perceived to have high default risk. Greece has needed **three bail-out programmes totalling 300 billion euros**.

## 4.3 Foreign Exchange Markets

In the FX market, financial institutions, commercial banks, business firms, governments and central banks, tourists, speculators and investors buy and sell currencies. Unlike the stock or futures market, the FX market is **not confined to a specific geographical location**. Transactions are made through a complex communication network of computer terminals, telephones, faxes, telexes, cable and communication satellites, and personal devices.

The market works **round the clock**: although markets open and close through a 24-hour trading day, dealing runs continuously, starting in **Sydney, then Tokyo, Singapore/Hong Kong, London, New York and San Francisco**.

## 4.4 Foreign Exchange Rate Quotations

FX rates can be quoted on a **direct**, **indirect** or **cross** basis, and are usually quoted to the **fourth decimal place**. The last digit is a **pip** (= 0.0001), the smallest unit of price movement or quotation for FX, just as a basis point is for interest rates and fixed income.

### 4.4.1 Bid-Offer Spread

A bank or authorised FX market maker always provides **two** prices: the **bid**, at which it will buy a currency, and the **offer** (or ask), at which it will sell.

For **USD/SGD 1.2520-25**, the dealer pays SGD 1.2520 to buy 1 USD and sells 1 USD for SGD 1.2525. The difference, **0.0005 or 5 basis points**, is the spread or profit earned by the bank. The spread varies with market conditions and transaction size: **the greater the uncertainty and volatility, the wider the spread**. The party asking for a quote always **buys at the offer and sells at the bid** — in market parlance, the price is always **against the price taker**.

### 4.4.2 Direct Quotes / European Convention

A **direct quote** expresses the price of one unit of **foreign** currency in units of **domestic** currency: foreign/domestic. Because the USD is the most widely accepted currency, most direct quotes use it as the base:

- USD/CNY 6.6186 — 1 USD = CNY 6.6186
- USD/JPY 101.86 — 1 USD = JPY 101.86
- USD/SGD 1.2520 — 1 USD = SGD 1.2520

### 4.4.3 Indirect Quotes / American Convention

Currencies in the old **sterling bloc** — **GBP, AUD and NZD** — are the base currency when quoted against the USD, which becomes the counter currency. The **EUR** is quoted the same way:

- GBP/USD 1.6816 — 1 GBP = USD 1.6816
- AUD/USD 0.9235 — 1 AUD = USD 0.9235
- NZD/USD 0.8630 — 1 NZD = USD 0.8630
- EUR/USD 1.3622 — 1 EUR = USD 1.3622

An **indirect quote** expresses the price of one unit of **domestic** currency in units of **foreign** currency: domestic/foreign. So GBP/USD, as typically quoted, is an indirect quote **in the UK**.

Whether a quote is direct or indirect therefore depends on context — specifically, which currency is domestic and which is foreign. The indirect quote is simply the **reciprocal** of the direct quote: in Singapore, if the direct quote is USD/SGD 1.2520, the indirect quote is SGD/USD **0.7987** (1/1.2520).

### 4.4.4 Cross Quotes

Most FX transactions involve the USD, but the growing importance of other major currencies has produced direct quotation of two non-USD currencies. A **cross-currency rate**, or **cross rate**, refers to currency pairs that do not involve the USD. The idea is that two exchange rates sharing a common currency allow the rate between the remaining two to be calculated.

**The general rule:** divide the term currency on the same side of the spread by the base currency on the opposite side of the spread. To get the SGD/JPY **bid**, divide the USD/JPY **bid** by the USD/SGD **ask**. To get the SGD/JPY **ask**, divide the USD/JPY **ask** by the USD/SGD **bid**.

| Currency pair | Bid | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| USD/SGD | 1.3362 | 1.3382 |
| USD/JPY | 112.6530 | 112.7330 |
| **SGD/JPY** | **84.1825** | **84.3684** |

- SGD/JPY bid = 112.6530 / 1.3382 = **84.1825**
- SGD/JPY ask = 112.7330 / 1.3362 = **84.3684**

**When the common currency is the term currency in both quotes, multiply on the same side of the spread.** To get the GBP/SGD bid, multiply the GBP/USD bid by the USD/SGD bid; to get the GBP/SGD ask, multiply the GBP/USD ask by the USD/SGD ask.

| Currency pair | Bid | Ask | |
|---|---|---|---|
| GBP/USD | 1.3501 | 1.3510 | GBP is base, USD is term |
| USD/SGD | 1.3362 | 1.3382 | USD is base, SGD is term |
| **GBP/SGD** | **1.8040** | **1.8079** | USD cancels out |

- GBP/SGD bid = 1.3501 × 1.3362 = **1.8040** — the rate at which the market buys GBP and sells SGD.
- GBP/SGD ask = 1.3510 × 1.3382 = **1.8079** — the rate at which the market sells GBP and buys SGD.

The two rules are the same rule seen from different sides: whichever currency is common must cancel, so it is divided out when it is the base of the second quote and multiplied through when it is the term of both.

### 4.4.5 Settlement Dates

A **spot** transaction settles in **two working days**. A **forward** transaction is a contract to exchange one currency for another at a future date later than spot, at a rate determined on the trade date. The purpose of a forward is to establish and fix the cost of exchange for a known FX commitment at a future date.

## 4.5 Forward Foreign Exchange Contracts

An investor enters a forward exchange contract for one of two reasons:

- To **hedge** a currency exposure.
- To **speculate** by taking a position in the currency.

An investor who has bought or sold foreign securities may cover the FX exposure by fixing a forward rate. By locking in a known forward rate, the investor also **forfeits potential gains** from favourable market movements. A single forward transaction of this kind is an **outright**.

Some investors or traders have no initial exposure but hope to profit from a view on the currency's direction: they buy the forward position if the currency is expected to appreciate, and sell if it is expected to depreciate.

### 4.5.1 Forward Rates

A forward transaction has two components:

- The **spot exchange rate**.
- The **interest differential**, expressed in FX rate terms.

The forward rate therefore depends on the movement of the spot rate of the pair and the respective interest rates of the two currencies. The 3-month USD/SGD forward rate depends on the USD/SGD spot rate, the 3-month USD rate and the 3-month SGD rate.

The interest differential is converted into **forward (or swap) points**, which are added to or subtracted from the spot rate:

$$\text{Forward exchange rate} = \text{Spot rate} \pm \text{Forward points}$$

| Forward points | Term | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Added** to spot | Forward **premium** | Forward rate more expensive than spot |
| **Subtracted** from spot | Forward **discount** | Forward rate cheaper than spot |

Generally, the currency of the country with the **higher** interest rate trades at a **forward discount**, and the currency with the **lower** interest rate trades at a **forward premium**.

### 4.5.2 Calculating Forward Points

$$\text{Forward Points} = \frac{S \times (R_c - R_b) \times n/360}{1 + (R_b \times n/360)}$$

where $$S$$ is the spot rate, $$R_c$$ is the annualised interest rate of the **counter** currency, $$R_b$$ is the annualised interest rate of the **base** currency, and $$n$$ is the number of days from the spot date to the forward date.

Sometimes supply or demand for outright forwards is so great that the forward rate does not reflect the interest differential. When that happens there is room for **arbitrage between the money market and the FX market**. Banks also run proprietary trading of the forward FX market, taking positions based on expectations of swap-point movements — the relative movement of the two currencies' interest rates — known as **forex gap trading**.

Forward points reflect **only** relative interest rates. They are **not** an indication of what the future spot rate will actually be.

**Worked example.** Spot USD/SGD = 1.2520, $$R_b = R_{USD} = 1.75\%$$ p.a., $$R_c = R_{SGD} = 0.75\%$$ p.a., $$n = 30$$ days.

$$\text{Forward points} = \frac{1.2520 \times (0.0075 - 0.0175) \times 30/360}{1 + (0.0175 \times 30/360)} = \frac{-0.0010}{1.0015} = -\text{SGD } 0.0010$$

That is **−10 points**, so the 30-day USD/SGD forward is quoted at a **discount** of **USD/SGD 1.2510**. The USD has the higher interest rate, so it trades at a forward discount — consistent with the rule above.

### 4.5.3 Interest Rate Parity

The **interest rate parity** theorem states that, except for small transaction costs, the forward premium or discount should equal the difference in national interest rates for securities of the same maturity. If the relationship is violated, arbitrageurs can make risk-free profits, and their actions force the forward and spot rates back into alignment.

$$F = S \left[ \frac{1 + R_c \left( \frac{n}{360} \right)}{1 + R_b \left( \frac{n}{360} \right)} \right]$$

where $$F$$ is the forward rate, $$S$$ the spot rate, $$R_c$$ the annualised counter-currency rate, $$R_b$$ the annualised base-currency rate, and $$n$$ the number of days.

**Worked example.** With $$R_{USD} = 0.06$$, $$R_{GBP} = 0.05$$ and GBP spot at USD 1.50, the 1-year forward is:

$$F = 1.50 \times \frac{1.06}{1.05} = \text{USD } 1.5143 \text{ per GBP}$$

**Covered interest arbitrage.** Suppose the forward price is quoted at **USD 1.5100** instead of 1.5143 — parity is violated. The arbitrage:

| Step | Action | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Borrow GBP 1,000,000 for 1 year at 5% | Repayment due: **GBP 1,050,000** |
| 2 | Sell the borrowed GBP at spot USD 1.50 | Receive **USD 1,500,000** |
| 3 | Invest the USD for 1 year at 6% | Receive **USD 1,590,000** |
| 4 | Buy GBP 1,050,000 forward at USD 1.5100 | Pay **USD 1,585,500** |
| 5 | Close out: receive USD 1,590,000, pay USD 1,585,500, repay GBP 1,050,000 | **Profit USD 4,500** |

The net position is zero because all transactions close out, but the arbitrageur keeps **USD 4,500**. Effectively they sold GBP at 1.5143 (1,590,000 / 1,050,000) and bought it forward at the lower 1.5100. The whole process — borrowing in one currency, investing in another, and covering the exchange position forward — is **covered interest arbitrage**.

### 4.5.4 Forward Rate Quotes

Most market information services show forward rates not as outright rates but as **margins in points** above or below spot.

| | Spot | 1-month | 2-month |
|---|---|---|---|
| USD/JPY | 119.22/25 | 52/51 | 106/104 |
| AUD/USD | 0.6475/80 | 17/21 | 36/43 |

Forward points often carry no sign. Like spot rates, market makers quote forward points on a bid-offer spread. For a currency at a **forward discount**, the bid (left) is **higher** than the offer (right): the market maker buys at a deeper discount and sells at a lesser discount. For a currency at a **forward premium**, the bid is **lower** than the offer: the maker buys at a lesser premium and sells at a higher premium.

To derive outright rates:

- **Subtract** the points from the corresponding spot rates if the points are **descending** (left higher than right, e.g. 52/51).
- **Add** the points if they are **ascending** (left lower than right, e.g. 17/21).

| | Spot | 1-month | Outright |
|---|---|---|---|
| USD/JPY | 119.22/25 | −52/−51 | **119.2148 / 119.2449** |
| AUD/USD | 0.6475/80 | +17/+21 | **0.6492 / 0.6501** |

Intuitively, to maintain a bid-offer spread the market maker always quotes the **lower-valued** forward point first, followed by the higher. That can only be true if 52/51 are negative, that is −52/−51.

### 4.5.5 Non-Deliverable Forwards

Some countries impose tight capital and exchange controls to reduce currency outflow and prevent exchange-rate volatility. The currency market in those countries often has low liquidity and is inaccessible because of the restrictions. A **non-deliverable forward (NDF)** is a financial instrument commonly used to hedge exposure to currencies offshore that are not internationally traded, or whose trade is limited or legally restricted to the domestic market. The NDF market is an **OTC** market that developed over the last two decades with the growth of emerging economies such as **China, Taiwan, Vietnam and Indonesia**.

An NDF is similar to a regular FX forward, with one key difference: there is **no physical delivery or exchange of the domestic currency** in the offshore market. An NDF is a **cash-settled, short-term forward contract** settled at an agreed forward rate at maturity in a freely traded currency. NDFs are typically quoted with **USD as the reference currency**. The only exchange of cash flows, or calculation of profit and loss, is based on the difference between the agreed (contracted NDF) rate and the spot rate prevailing at settlement, with **no exchange of the notional amount**. The normal tenor ranges from **1 month to a year**.

Besides hedging currency risk in countries with rigid capital controls, NDFs provide arbitrage opportunities and allow speculative positions.

**Worked example.** A Singapore client imports goods from China. The supplier quotes in CNY but requires payment in USD at the prevailing rate. The invoice shows the USD equivalent of **CNY 10 million**, payable in 3 months. Current USD/CNY spot is **6.5845**.

| | Current rate 6.5845 | If USD/CNY rises 5% to 6.9137 | If USD/CNY falls 5% to 6.2553 |
|---|---|---|---|
| CNY 10 million in USD | USD 1,518,718 | USD 1,446,404 | USD 1,598,644 |
| FX gain (loss) | — | FX saving: **USD 72,314** | FX loss: **USD 79,926** |

If the client does nothing, the risk is that the **USD depreciates against the CNY**: at 6.2553 the CNY 10 million would require an additional **USD 79,926** in three months.

To remove the uncertainty, the client enters a **3-month USD/CNY NDF to sell USD (buy CNY)**, fixing USD/CNY at 6.5845 for the CNY 10 million notional. If USD/CNY falls about 5% to 6.2553, the bank pays the client about **USD 79,926**, offsetting the extra amount payable to the supplier. If instead the CNY falls and USD/CNY rises to 6.9137, the client pays the bank **USD 72,314** on the notional loss, offset by the USD 72,314 less needed for the supplier. The NDF fixes the rate at a comfortable level and removes the uncertainty.

## 4.6 Risks in Foreign Exchange

Most FX trading is very short-term, with proprietary trading making profits and losses over periods as brief as minutes. Four risks must be managed:

| Risk | Description |
|---|---|
| **Market risk** | Movement and volatility of FX rates |
| **Price liquidity** | In less-traded currencies, and even in major currencies during an extreme market event such as a major power failure |
| **Credit risk** | The counterparty fails to honour the deal. In FX, credit risk is only a **fraction** of the total transaction amount, because the transaction is an exchange (buy and sell), not an outright extension of credit |
| **Settlement/delivery risk** | The counterparty fails to pay on time. This is a specific counterparty credit risk occurring in the period just before settlement — in FX, **2 days before payment**. **Continuous Linked Settlement (CLS)** has reduced delivery risk significantly |

## 4.7 Managing Foreign Exchange Risks

Several strategies reduce exchange-rate risk. Some — currency intervention and controls — can only be administered by governments or central banks. Besides money market instruments and exchange-traded FX derivatives such as forwards and futures, firms and individuals use options and swaps to hedge risk and exploit opportunities. Futures, options and swaps are covered in the CM-SIP syllabus; this chapter covers currency forwards and the alternative strategies below.

### 4.7.1 Exchange Rate Regimes

An **exchange rate regime** is the policy framework a central bank adopts to manage its currency against other currencies and the FX market. Each country is free to choose a regime aligned with its monetary or fiscal policy. Most choose a fixed rate pegged to a dominant world currency, a floating rate, or a variant in between such as a managed trade-weighted float.

| Regime | Description |
|---|---|
| **Fixed or pegged** | The central bank or government manages its currency against a major international currency. Many currencies are pegged directly to the USD or EUR |
| **Managed peg (soft peg)** | No official fixing, but the government uses another currency as a reference point. It may allow a wider drift up or down against that currency while keeping within a pre-defined limit |
| **Trade-weighted band** (trade-weighted peg or float) | Managed within a trading band based on a trade-weighted basket. Countries preferring a tighter band (China) require more central bank intervention, making the process more like a peg against the trade-weighted benchmark. Where a wider band is allowed (**SGD**), the currency behaves closer to a managed float |
| **Floating** (or managed free float) | The currency floats freely and adjusts to demand and supply with little government interference. Many central banks claim this, but in reality governments often step in by buying or selling reserves to weaken or strengthen the currency when the trend interferes with the economy |

**Other arrangements.**

- **Currency substitution or dollarisation** — a country adopts another nation's currency as legal tender. The adopting country inherits the **credibility** of that currency, but **not** that country's creditworthiness.
- **Monetary (currency) union** — several countries share a common currency (EUR). Such unions are difficult because some countries and governments may be more extravagant than others.
- **Currency board** — an institutional arrangement based on full convertibility of a local currency into a reserve currency, backed by foreign currency reserves (HKD).

**Singapore.** The SGD is managed as a **trade-weighted currency band** based on Singapore's major trading partners and competitors. MAS manages the SGD on a **managed float within an undisclosed currency band**, which provides flexibility for short-term market fluctuations, and the band is reviewed from time to time against economic fundamentals. Under a **Currency Interchangeability Agreement in 1967**, the Brunei Dollar is interchangeable with the Singapore Dollar **at par**, and each is legal tender in the other's country.

**The cost of defending a rate.** It can be costly for a central bank to attain a desired exchange rate. The level of foreign currency reserves, capital outflows, economic fundamentals and market conditions all make it challenging. When the Chinese government moved towards a managed trade-weighted approach in **late 2015**, major currency outflows and pressure on the CNY followed. From **July 2014 to December 2016**, USD/CNY rose only about **11%**, from CNY 6.20 to CNY 6.90, but China ran down its foreign currency reserves by almost **USD 1 trillion (25%)** to defend the CNY.

### 4.7.2 Hedging Currency Risk by Borrowing in Similar Currency

Investors and entrepreneurs often borrow in the currency of the investment to mitigate a fall in that currency. A Singapore investor in UK property would establish borrowings in **GBP**: if GBP weakens, the GBP borrowing is a **natural hedge** against the GBP exposure, and because the investment was not funded in SGD, the impact of the fall is less damaging.

**Worked example.** A Singaporean firm wants to buy a **GBP 1 million** UK property and must borrow either SGD or GBP to fund **80%** of the value. GBP/SGD is **1.8060** now and the GBP depreciates **5% to 1.7157** in a year. Ignoring interest and transaction costs, and assuming the property is sold after a year at the same price:

**If the firm borrows in SGD:**

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Down payment | 0.2 × 1,000,000 × 1.8060 | SGD 361,200 |
| SGD loan | 0.8 × 1,000,000 × 1.8060 | SGD 1,444,800 |
| Sale proceeds after 1 year | 1,000,000 × 1.7157 | SGD 1,715,700 |
| **Total profit/(loss)** | 1,715,700 − 361,200 − 1,444,800 | **−SGD 90,300** |

The firm had to borrow SGD 1,444,800 and find another SGD 361,200 for the down payment, a total outlay of **SGD 1,806,000**, which it converted into GBP 1 million at 1.8060 to buy the house. After a year the house is sold for GBP 1 million, converted into SGD 1,715,700 at 1.7157, and the SGD 1,444,800 loan is repaid.

**If the firm borrows in GBP:**

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Down payment | 0.2 × 1,000,000 × 1.8060 | SGD 361,200 |
| Net proceeds after repaying the GBP 800,000 loan | (1,000,000 − 800,000) × 1.7157 | SGD 343,140 |
| **Total profit/(loss)** | 343,140 − 361,200 | **−SGD 18,060** |

The firm is better off borrowing in **GBP**, because the loss is smaller — **SGD 18,060** against SGD 90,300. The GBP loan hedged the currency exposure.

### 4.7.3 Carry Trades

A **carry trade** borrows at a low interest rate to invest in an asset likely to provide a higher return. A currency carry trade sells a currency with a relatively low interest rate and uses the funds to buy a different currency with higher yields. While paying low interest on the currency borrowed or sold, the investor captures the **interest rate differential**.

The strategy relies on **relative stability in currency prices**, because an adverse exchange-rate movement can wipe out the returns from the interest differential. Where possible, carry trades should be structured on stable currency pairs, or when one is confident of weakness in the borrowing currency. If the invested currency weakens, the investor loses on both the additional interest incurred and the FX loss. Investments become highly speculative when using currencies facing high inflation and weak economies with a history of weakness and devaluation.

**Worked example — borrowing in JPY and investing in AUD, 2008–2016.** A Singapore investor borrows **JPY 100 million** each year and invests in Australian corporate bonds, creating a **350–400+ basis point** spread annually over the Japanese loan rate. The strategy worked in most years but failed in **2008, 2011 and 2015**, when the AUD (the invested currency) fell sharply against the JPY (the borrowed currency).

## 4.8 Summary

- The leading factors explaining exchange-rate fluctuations are interest rates, inflation rates, political stability, foreign currency reserves, trade account balances and terms of trade, and government or public debt.
- Higher interest rates offer lenders a higher return relative to other countries, attracting foreign capital inflow and causing the exchange rate to rise.
- A country with consistently lower inflation than others typically sees its currency appreciate; high inflation tends to produce depreciation.
- Countries with stable governments executing sound financial and trade policies attract investment, and currencies tend to appreciate with strong capital inflows and demand for domestic investments. The opposite happens with corruption, social unrest, frequent government change and inconsistent policy.
- Countries with strong foreign currency reserves are better positioned to defend and manage their currency levels than those with weak reserves.
- If public debt is repaid by printing money, the country can experience massive inflation that further devalues the currency and makes local-currency financing even harder.
- An FX transaction is the purchase of one currency with the simultaneous sale of another for settlement or delivery on a specified date. Rates can be quoted on a direct, indirect or cross basis.
- A spot transaction exchanges one currency for another for settlement usually in two working days. A forward transaction exchanges currencies at a future date at a rate determined on the trade date.
- An investor may enter a forward exchange contract to hedge a currency exposure or to speculate by taking a currency position.
- The major factor determining the forward rate is the difference in interest rates in the two countries over the forward period. The interest differential converts into forward or swap points, added to or subtracted from the spot rate.

## Exam cues

- **Base currency first, counter (term) currency second.** USD/SGD 1.2520 means USD 1 = SGD 1.2520. The base currency is the investor's reference currency, and all profit and loss is measured in it.
- **Pip = 0.0001**, the fourth decimal place — the FX equivalent of a basis point.
- **Bid is where the dealer buys; offer is where the dealer sells.** The price taker always **buys at the offer and sells at the bid**. USD/SGD 1.2520-25 → spread **5 basis points**. Wider spread when volatility and uncertainty rise.
- **Direct quote = foreign/domestic** (USD/SGD in Singapore). **Indirect quote = domestic/foreign** (GBP/USD in the UK). The indirect quote is the **reciprocal** of the direct: 1/1.2520 = **0.7987**.
- **Sterling-bloc currencies and the EUR are quoted as base against the USD:** GBP/USD, AUD/USD, NZD/USD, EUR/USD.
- **Cross-rate rule:** divide the term currency on the same side by the base currency on the **opposite** side. SGD/JPY bid = USD/JPY bid ÷ USD/SGD ask = 112.6530/1.3382 = **84.1825**; ask = 112.7330/1.3362 = **84.3684**.
- **Spot settles T+2.** Value today and value tomorrow also exist.
- **Forward = spot ± forward points.** Points **added** = forward **premium**; **subtracted** = forward **discount**. The **higher-interest-rate currency trades at a forward discount**; the lower-interest-rate currency at a premium.
- **Forward points formula:** $$S(R_c - R_b)(n/360) / [1 + R_b(n/360)]$$. Worked: spot 1.2520, USD 1.75%, SGD 0.75%, 30 days → **−10 points**, forward **1.2510**.
- **Forward points reflect relative interest rates only** — they are not a forecast of the future spot rate.
- **Interest rate parity:** $$F = S[(1 + R_c n/360)/(1 + R_b n/360)]$$. Worked: spot 1.50, USD 6%, GBP 5% → **1.5143**.
- **Covered interest arbitrage:** borrow GBP 1m at 5%, sell spot at 1.50, invest USD at 6%, buy GBP forward at 1.5100 → **profit USD 4,500**.
- **Descending points (52/51) are negative — subtract.** Ascending points (17/21) are positive — add. USD/JPY 119.22/25 with −52/−51 → **119.2148/119.2449**; AUD/USD 0.6475/80 with +17/+21 → **0.6492/0.6501**.
- **NDF:** cash-settled, short-term forward, **no delivery of the domestic currency**, no exchange of notional, USD reference currency, tenor **1 month to 1 year**. Used for currencies with capital controls (China, Taiwan, Vietnam, Indonesia).
- **NDF worked example:** CNY 10m at 6.5845 = USD 1,518,718. At 6.2553 → USD 1,598,644 (**loss USD 79,926**); at 6.9137 → USD 1,446,404 (**saving USD 72,314**).
- **Four FX risks:** market, price liquidity, credit, settlement/delivery. **Credit risk is only a fraction of the transaction amount** because FX is an exchange, not a loan. **Settlement risk arises 2 days before payment**; **CLS** reduces it.
- **Four exchange-rate regimes:** fixed peg, managed soft peg, trade-weighted band, free float. Plus dollarisation, monetary union and currency board.
- **Singapore:** trade-weighted band, managed float within an **undisclosed** band, reviewed periodically. **Brunei–Singapore Currency Interchangeability Agreement 1967 — at par.**
- **China's defence cost:** July 2014–December 2016, USD/CNY rose only about **11%** (6.20 → 6.90) while reserves fell almost **USD 1 trillion (25%)**.
- **Borrowing in the investment currency is a natural hedge.** Worked: GBP 1m property, 80% borrowed, GBP falls 5% from 1.8060 to 1.7157 → loss **SGD 90,300** if borrowing SGD, **SGD 18,060** if borrowing GBP.
- **Carry trade:** borrow low-yield, invest high-yield, capture the differential — but an adverse FX move wipes it out. JPY/AUD 2008–2016: failed in **2008, 2011, 2015**. In 2008 the AUD fell **34.2% vs JPY** and **19% vs SGD**, producing a **SGD 503,946 loss**.
- **Reserve rankings (June 2022):** China 3,480; Japan 1,376; Switzerland 1,033; Russia 630; India 599; Taiwan 548; Hong Kong 504; Saudi Arabia 451; South Korea 449; Singapore 365 (USD billions).
- **BIS 2019:** daily FX turnover **USD6.6 trillion**; FX swaps **USD3.2 trillion** (almost half); spot **USD2 trillion**; USD on one side of **88%** of trades; EUR **32%**; JPY **17%**; EM currencies **25%**; CNY **USD284 billion**, eighth most traded.
- **Trap:** a **direct** quote in one country is an **indirect** quote in another. The label depends on which currency is domestic — never answer without establishing the home currency.
- **Trap:** forward points are **not** a prediction of the future spot rate. They are the interest differential expressed in FX terms.
- **Trap:** a **higher** interest rate currency is at a forward **discount**, not a premium — the higher rate is offset by the cheaper forward rate.
