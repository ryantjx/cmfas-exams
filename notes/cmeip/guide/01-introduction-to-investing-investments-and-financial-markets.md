# Chapter 1 — Introduction to Investing, Investments and Financial Markets

This chapter establishes the vocabulary the rest of the syllabus assumes: who the investor is, what a financial asset is, and how markets are organised. Every later chapter classifies a product against the four investor categories and the four asset classes introduced here.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Understand the definition and needs of different types of investors.
- Describe what makes up the financial markets.
- Define the term "financial assets".
- Distinguish between financial assets and real assets.
- Describe the types of money market securities.
- Describe the types of capital market securities.
- Explain the differences between the primary market and the secondary market.
- Identify the characteristics of a good financial market.
- Distinguish the money market from the capital market.
- Discuss broadly the bond markets, the equity markets, and the options and futures markets.
- Understand the inter-market relationships and how the economy is sustained by the financial markets.

## 1.1 Introduction

The investment environment is changing quickly. Regulatory infrastructure has developed, the mix of promoted products has shifted, and financial technology has widened access to investments while admitting new players who redefine how money, wealth and value are transferred and stored. A representative of a financial institution must keep expanding the breadth of investment knowledge to stay relevant.

Representatives must also be able to identify suitable products and services for the varying needs, expectations, risk profiles, preferences and limitations of their clients, subject to regulatory requirements and industry ethical considerations. Understanding client needs is the basis of every good recommendation, but client needs and return expectations must match what the markets can actually deliver.

### 1.1.1 Definition and Needs of Different Types of Investors

Whether the client is institutional or retail, the first step is to understand their needs before any advice or product recommendation is made. Section 4A of the Securities and Futures Act 2001 (SFA) defines Accredited Investors (AIs), Expert Investors (EIs) and Institutional Investors (IIs); anyone outside those definitions is a Retail Investor.

| Category | Statutory basis | Why the classification matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Institutional Investor (II)** | SFA s 4A: government, statutory boards, central banks, banks, insurers, CMS licensees, approved exchanges and clearing houses, pension funds, CIS, and similar prescribed entities | Sophisticated, structured, and permitted the widest product access |
| **Accredited Investor (AI)** | SFA s 4A: an individual meeting the wealth or income tests, or a corporation, trust, partnership or joint account meeting the prescribed tests | Presumed able to bear more risk; may access unrated bonds and products needing neither a prospectus nor a reasonable basis for recommendation |
| **Expert Investor (EI)** | SFA s 4A: a person whose business involves acquiring, disposing of or holding capital markets products | Presumed to understand product risk through occupation, not wealth |
| **Retail Investor** | Everyone not in the three categories above | Receives the highest level of protective regulation |

**Accredited Investor thresholds.** An individual qualifies as an AI on any one of three tests:

- **Net personal assets** exceeding **S$2 million**. The primary residence is counted net of any outstanding secured credit facility and is **capped at S$1 million**.
- **Financial assets** (bank deposits, investment products and other prescribed assets, net of liabilities) exceeding **S$1 million**.
- **Income** in the preceding 12 months of not less than **S$300,000**.

A corporation or other entity qualifies with net assets exceeding **S$10 million**, as determined by its most recent audited balance sheet, or by a balance sheet it certifies as giving a true and fair view, dated within the preceding 12 months. A partnership (other than an LLP) qualifies when each partner is an AI; a corporation qualifies when its entire share capital is owned by one or more AIs; and a person holding a joint account with an AI qualifies in respect of dealings through that joint account.

**The AI opt-in regime.** MAS raised the qualifying benchmark for AIs and introduced an opt-in regime: a capital markets intermediary (CMI) can no longer automatically treat anyone who qualifies as an AI unless the investor opts to be treated as one. An AI may also elect to benefit from the full range of retail safeguards.

**Institutional Investors.** IIs are "super-investors" by size and market impact. Their capital and resources give them timely, wide access to market information and professional research. They generally have clearly defined investment goals with prescribed directives and explicit requirements covering costs, execution efficiency, research and reporting support, volatility and return ranges, and asset-allocation, concentration and exposure limits. Because they are sophisticated and act within an institutional structure, they are less likely to make uneducated investments and are typically permitted wider product access than other investor types.

IIs are commonly classified by the purpose for which the money is invested:

| Type of II | Purpose of the funds |
|---|---|
| Pension funds | Providing retirement income |
| Insurance funds | Meeting expected claims |
| Endowment funds | Satisfying philanthropic spending commitments |
| Sovereign wealth funds | Stabilising the national economy, or saving for future generations |

**Expert Investors.** An EI trades capital markets products in the ordinary course of business. Like AIs, EIs receive less regulatory protection than retail investors, but for a different reason: not greater wealth, but occupational understanding of product risk. An EI is effectively a sub-set of retail investors meeting specific experience requirements in the SFA, so the same factors generally influence their decisions. Two differences matter: market rumours that move retail investors may not move EIs, and EIs tend to have a clearer idea of their objectives and constraints and may prefer greater involvement in the investment process.

**Retail Investors.** A retail investor buys securities for a personal account rather than for an organisation. Retail investors are the opposite of IIs: they trade in small amounts, trade less frequently, and have little influence over corporate decisions or the capital markets. Lacking access to boardrooms or timely market information, they are assumed to be less financially savvy and more risk-averse than AIs and IIs, and are therefore given more protective regulation.

Three regulatory developments shape how retail investors are treated in Singapore:

- More stringent regulation of non-conventional products such as precious metal buy-back arrangements, and the regulation of collectively managed investment schemes as Collective Investment Schemes (CIS).
- Refinement of investor classifications under the SFA and the Financial Advisers Act 2001 (FAA).
- An option for AIs to benefit from the full range of retail safeguards.

Most factors influencing a retail investor's decision coincide with those of an individual AI, because an AI is a sub-set of retail investors meeting specific wealth and income requirements.

**Factors influencing investment decisions.** The factors differ between individual and corporate investors.

| Individual | Corporate |
|---|---|
| Demographics (age, gender, education level) | Management outlook (growth vs decline) |
| Lifestyle (confidence level, number of dependants) | Size of firm |
| Risk tolerance (volatility range, risk-return spectrum) | Corporate governance — balancing vested interests against management control while safeguarding all stakeholders |
| Market trends and forecasts | Market trends and forecasts |
| Tax (higher brackets prefer tax-exempt returns) | Tax (fiscal incentives or barriers on asset classes) |
| Investment horizon | Investment horizon |
| Liquidity | Cash flow |
| Psychological influences (desires, prejudices, emotions) | Corporate culture (aggressive vs passive) |
| Personal values (social or religious values shaping attitude to certain asset classes) | Corporate values |

**Client profiling.** A representative maps the client against a risk-and-return grid. The standard return expectations quoted in the guide are:

| Objective | Expected return |
|---|---|
| Preserve wealth and purchasing power | 2–4% |
| Income with some growth | 4–6% |
| Steady balanced growth | 6–8% |
| Aggressive growth | 8–12% |

The profile also records investment knowledge (limited and low, average and occasional, experienced and regular, sophisticated and high), risk tolerance (low, medium, high), investment horizon (less than 1 year, 2–5 years, more than 5 years), the type of service sought (discretionary management, advisory, or transaction ideas and execution), product preferences (equities, fixed income, funds, derivatives), and the amount to invest (less than S$2 million, S$2–10 million, S$10–50 million, more than S$50 million).

### 1.1.2 Investment Products and Services

Products range from those actively traded and aimed mainly at IIs to those designed for AIs, high net worth individuals (HNWIs) or retail investors. Most products built from traditional asset classes — deposits, foreign currency, fixed income and equity — seek to provide yield and/or capital gains. Financial innovation also lets investors take a bearish position using put options, futures and inverse exchange-traded funds (ETFs) to generate potentially higher yields.

### 1.1.3 Financial Assets and Real Assets from the Individual Investor's Perspective

Beyond financial assets, individuals may own real physical assets and business assets whose values and cash flows can be significantly larger than their financial assets. For individuals the flow of funds between business, physical and financial assets is closely related.

Business owners have business financing needs and require corporate finance products and services as well as cash management. Business salaries and profits provide income that can be invested in financial assets or ploughed back into the business. Purchases of real physical assets such as real estate require financing and can be funded from financial assets. A representative must therefore be alert to business needs, changes and decisions about business assets and physical assets, because those can have a major impact on financial assets.

| Financial assets | Real, physical and business assets |
|---|---|
| **Traditional asset classes:** cash deposits, foreign currencies, short-term money market instruments, bonds and other fixed income instruments, equities, insurance | **Commodities:** energy (crude oil, LNG, refined products, petrochemicals, coal, electricity), metals (precious, base, ferrous), agriculture (grain, softs, livestock, biofuel, dairy, forestry) |
| **Derivatives:** exchange-traded derivatives, financial futures, options, swaps, warrants, OTC structured products | **Real estate:** commercial, residential, industrial, agricultural |
| **Investment funds:** ETFs, private funds sold OTC, pure asset class vs diversified funds, strategy funds, capital guaranteed funds | **Businesses:** privately owned plant, property, equipment, rights, patents, technology, network, distribution |
| **Alternative investments:** hedge funds, commodity funds, REITs and real estate funds, private equity funds | **Precious stones and jewellery:** diamonds, rubies, emeralds |
| | **Collectibles:** art, antiques, wine, limited-edition memorabilia |

## 1.2 Financial Assets

Financial assets are paper or electronic claims on an issuer. The issuer may be from the public sector — a government such as the Singapore Government, or an agency such as the Housing and Development Board — a supranational such as the World Bank, or the private sector, such as a corporation. Financial assets are broadly classified into debt, equity and derivative instruments or securities.

### 1.2.1 Local and Foreign Cash Deposits

The most basic asset class is cash: Singapore dollar deposits in current, savings or fixed (time) deposit accounts, plus foreign currency deposit accounts. In a low interest rate environment, holding investible funds in cash carries a high opportunity cost, because inflation erodes purchasing power. Foreign currency deposits carry currency exchange risk and have been relatively volatile since the global financial crisis.

### 1.2.2 Fixed Income Securities

Fixed income securities are primarily interest-bearing instruments providing a fixed return. They include treasury bills, fixed-rate bonds, certificates of deposit, commercial papers and debentures.

**Short-term money market securities.** Money market instruments are relatively low-risk, highly liquid, short-term debt instruments issued by governments, financial institutions and corporations. Minimum denominations are relatively large and transaction sizes can be substantial. Maturities range from **1 day to 1 year**, and are often **less than 90 days**.

| Instrument | What it is | Key examinable feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Treasury bill** | Issued by a central bank on behalf of the government to raise short-term funds | Obligation of the government, so treated as free of default; its return is the proxy for the **risk-free rate**. Issued on a **discounted basis by tender**, payable or redeemable at **face value** at maturity |
| **Repurchase agreement (repo)** | Sale of a security at a specified price with a simultaneous commitment by the seller (borrower) to buy it back at a specified price and future date | A form of **borrowing** for the seller. The effective interest is the difference between the purchase and sale prices |
| **Reverse repo** | The converse: purchase of a security by the lending party combined with a forward sale back to the borrowing party | A form of **lending** |
| **Commercial paper** | Short-term **unsecured** promissory note issued by a corporation | A **discounted** money market instrument, like a treasury bill |
| **Banker's acceptance** | A time draft drawn on a bank by a customer, which the bank agrees to pay on a specified future date | Created to facilitate commercial trade, particularly international trade; **negotiable**, so the holder can discount it in the money market |
| **Certificate of deposit (CD)** | Certificate issued by a bank in exchange for a deposit placed with it | Yields are quoted on an **interest-bearing** basis, not a discount basis |

**Debt securities.** Debt securities are long-term fixed borrowings by issuers. The investor lends a principal to the issuer, which agrees to make periodic interest payments and repay the principal at maturity. Debt securities are also called bonds. Singapore has one of the most developed bond markets in Asia, offering Singapore Government securities, statutory board securities, supranational bonds and corporate bonds.

| Bond label | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Eurobond** | An international bond denominated in a currency **not native** to the country where it is issued |
| **Eurodollar bond** | Denominated in US dollars and sold **outside the United States** to non-US investors |
| **Euroyen bond** | Denominated in yen and sold outside Japan — for example, a Japanese company issuing Euroyen bonds for sale in London |
| **Yankee bond** | Sold **in the United States**, denominated in US dollars, but issued by a **foreign** corporation or government |
| **Domestic bond** | Sold by an issuer **within its own country** in that country's currency |

### 1.2.3 Equity Securities

Equity securities represent an ownership interest in a company. There are two forms:

- **Ordinary shares.** Ordinary shareholders elect the directors and vote on company issues, usually with votes equal to the number of shares owned.
- **Preference shares.** Technically equity, but also a **hybrid** security: voting rights are limited (they can vote only in limited circumstances) and the instrument resembles both equity and fixed income.

Priority of claim on the company's assets runs **creditors first, then preference shareholders, then ordinary shareholders**, who hold the residual claim.

### 1.2.4 Derivatives

Derivatives derive their value from the price of an underlying asset such as a stock, a bond or a market index. They include options, warrants, futures and convertible securities.

| Instrument | Definition | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Structured product** | A note or deposit bundled with one or more derivatives | Return is linked to the performance of one or more underlyings |
| **Option** | Gives the buyer the right to buy (call) or sell (put) a specified amount of an underlying within a specified period at a specified price | A right, not an obligation |
| **Company-issued warrant** | Right to buy a given number of the company's shares at a specified price over a given period | Usually runs for **several years** |
| **Structured warrant** | Issued usually by banks; lets investors profit from upward (call warrant) or downward (put warrant) price performance of an underlying | Costs a **fraction** of the underlying's price |
| **Futures contract** | Agreement for future delivery of an asset between buyer and seller at a predetermined price | **Exchange-traded** with **standardised terms**; may be cash settled or physically settled |
| **Convertible security** | Holder may convert into a predetermined number of ordinary shares within a specified period | Pays interest at a **lower yield** than the same issuer's plain corporate bond; the difference is the value of the embedded share option |

### 1.2.5 Portfolio Management and Investment Funds

Clients may invest directly in single securities or entrust funds to portfolio managers. Professional management benefits clients who lack the time, information, expertise, discipline or emotional temperament to invest actively.

Different asset classes and securities are pooled and managed by professional fund managers, either as pure asset funds or diversified under asset allocation strategies matched to client risk profiles and objectives. Hedge fund managers offer alternative approaches that seek returns irrespective of market direction. Funds are offered on stock exchanges worldwide as ETFs, and over-the-counter (OTC) by financial institutions.

## 1.3 Financial Markets

Financial markets are where assets are bought or sold, borrowed or lent, or otherwise transacted among issuers, investors, speculators, hedgers and intermediaries. Investments may be made in equity securities (shares, equity unit trusts) and debt securities (short-term money market paper, long-term fixed income bonds); sophisticated investors may use derivatives such as equity options and futures. The resulting markets include the equities market, money market, fixed income market, commodities market, options and futures market, and foreign exchange market.

These markets have seen extensive liberalisation and global consolidation, the impact of information technology enabling alternative platforms such as Electronic Communications Networks (for example Instinet), and rapid adoption of online technology letting professional and retail investors use brokerage services through computer networks and personal devices.

Technology also enables **algorithmic trading**, which uses a computer program following a defined set of instructions to execute a trade and can slice large trades to minimise market impact. A form of algorithmic trading is **high frequency trading**, which relies on computer speed to analyse large amounts of market information and self-execute trades in fractions of a second.

**Structure of the financial markets.** Four asset-class pillars sit under the markets:

| Debt securities | Equity securities | Derivatives | Foreign exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money market, fixed income | Ordinary shares, preference shares | Options, futures, swaps, structured products | Spots, forwards |

**Participants in financial markets:**

- **Investors** — retail, private bank and institutional investors, including fund management companies.
- **Issuers** — public and private sector companies.
- **Intermediaries** — broker/dealer firms and market makers.
- **Regulators.**
- **Electronic exchanges** — for equities, debt, derivatives and commodities.
- **Infrastructure providers** — clearing houses, central depositories, communications networks, trade repositories.
- **Financial institutions** — distribution and underwriting of initial public offerings (IPOs), and participation in the secondary markets.

**How markets sustain economic growth.** Capital seekers (issuers of stocks and bonds) pay through arrangers and intermediaries (investment banks, underwriters, distributors, brokerages) to reach wholesale capital providers (banks, hedge and pension funds, insurance companies), who in turn serve retail capital providers (individuals, small firms).

Beyond regulated exchange-based capital markets there is growing activity in private equity and mezzanine financing, where capital seekers and providers negotiate funding for mergers, acquisitions or buy-outs. In these pre-primary market sectors, capital is raised from private investors rather than from the public. Some forms are referred to as "shadow banking".

### 1.3.1 Primary Market

The primary market is where new issues of securities are listed on the stock exchange and sold to the public. New sales of debt or equity take place through IPOs. The **issuer receives the cash** from subscribers, who receive financial claims that previously did not exist.

### 1.3.2 Secondary Market

Once new securities have been sold in the primary market they are listed and begin trading in the secondary market, which involves buying and selling securities initially sold in the primary market. Proceeds from a secondary-market sale go to the **selling investor, not the original issuer**. Secondary markets provide the liquidity essential to the proper functioning of capital markets.

### 1.3.3 Characteristics of a Good Financial Market

| Characteristic | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **Availability of information** | Timely, accurate information on the volume and prices of past transactions and on all currently outstanding bids and offers, so participants can determine an appropriate price |
| **Liquidity** | Ability to buy or sell quickly at prices not substantially different from previous transactions, absent new information. **Marketability** (likelihood of a quick sale) is necessary but not sufficient; **price continuity** (prices do not change much between transactions) is the other component. A continuous market requires **depth** — numerous buyers and sellers willing to trade at current prices |
| **Transaction costs** | Low across all aspects: the cost of reaching the market, brokerage, and transferring the security. This attribute is **internal efficiency** |
| **Bid-offer spread** | Narrow, reflecting wide and deep market-making where market makers readily quote two-way prices |
| **Information efficiency** | Prices adjust quickly to new information on supply or demand, so prices reflect all available information |
| **Price discovery** | The most important function of any financial market: with the other characteristics in place, prices become transparent and responsive to market forces |

### 1.3.4 Bond Markets

The largest domestic bond market in the world is the **US** bond market. The US Treasury issues notes and bonds with maturities greater than **1 year**, and is the largest single issuer of debt globally, making the treasury market the most active and liquid in the world. Treasury bills, notes and bonds are usually issued through an **auction** where dealers submit competitive bids.

The **Eurobond** market remains important for investors seeking foreign currency bonds, particularly while quantitative easing held interest rates very low and pushed investors into long-term issues. Because participation is worldwide, dealing and settlement procedures are governed by the rules of the **Association of International Bond Dealers (AIBD)**.

Although many corporate bonds are listed on major exchanges such as the NYSE, bond trading is mostly **OTC through bond dealers**, which are mostly banks and large investment banking firms.

### 1.3.5 Equity Markets

Ordinary shares, preference shares, warrants and ETFs trade actively on equity markets. Equity markets differ across countries in size, liquidity, degree of regulation, trading and settlement systems, and restrictions on foreign ownership. Internet brokerage has made equity investment more international.

Size and structure facts to remember:

- The **NYSE and NASDAQ combined** account for slightly more than **half** of world stock market capitalisation.
- Singapore is **not** in the top twenty global exchanges by market capitalisation, but **SGX** is the world's most liquid offshore market for the benchmark equity indices of **China, India, Japan and ASEAN**, and offers commodity and currency derivative products.
- **Japan has 8 stock exchanges**, with the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) dominating.
- The US has regional exchanges alongside the NYSE and the American Stock Exchange (AMEX), listing small companies with geographic interest.

**Settlement periods** vary by market:

| Market | Settlement |
|---|---|
| China | **T+0** — settlement on the trade date, on the philosophy that the investor must have funds on hand |
| United States | **T+2** |
| Singapore | **T+2** (SGX ST Rule 9.2.1: 2 exchange business days after the trade day) |
| Hong Kong | **T+2** |

Securities not listed on an organised exchange may trade in the **OTC market**. In the US this is a negotiated market of dealers or market makers standing ready to buy and sell at market prices. Unlike brokers, **dealers have a vested interest in transactions and earn the spread** between bid and offer.

### 1.3.6 Options and Futures Markets

A futures exchange is a marketplace for financial instruments and commodities where buying and selling of underlying products is contracted for future delivery.

| Exchange | Position |
|---|---|
| **Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) Group** | Largest futures exchange; processes **3 billion contracts** of diverse derivatives worth approximately **USD1 quadrillion annually** on average |
| **Intercontinental Exchange (ICE)** | Second largest, created through mergers and acquisitions among exchanges and clearing houses mainly in the US and Europe |
| **SGX** | Operates the **largest derivatives market in Asia**, benefiting from Singapore's position as the largest foreign exchange centre in Asia; offers futures on all the major Asian equity indices |
| **Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited** | Owns the London Metal Exchange in addition to its stock and futures exchange |

Futures trade on electronic exchanges, with trades cleared through the clearing house each business day. The clearing house lets participants reverse a position before maturity because it tracks each participant's outstanding contracts.

US options trade on an exchange floor such as the **Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE)** using a system of market makers who quote bid and ask prices, with floor brokers trading either with the market maker or with other floor brokers. The **Options Clearing Corporation (OCC)** acts as intermediary between the brokers representing buyers and sellers (writers): it becomes **the buyer for every seller and the seller for every buyer**, guaranteeing that all contract obligations are met.

## 1.4 Digital Disruption and Fintech

The financial industry has undergone significant technological change and digital disruption. With internet access, the roles of financial institutions and capital markets are being challenged by new entrants, and banks work with fintech companies to design equivalent internet platforms and solutions.

| Digital development | Impact on the financial sector |
|---|---|
| **Internet banking** | Consumer and branch banking redefined; tasks split between what can be done online and what needs face-to-face interaction; fewer brick-and-mortar branches; roles and activities redefined |
| **Rise in non-bank internet lending** | New non-bank entrants provide direct financing over the internet; big-data credit analysis, profiling and direct access let them compete with banks, finance companies and traditional moneylenders |
| **Internet business transactions reducing cash float** | Transactions that took days to clear through the banking system now clear directly, reducing the daily float of cash |
| **Non-bank digital wallets bypassing banks** | Excess cash moves from current and savings accounts into digital wallets (for example Tencent, Alibaba); platform providers now recommend investment funds, bypassing retail banks offering lower deposit rates |
| **Digital currencies for transactions in cyberspace** | Cryptocurrencies are not backed by reserves or central banks, are authenticated through blockchain, bypass the banking system and provide some anonymity. They are **not recognised as legal tender** and make up only about **1% of currency transactions**; Bitcoin futures trade on the CME and CBOE |

## 1.5 Summary

- Representatives must distinguish institutional, accredited, expert and retail investors in the local context and identify their investment needs.
- Financial assets are broadly classified as debt securities, equity securities, collective investment schemes, funds or derivatives.
- Money market instruments are highly liquid, short-term debt instruments with maturities of less than one year.
- Fixed income debt securities are borrowings by issuers who agree to make periodic interest payments and return principal at maturity.
- Equity securities represent ownership interest in a corporation; the two types are ordinary shares and preference shares.
- Derivative securities derive their value from underlying assets; options, warrants, futures and convertible bonds are examples.
- Investors may invest directly in single securities or entrust funds to portfolio managers.
- The primary market is where new securities are distributed by the issuer to investors; the secondary market is where those securities then trade.
- In evaluating the quality of a financial market, consider availability of information, liquidity, transaction cost, the bid-offer spread, and informational and price-discovery efficiency.
- Bond trading is mostly over-the-counter through bond dealers rather than through organised exchanges.
- Equity markets differ across countries in size, liquidity, degree of regulation, trading and settlement systems, and restrictions on foreign ownership.
- The financial sector and capital markets are going through a digital revolution, upgrading traditional functions and admitting new entrants and joint ventures with financial institutions.

## Exam cues

- **Four investor categories, one statutory source.** SFA **s 4A** defines II, AI and EI; everyone else is retail. Retail gets the most protection; II gets the widest product access.
- **AI thresholds — memorise all three individual tests:** net personal assets **> S$2m** (primary residence net of secured credit, **capped at S$1m**), financial assets **> S$1m** net of liabilities, or income in the preceding **12 months ≥ S$300k**. Corporate AI: net assets **> S$10m** on the latest audited balance sheet, dated within the preceding 12 months.
- **AI opt-in.** A CMI cannot automatically treat a qualifying investor as an AI; the investor must opt in, and may instead elect full retail safeguards.
- **EI is about occupation, not wealth.** An EI trades capital markets products in the ordinary course of business — a sub-set of retail investors meeting experience requirements.
- **Money market vs capital market.** Money market: maturity **1 day to 1 year**, often **< 90 days**, large denominations, low risk, highly liquid. Capital market: **> 1 year** (bonds, equities).
- **Discount vs interest-bearing.** Treasury bills, commercial paper and banker's acceptances are **discounted** instruments; certificates of deposit are quoted on an **interest-bearing** basis.
- **Repo vs reverse repo.** A repo is **borrowing** by the seller of the security; a reverse repo is **lending** by the buyer. The effective interest is the difference between the purchase and sale prices.
- **Bond labels turn on two questions:** which currency, and where sold. Eurobond = currency not native to the place of issue; Eurodollar = USD sold outside the US; Yankee = USD sold **in** the US by a foreign issuer; domestic = issuer's own country and currency.
- **Claim priority:** creditors → preference shareholders → ordinary shareholders (residual).
- **Primary vs secondary.** Primary: issuer receives the cash, new claims are created. Secondary: the selling investor receives the cash, and the market supplies liquidity.
- **Six characteristics of a good market:** information availability, liquidity (marketability + price continuity + depth), low transaction costs (internal efficiency), narrow bid-offer spread, information efficiency, price discovery.
- **Settlement:** China **T+0**; US, Singapore and Hong Kong **T+2**.
- **Clearing house roles:** futures clearing house tracks outstanding contracts so positions can be reversed; the **OCC** becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer.
- **Trap:** a **dealer** earns the bid-offer spread and has a vested interest in the transaction; a **broker** acts as agent for a commission. Do not use the terms interchangeably.
- **Trap:** preference shares are **equity** in law but behave like debt in priority and payout — the examination tests the hybrid character, not a simple label.
- **Trap:** the AI net-personal-assets test counts the primary residence **net of the secured loan and capped at S$1m**, not at market value.
