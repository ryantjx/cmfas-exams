# Chapter 3 — Key Drivers of Market Movements and Asset Values

Fair value comes from fundamental analysis; market price comes from three interacting forces. This chapter separates those forces — fundamental, market-driven and behavioural — and gives the frameworks the examination tests: fiscal and monetary policy, the business cycle, the three classes of economic indicator, the industry life cycle, Porter's five forces, and the behavioural biases that move prices away from value.

## Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

- Identify and explain the key drivers of asset values and market movement.
- Describe and explain how key economic factors affect market movement and asset values.
- Follow the economic calendar and explain the three main classes of economic indicators — leading, coincident and lagging.
- Discuss how industry and company-specific factors affect market value.
- Describe how market factors affect asset prices.
- Describe how investment behaviour influences investment decisions, market movements and asset prices.

## 3.1 Introduction to Market Movements and Asset Values

An analyst assessing asset values typically calculates an **intrinsic or fair value** from fundamental analysis using a set of assumptions and parameters, then compares it with the market value. Fair-value assessments and recommendations often differ across analysts because the assumptions and projections differ, and perceptions of fair value vary among investors because they have different horizons and objectives.

**Market price** is influenced by many factors, ultimately resolving into the price agreed for the asset given the supply of the security and its demand at a point in time. Three groups of influence act on it:

- **Fundamental factors** — economic, industry and company-specific factors affecting prospects and value.
- **Technical factors** — investment behaviour and sentiment.
- **Market-driven factors** — market structure, trading rules and parameters, the type of participants, and the flow of funds and assets available at any one time.

**The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)**, postulated by Eugene Fama, contends that markets are efficient and asset prices adjust rapidly to reflect new publicly available information. On that view an asset's price **is** its fair value and no individual can consistently outperform the market. Yet periods of information inefficiency appear to exist, producing under- or over-valuation over extended periods. Investors have also been found to react irrationally because of cognitive biases such as overconfidence, emotional over-reaction, information bias and other predictable errors in reasoning and information processing. For astute and alert investors, those periods of under- and over-valuation are the opportunity.

### 3.1.1 Factors Driving Asset Values

Three primary factors influence asset values and market movement:

| Factor group | What it covers | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental factors** | Economic factors, industry factors, company factors | The rational, logical explanation of how an asset's worth is reflected in its price |
| **Market-driven factors** | Liquidity and funds flow, new securities and IPOs, derivatives influence, market structure | The rational, logical explanation of how an asset's worth is reflected in its price |
| **Investment behaviour** | Herd instinct (greed and fear), loss aversion, cognitive bias | How emotion and market sentiment move the price |

Market participants must be aware of all three and incorporate them when analysing and making sound investment decisions.

## 3.2 Fundamental Factors and Analysis

Fundamental analysis attempts to understand and estimate the fair value of an asset by examining:

- **Macroeconomic factors** — fiscal and monetary policy.
- **Industry-specific factors** — the industry life cycle and competitive structure.
- **Firm-specific factors** — business strategy and financial condition.

### 3.2.1 Economic Analysis

Expectations about the future health of an economy have direct and significant effects on its financial markets, because economic data reflects the composite performance of industries and companies and forms the basis for projections. When investors expect steady growth, stock markets are typically bullish; expectations of significant slowdown or the threat of recession usually trigger corrections and bearish conditions.

Investment instruments are affected by fiscal and monetary policy. Policy changes affect interest rates, currency, inflation, employment, consumption and corporate profits, and **expectations** of policy changes also move asset prices. The investment community therefore tracks leading economic indicators to gauge the significance of policy changes and their impact on different investments and industries, and looks to invest in the securities likely to benefit most.

Economic analysis also drives allocation. In **top-down asset allocation**, a larger proportion of funds goes to developed economies, growing sectors and industries, and the leading corporate performers within them — those with stronger financials and better earnings-growth potential.

### 3.2.2 Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy is the use of **government expenditure and taxation** to influence aggregate demand and supply. Changes in government spending on infrastructure influence the general economy.

Money from fiscal expenditure passes through the economy and eventually reaches consumers, producing a **multiplier effect**. Greater construction and infrastructure spending raises demand for construction materials, equipment, services and labour; wages and profits rise in those industries, creating higher disposable incomes; that triggers further consumption of goods and services, benefiting suppliers in other industries; and with healthier consumption, private capital expenditure rises too, adding to the expansion. The initial round of spending ripples through different industries.

Governments can also cut taxes, creating higher profits and disposable income, which triggers greater consumption and stimulus. To fund the initial spending, governments may run a **budget deficit**. Excess spending is funded by drawing down surpluses from prior years or by issuing government bonds. Most governments ran higher debt to support fiscal stimulus immediately after the 2008 Global Financial Crisis; **US federal government debt as a percentage of GDP was at its highest in 2017**, back to post-World War II levels, when heavy stimulus was needed.

### 3.2.3 Impact of Fiscal Measures on Gross Domestic Product

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the most widely used measure of economic performance. It measures the market value of goods and services produced in the domestic economy during a specific period, usually one year. Four components are considered:

$$GDP = C + I + G + NE$$

where $$C$$ is consumption expenditure by individuals on goods and services, $$I$$ is private investment in capital goods (capital expenditure for replacing equipment and expanding the supply of capital assets), $$G$$ is government spending (both investment and consumption, including education, defence, capital investment, infrastructure and health services), and $$NE$$ is net exports — total exports less total imports, which may be positive or negative. When a country sells more to foreigners than it buys, net exports are positive.

To evaluate how fiscal policy affects the economy, consider how it affects each component. With the intent of sustaining a higher GDP growth target of **3–4%** compared with prior periods averaging **2.2% (1998–2017)**, the US government adopted a more aggressive expansionary stance:

| Measure | GDP component affected |
|---|---|
| More infrastructure development projects | Raises $$G$$ |
| Tax incentives for US companies to relocate factory operations back to the US | Raises $$I$$ |
| Lowering the personal income tax rate | Raises $$C$$ |
| Tariffs on selected foreign goods, making them less attractive relative to US-made goods | Intended to improve $$NE$$, traditionally negative |
| Increasing job creation and retention to raise the employment rate | Increases $$C$$ |

Expectations that the US government would follow through led to a major rally after the November 2016 election, and in **2017 the S&P 500 returned 21%**. Successful near-term stimulus can create future problems, however — the threat of inflation and higher budget deficits.

### 3.2.4 Monetary Policy

Monetary policy tools stimulate or cool the economy by increasing or decreasing liquidity and/or by decreasing or increasing the cost of funding for short-term liquidity and bank reserves.

| Stance | Mechanism | Effect on asset values |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary** | Expand money supply, lower interest rates, increase aggregate demand | Lower deposit rates push funds into equity and real estate, driving up asset values; lower borrowing costs encourage spending |
| **Contractionary** | Decrease money supply, raise interest rates, contain inflation | Higher borrowing costs discourage spending and borrowing, reducing asset demand and lowering asset values; can also cut production and employment, lowering disposable income and further discouraging spending |

For the US economy, an important facet is the rate at which the Federal Reserve allows money supply to grow. Studies of US monetary history show that **declines in the growth rate of money supply have preceded business contractions**, while increases have preceded expansions. The Fed influences money supply through three channels:

- **Open Market Operations (OMOs)** — the central bank buys and sells government securities.
- **The Fed Funds Rate** — the rate at which banks lend excess reserves to one another so banks with shortfalls can meet reserve requirements.
- **The discount window** — a lending facility extended to eligible financial institutions to meet temporary shortfalls in available credit caused by short-term illiquidity.

Of the three, the Fed has relied heavily on **OMOs** as its key method for influencing market liquidity, using them extensively to pump liquidity into the market in 2008. This type of liquidity change **first affects bond prices, then common stocks, and finally the aggregate economy**.

**Quantitative easing.** The 2008 crisis produced the greatest recession since the US Great Depression of the 1930s. To stimulate consumption, employment and growth, the US, UK and European central banks embarked on quantitative easing (QE) programmes, producing a protracted period of very low interest rates (**2009–2017**). Asian central banks also kept rates low to increase domestic liquidity.

The Fed openly tied QE to unemployment. Rates were held at a low of **0.25% from December 2008 until December 2016**, then raised by **25 basis points** at a time as employment and job creation improved. With US unemployment reaching **3.8% in May 2018** — the lowest in 48 years — and core inflation reaching the Federal target of **2.0%**, the Fed signalled continued normalisation and balance-sheet reduction. The Fed Funds Rate was raised to **2.25% in September 2018**.

When the economy overheats, inflation control becomes the focus. Central banks contain inflationary pressure and excessive capacity build-up for fear that unsustainable growth and eventual correction cause a hard landing with major unemployment, destruction of asset values and social hardship. They then adopt a contractionary policy to moderate growth, triggering consolidation and engineering a **soft landing**.

**a. Singapore's trade-weighted exchange rate index.** Because its economy is open, Singapore's monetary regime centres on managing the exchange rate, which has a major influence on local inflation and international competitiveness. MAS conducts policy by managing a **trade-weighted exchange rate index**. Money supply is largely driven from within and has only limited impact on Singapore's economic activity. Independent policy targets cannot be set for both exchange rates and interest rates, because one affects the other, so Singapore has **no independent policy targets for interest rates and money supply**.

**b. Singapore's exchange-rate-based monetary policy.** Since **1981**, monetary policy in Singapore has centred on exchange-rate management, with price stability as the basis for sustainable growth. The exchange rate is an ideal intermediate target for a small, open economy: it is relatively controllable through direct intervention in the foreign exchange market and bears a stable, predictable relationship with price stability over the medium term.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Basket** | The Singapore dollar is managed against a basket of currencies of major trading partners and competitors, weighted by each country's importance to Singapore's trade. The basket composition is revised periodically to reflect changes in trade patterns |
| **Managed float** | The trade-weighted exchange rate may fluctuate within a **policy band**, whose level and direction are announced **semi-annually**. The band accommodates short-term FX fluctuations and gives flexibility |
| **Review cycle** | The policy band is reviewed periodically — typically every **six months** — to stay consistent with fundamentals and avoid currency misalignment. A **Monetary Policy Statement (MPS)** is released after each review, with an accompanying **Macroeconomic Review** |
| **Interest rates** | Choosing the exchange rate as the intermediate target means MAS gives up control of domestic interest rates and money supply. With free capital movement, Singapore rates are largely determined by foreign rates and expectations of future SGD movement; domestic rates have typically been **below US rates**, reflecting expectations of gradual SGD appreciation |

The exchange rate has been an effective anti-inflation tool: domestic inflation averaged **1.9% per annum from 1981 to 2010**. The system's success owes much to strong fundamentals — prudent fiscal policy, flexible product and factor markets, a sound financial system and a robust corporate sector.

**c. Money supply, credit creation and required reserves.** Money supply also increases through credit creation when reserve requirements are lowered. A bank takes a new deposit and lends out 90%, keeping 10% as statutory reserves; the process repeats through other banks, producing a **10-fold increase** in money supply.

| Round | New deposit | New investment (loan) | New reserves |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 10,000 | 9,000 | 1,000 |
| 2nd | 9,000 | 8,100 | 900 |
| 3rd | 8,100 | 7,290 | 810 |
| 4th | 7,290 | 6,561 | 729 |
| … | … | … | … |
| **After all rounds** | **100,000** | **90,000** | **10,000** |

When the economy overheats, the central bank raises required reserves, indirectly reducing money supply and liquidity. Lowering required reserves expands money supply and multiplies lending. China applies this actively: with US–China tariff trade wars looming and a slowdown expected, the Chinese central bank **lowered the Reserve Requirement Ratio three times in the first half of 2018** to increase liquidity and lending.

**d. Quantity theory of money.** When money supply increases, central banks fear that excess liquidity causes asset inflation. The quantity theory of money states a direct proportional relationship between the amount of money in the economy and the price of goods and services — too much money in the system causes inflation. Fisher's **equation of exchange**:

$$M \times V = P \times Q$$

where $$M$$ is the stock of money (currency outside banks plus demand deposits), $$V$$ is the velocity of money circulation (the number of times per period an average unit of money changes hands), $$P$$ is the general price level or a price index, and $$Q$$ is the number of transactions in an economy during a year.

When the equation is fully operative, the central bank cannot increase output by increasing money supply: as $$M$$ rises, $$P$$ rises (more money chasing goods, causing inflation), and velocity $$V$$ — which is socially determined, decided by consumers — then determines output $$Q$$.

**e. Inflation, interest rates and asset values.**

**Interest rates and bond prices.** Lower interest rates mean lower discount rates, giving higher fair-value targets. When rates rise, higher discount rates applied to bond cash flows mean lower intrinsic bond values. Because interest-rate inflection points tend to coincide with business-cycle peaks, **the highest bond yields tend to occur close to the peak of the business cycle**, at the tail end of a series of rate hikes; the lowest yields tend to be offered at the bottom of a cycle before recovery.

**Interest rates and stock prices.** The relationship is more complex. The stock market can rise through periods of rising rates when global economies are strong, even with rising inflation. Expectations and announcements of hikes can bring short-term corrections, but markets can continue upward through several hikes if the economy keeps growing. At some point the market recognises that further hikes to control inflation will no longer be accompanied by strong earnings and economic performance; any further hike is then read as a negative trigger for an overvalued market, and a deeper correction follows.

| Scenario | What happens | Stock price effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Rates rise with inflation and strong corporate earnings; firms pass cost increases on to consumers because employment is high | Prices may stay fairly stable, because the rise in required return $$k$$ is offset by the rise in earnings growth $$g$$ |
| **Slightly negative** | Rates rise but expected earnings barely change because firms cannot raise prices to match higher costs | Prices fall, because $$k$$ rises while $$g$$ is constant |
| **Very negative** | Rates rise while cash flows decline; sales revenue and earnings fall, firms cut wages and labour, lay-offs raise unemployment | Prices decline significantly, because $$k$$ rises while $$g$$ falls, widening the $$k - g$$ spread |

Rising rates are not negative for the whole market. Some industries react positively to inflation and rate hikes. During the initial expansion, **banks** benefit from widening the net interest spread — raising lending rates faster than deposit rates — while still enjoying loan growth, so net interest income expands. Banks typically benefit from a gradual rise in rates.

**f. Effectiveness of fiscal and monetary policy.** Five factors limit efficacy:

- **Opposing policies cancel out.** In the US, fiscal policy is under the government and monetary policy under the Fed; politically motivated fiscal priorities can run contrary to monetary policy. In most countries, however, central bank policy is generally aligned with the ruling government.
- **High savings rates blunt fiscal stimulus.** In Asian markets with a culturally high propensity to save (Japan), higher disposable income from tax breaks produces a smaller-than-expected consumption response.
- **Open economies leak stimulus.** Where there is heavy dependence on major foreign companies and construction labour, infrastructure stimulus leaks abroad, lowering the domestic impact.
- **Policies take time.** The deeper the recession, the longer the stimulus takes to flow through.
- **Government strength and stability matter.** Fiscal policy relies on the ability to keep funding it; monetary policy relies on belief in the government's ability to control money supply. With political corruption, social unrest, lack of taxable income, or hyperinflation, neither works.

**g. Negative interest rates as a monetary policy tool.** After the Global Financial Crisis, a few central banks — **Sweden, Denmark, Switzerland and Japan** — set negative rates on commercial banks holding excess funds as reserve deposits with the central bank, so banks pay to keep excess funds on deposit. The aim for the Eurozone-related central banks was to stimulate growth and escape deflation, pushing banks to put money to productive use by lending rather than paying to hold cash.

**h. Money supply from investment flows.** Money supply also comes from external sources. External flows can come from **flights to quality**, as investors reallocate to markets with stronger currency, better economic outlook, healthier earnings growth and potentially higher returns. For example, there was strong demand for the US dollar for most of 2018 as money flowed out of emerging markets into the US on expectations of an emerging-market slowdown caused by US trade tariffs.

### 3.2.5 Business Cycles and Market Movement

Business cycles reflect the rise and fall in the general level of economic activity. No two cycles are identical, but they share common traits: a **business peak**, a **slowdown or contraction**, a **recessionary trough**, then **recovery and expansion**.

**The stock market is predictive of economic conditions.** Stock market peaks and troughs have typically preceded turning points in general business activity — stock prices turn ahead of the economy. Before GDP figures showing two quarters of contraction (a **technical recession**) are announced, equity markets have typically corrected. Equity investors read the trends from leading indicators and adjust; while conditions are still weak, the market waits for leading strategists to declare the worst is over, so as to participate in a rally ahead of reported improvement.

| Phase | Typical observations and policy | Investment strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Early bear to mid bear** | Negative GDP; central bank cuts rates and raises liquidity; equity markets fall; asset deflation; loan losses increase; credit defaults; risk premiums rise; government spending rises; tax cuts to stimulate recovery | Keep cash, investment-grade bonds, precious metals |
| **2. Late bear to early bull** | Negative GDP growth persists; rate cuts continue; fiscal and monetary stimulus; provisions for loan losses; further retrenchments and business cuts; markets perceive the worst is over; asset values begin to rebound sharply | Invest in cyclical and high-beta equities; reduce cash holdings |
| **3. Early bull to mid bull** | GDP turns positive; rates start to rise; corporate profits improve sharply; policy stimulus moderates; attention refocuses on inflation; employment rises; job creation improves | Buy growth stocks and high-yield bonds; sector rotation for laggards |
| **4. Late bull to early bear** | Inflationary pressures rise; central bank targets inflation; factor costs rise and productivity falls; rate hikes; taxation increases; corporate earnings fall; excess capacity emerges, job cuts | Hold defensive, high-dividend stocks and investment-grade bonds |

The equity market reflects the broad range of companies and industries in the economy, so aggregated earnings forecasts represent the growth and performance of the economy. Given the strong correlation between business cycles, the stock market and the overall economy, insight into the phases of the cycle improves the ability to forecast major turning points in stock prices.

The **bond market** is predictive of expected interest-rate trends: with expectations of hikes, yields start rising ahead of actual central bank increases. The bond market also reflects the market demand for credit. When the economy is doing well, credit should expand, so expected long-term credit demand is higher and, on top of the liquidity premium, yields should be upward sloping. When longer-term credit demand appears weak, long-maturity rates may not rise as much even with Fed Funds hikes, **flattening the yield curve — one of the early predictors of an upcoming recession**.

### 3.2.6 Economic Calendars

Economic calendars document the release dates of key economic data and are published by various financial information providers. Investors monitor releases closely to evaluate their impact on financial markets. Price volatility of affected assets tends to increase in the period immediately before and after a release.

### 3.2.7 Economic Indicators

Economic indicators are key statistics giving cues and signals about the direction of the economy. Three broad categories are most widely used.

**a. Leading indicators.** Leading indicators have peaks and troughs that **precede** the economy and are useful short-term predictors. In the US, the **Composite Leading Economic Index (CLEI)** published by The Conference Board comprises **10 indicators**:

| # | Leading indicator | Source and description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Average weekly hours (manufacturing) | Monthly average hourly earnings and average work-week hours, US Department of Labor |
| 2 | Unemployment claims | Weekly first-time claims for state unemployment insurance, US Department of Labor |
| 3 | Manufacturers' shipments, inventories and orders | Monthly preliminary report from the consumer goods and materials report, US Census Bureau |
| 4 | Manufacturers' shipments, inventories and orders for non-defence capital goods | Monthly preliminary report from the non-defence capital goods report, US Census Bureau |
| 5 | Housing starts and building permits | Monthly report, US Census Bureau |
| 6 | 10-year T-bond spread over the Fed rate | Spread between the 10-year US Treasury Note and the Fed Funds Rate |
| 7 | Adjusted M2 money supply | The Fed's inflation-adjusted measure of M2 |
| 8 | ISM Index of Manufacturing (new production orders) | Supplier deliveries, imports, production, inventories, new orders, new export orders, order backlogs, prices and employment |
| 9 | The S&P 500 Index | Free-float capitalisation-weighted index of 500 companies listed on the NYSE, NASDAQ or Cboe BZX |
| 10 | Consumer sentiment | University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index |

A downward spiral in the CLEI components typically signals a forthcoming recession; an upturn indicates an upcoming expansion. Composite leading indicators (CLIs) are also compiled by bodies such as the OECD. Such composites are useful as short-term indicators of turning points, but **they say nothing about the magnitude** of the shifts.

**Singapore's CLI**, published by the Singapore Department of Statistics, comprises:

- Total new companies formed.
- Money supply (M2).
- Stock Exchange of Singapore indices.
- Business expectations for wholesale trade.
- Business expectations for stock of finished goods (manufacturing).
- Purchasing Managers' Index (manufacturing).
- Total non-oil seaborne cargo handled.
- Domestic liquidity indicator.
- Total non-oil retained imports.

**The Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI)** is a widely followed leading indicator measuring the activity level of purchasing managers in the manufacturing, construction and services sectors. In the US it is compiled monthly by the Institute of Supply Management (ISM). **A reading of 50 or higher suggests expansion; below 50 indicates contraction.** The PMI is followed closely for China and the US, the two largest economies, because China is the factory of the world and expansion or contraction in either manufacturing sector ripples through other economies. In Singapore the PMI is compiled and published by the Singapore Institute of Purchasing and Material Management and is a key barometer of manufacturing health.

**b. Coincident indicators.** Coincident indicators have peaks and troughs that **align with** business cycles and move in tandem with the overall economy, reflecting the current state. Sales and personal income are examples: they peak or bottom as the economy does. In the US, the **Coincident Economic Indicators (CEI)** comprise **4 series**:

| # | Coincident indicator | Source and description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nonfarm payroll employment | Excludes farm workers, private household employees and non-profit employees; US Bureau of Labor Statistics |
| 2 | Industrial production index | Inflation-adjusted production output from manufacturing, mining and utilities |
| 3 | Manufacturing and trade sales index | Monthly inflation-adjusted sales index, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis |
| 4 | Personal income and consumption expenditure | Change in personal income reported by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, showing disposable personal income (DPI) and personal consumption expenditures (PCE) |

Singapore's equivalent is the **Composite Coincident Index (CCI)**, published by the Singapore Department of Statistics, comprising:

- GDP at constant prices.
- Index of industrial production.
- Non-oil domestic exports at constant prices.
- Total employment.
- Retail sales index at constant prices, excluding motor vehicles.

**c. Lagging indicators.** Lagging indicators peak and trough **after** the aggregate economy. They change only after the economy has begun to form a pattern and are useful for **confirming** turning points, typically lagging by a few quarters. The Conference Board's index of lagging indicators comprises **7 components**:

- Average duration of employment.
- The manufacturing and trade inventory-to-sales ratio.
- The change in labour cost per unit of manufacturing output.
- The average prime rate charged by banks.
- The value of outstanding commercial and industrial loans.
- The consumer credit outstanding to personal income ratio.
- The change in the consumer price index for services.

### 3.2.8 Other Economic Factors Affecting Asset Values and Market Movements

**a. Foreign currency reserves and balance of payments deficit.** A country with a positive balance of payments (BoP) year after year generates a BoP surplus. Whether it comes from the trade account (exports exceed imports) or the capital account (inflows exceed outflows), foreign exchange reserves increase. A country with a widening trade deficit can become a target of speculative attack because the deficit makes it a "debtor nation". Such weaknesses are generally believed to have led to the **Asian Currency Crisis in 1997**, when weaker Asian currencies came under speculative attack and plummeted, in some cases by **more than 50%**.

Singapore and Hong Kong defended their currencies. Singapore has one of the largest foreign reserves in the world — **USD337 billion as at July 2023**, ranking 11th globally. Countries with no natural resources should maintain high reserves to weather financial storms and global downturns.

A country's exchange rate also affects asset values significantly. If the market expects a sharp fall in a currency, all securities denominated in it are affected, producing an outflow from investments in that currency.

**b. Inflation and the Consumer Price Index (CPI).** The CPI measures the price level of goods and services purchased by households as an indication of inflation. Most central banks are wary of inflation because it is difficult to control once it gathers momentum. The US Fed uses **Core Personal Consumption Expenditure (Core PCE)** as its primary measure and has traditionally set **2%** as the long-run inflation rate, raising rates when there are signs of inflationary pressure. 2017 was different: the Fed raised rates even though Core PCE remained below 2%.

Inflation is a major concern because it:

- Erodes purchasing power.
- Pressures consumers to buy ahead for fear of escalating prices, especially big-ticket items such as housing.
- Encourages businesses to build in buffer pricing, aggravating the expected increase.
- Forces central banks to raise borrowing costs to control it.
- Raises costs that affect quality of life for most of the population and can create social unrest.

In Singapore, the Department of Statistics rebased the CPI to **2019 in February 2020**. For 2020, both MAS Core Inflation and CPI-All Items came in at **−0.2%**, lower than their 2019 outturns of **1.0%** and **0.6%**.

Despite low reported inflation, the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) ranked Singapore the **costliest city in the world from 2014 to 2018**; the 2020 ranking placed it fourth. Two reasons explain the inconsistency: the strong Singapore dollar kept costs lower for the local population than for expatriates, and the basket of goods differs — the EIU includes the cost of buying a car, which is not part of Singapore's CPI for a consumer using public transport.

**c. Persistent deflation.** Deflation is a fall in the real value of assets, which differs from a reduction in inflation (disinflation). Falling prices shift spending into the future, which can trigger a fall in consumption and investment and create an unwarranted economic contraction and negative growth.

Persistent deflation followed the implosion of the Japanese stock and real estate markets in **1989**. Japan's central bank deferred part of the huge corporate losses by allowing outstanding debts to be restructured into low-interest, longer-term government bonds — effectively a bailout. Those bonds were in turn purchased by local corporates, banks, public entities and the Japanese people. In deferring part of the pain from the crash, the Japanese economy endured **12 years of deflation**, low and negative GDP growth over **1989–2014**, and anaemic consumption. The efforts of the European and US central banks after 2008 were aimed at avoiding a similar fate of long-term deflation.

**d. Factor costs.** Crude oil is a key factor cost that has made a dramatic impact on global economic growth over the last century. Before fracking technology and the development of energy alternatives, the supply of crude oil was easily affected by output from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). Five of the last six US recessions since the 1970s — **1973, 1975, 1979, 1980/81, 1990 and 2008/09** — were preceded by spikes in crude oil prices.

From **2015 to 2017**, excess capacity and supply from new oil producers reversed the situation and crude oil prices plunged to **USD28 per barrel** at one point. The lower crude price reduced factor costs and inflation globally. While lower crude prices hurt oil-producing countries and oil companies, the rest of the world benefited from cost savings that supported the global economic recovery. US oil and refining companies emerged as a major producer and dominant player. Amid agreement from OPEC member nations and Russia to cap output in 2017, the excess supply was eventually absorbed and prices began to stabilise in 2017–2018.

Other major factor costs include rent and labour. High land prices, rental and labour costs raise the cost of doing business, stifle new business creation and skew the composition of viable industries, all of which has a negative long-run impact on growth.

### 3.2.9 Political Factors Affecting Markets and Asset Values

**Country and political risk.** To assess country risk, markets look beyond monetary and fiscal policy to the legal framework, foreign exchange reserves, political stability and credibility. Dedicated research organisations — BMI Group, Economist Intelligence Unit, Country Risk Solutions — have in-house assessment criteria, and Euromoney publishes survey reports gauging the market's view of country risk. Countries with stable governments, a good track record and consistency in driving their economic agenda, maintaining social peace and abiding by proper legal and accounting frameworks are ranked highly and attract investors because return uncertainty is lower. **Asset valuations are lower for higher-risk countries**, because higher yields are required to compensate investors for the country risk borne. For sovereign debt credit ratings, the renowned agencies are **Moody's, Standard & Poor's and Fitch Ratings**.

**Political shocks and uncertainty.** Brexit was a major surprise in 2016. The **51.9%** vote for Britain to leave the European Union produced panic selling of the GBP in June 2016: it **fell 10.5% in 4 days** and drifted down to a low of **GBP/USD 1.21714 (down 18%)** by October, because the investment and business community regarded exit as negative for Britain. During the night of the US election, when it became clear that Republican President Trump's unexpected win was imminent, the **DJIA plunged 800 points**; markets recovered soon after when the President highlighted fiscal stimulus plans including tax cuts and infrastructure spending.

**Continuity and political will.** Whatever the type of government — democratic, socialist or communist — investors want strong, capable leaders who uphold the legal framework and drive the economy for the good of people, businesses and industries. Uncertainty about succession and continuity, or a lack of political will to follow through, raises risk and tends to produce investment outflows.

**Sectors and companies that fall in and out of favour.** New governments' political and social agendas benefit some companies and threaten others. A socially conscious government may seek healthcare cost reductions and curb drug prices; a pro-defence government raises interest in the defence industry; governments supported by oil-rich or coal-producing states may be less inclined towards renewable energy than those seeking energy self-sufficiency; some governments welcome the gaming industry for taxable income while others curb it for social ills. Changes in government can therefore change the fortunes of businesses.

### 3.2.10 Industry Analysis

Some factors affect certain industries more favourably than others. At times industry-related factors outweigh economic factors and make certain industries more resilient to economic or financial crisis, which is usually reflected in share prices. Other factors — structural change and disruptive technology — can cause rapid decline.

Three approaches are considered: the **industry life cycle**, **Porter's five competitive forces**, and **business cycle analysis**.

**a. Importance of industry analysis.** Two reasons:

- Risks and rates of return vary widely across industries in a given period, because of different product and service mixes and different business-cycle sensitivities.
- Industries display different rates of return over time, so past industry performance cannot simply be extrapolated.

**b. Industry life cycle.** A framework for evaluating the general health and current stage of an industry. Development divides into five stages: **early development, rapid expansion, mature growth, stabilisation and market maturity, and deceleration of growth and decline**. Besides sales, profit margins and earnings growth can be analysed with the same framework.

| Stage | Characteristics | Risk and return |
|---|---|---|
| **i. Early development** (pioneering or start-up) | Introduction of a new technology and a product that did not previously exist — renewable energy, battery-driven electric cars. High start-up R&D costs; struggle for commercial viability; modest sales; margins very small or negative; low demand. Many start-ups enter, few survive. Tesla reported **10 years of losses** leading up to 2018; many pharmaceutical and biotech companies have no sales and burn equity on R&D | High risk in an unproven company, but high expected returns if it succeeds. Difficult, though very rewarding, to identify the winners |
| **ii. Rapid growth** | Awareness grows, a market develops, demand picks up significantly; capacity expands. The few survivors face less competition and can build substantial backlogs, becoming industry leaders. Rising sales plus greater efficiency accelerate profit growth; profits can more than double from a low base; margins very high. Examples: Amazon (2014–2018), Alibaba (2011–2018), Facebook (2010–2017), Google (2002–2012), with earnings compounding at **30–50%**. Industrial robotics in China, where only **20%** of industries use any form of industrial robot | Highest growth; the pace naturally slows as firm size increases |
| **iii. Mature growth** | Demand largely satisfied; sales growth stays above average but no longer accelerates. Industry sales can still run **12–20%**. Performance varies more year to year and between companies because of different business models. New competition emerges, attracted by high historic margins, and margins decline; established companies with excess cash may enter. Companies with dominant share and established brands keep growing | Growth continues but decelerates; margins compress |
| **iv. Stabilisation** | Probably the longest phase. Industry growth tracks overall economic growth. Profit growth varies with management's ability to control costs. Products become standardised and less innovative; the marketplace is full of competitors, margins are tight, and returns on capital eventually equal or fall slightly below the competitive level | Returns converge to the competitive level |
| **v. Decline** | Sales growth declines from shifts in demand or the emergence of substitutes; growth cannot keep pace with the economy. Margins are squeezed; some firms make low profits or losses; survivors show very low returns on capital and investors consider alternative uses for the capital. Example: the music record industry, whose CD and tape sales almost halted with YouTube and streaming, replaced by iTunes and online distributors | Avoid |

An understanding of the growth pattern helps assess the growth potential of companies in the industry. The average investor may prefer industries in rapid or mature growth; aggressive investors may consider early development, which offers the highest potential returns and the greatest risks; industries in decline should generally be avoided. Comparing an industry's sales with those of firms in the overall economy helps identify its stage.

**c. Competitive forces and industry structure.** Michael Porter holds that an industry's competitive environment and the intensity of competition determine a firm's ability to sustain above-average returns on invested capital. Five forces determine industry profitability:

- Rivalry among existing competitors.
- Threat of new entrants.
- Threat of substitute products.
- Bargaining power of buyers.
- Bargaining power of suppliers.

Where the five forces are favourable, many companies earn attractive returns. Where pressure from one or more forces is intense, it is harder to command high returns despite management effort.

| Force | What makes it intense | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **i. Rivalry among existing competitors** | Many firms of relatively equal size, including foreign competitors; slow growth forcing a fight for share; the desire to sell at full capacity to cover fixed costs, producing price cutting; **exit barriers** such as specialised facilities or labour agreements keeping firms in the industry despite below-average or negative returns | — |
| **ii. Threat of new entrants** | Low current prices relative to costs, small capital requirements, few economies of scale, no need for extensive distribution; low switching costs; government policy that does not restrict entry through licensing or material access | High barriers keep competition low |
| **iii. Threat of substitute products** | Substitutes cap the price firms can charge; the closer the substitute in price and function, the greater the threat. Rapid technological progress makes products and whole industries obsolete | Digital photography and imaging ended camera film, bankrupting **Kodak** |
| **iv. Bargaining power of buyers** | Buyers purchasing large volumes relative to supplier sales; the most vulnerable firm supplies a single large customer; the item is a significant share of the buyer's total costs; the buyer faces cost pressure from its own customers; the buyer has intimate knowledge of supplier costs, as when it partly supplies its own needs | The **US government** for defence contractors and military aircraft; **Apple** as key customer for many contractors |
| **v. Bargaining power of suppliers** | Few suppliers; critical inputs with few or no substitutes, creating a sellers' market; workers supported by strong unions; monopolistic or oligopolistic suppliers | Utilities with captive electricity consumers; pharmaceutical and biotech companies with specialty drugs |

Because an industry's competitive structure is not static, regular review is necessary.

**d. Business cycle analysis.** Another way to analyse industries is by performance relative to the overall economy. Earnings of many companies move with business cycles, and some industries cope with recession reasonably well while others perform badly.

| Industry type | Behaviour | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical** | Sales and earnings greatly affected by the business environment; do exceptionally well in expansions and rather badly in contractions. **Durable goods** are cyclical: demand for cars and refrigerators rises in good times and purchases are postponed in recession | Cars, refrigerators |
| **Defensive** | Least affected by recession and adversity | Food and public utilities — people need food regardless of the economy. Note that some food segments are not defensive, such as specialist coffee chains in Singapore |
| **Growth** | Earnings expected to be significantly above the average of all industries, and growth may occur notwithstanding economic setbacks | Mobile device game applications and music purchased via internet stores; historically cell phones, audio CDs and computers |
| **Interest-sensitive** | Sensitive to changes in interest-rate expectations | Financial services and real estate |

To predict industry performance, analyse the stage of the business cycle and its likely responses to macroeconomic changes — the balance of payments, the trade account, inflation and unemployment. If the economy heads into recession, cyclical industries are affected more than others and defensive industries least. The outcome of industry analysis lets the investor select the industry that better fits their risk and return requirements.

### 3.2.11 Firm-Specific Factors and Analysis

Just as industry factors affect firms across industries, unique characteristics of firms within an industry affect their attractiveness. Applying a **bottom-up** approach to stock-picking, firms with specific competitive advantages distinguish themselves — for example, what makes Alibaba and Tencent dominant among Chinese internet sales platforms, or why Nvidia traded at a significantly higher valuation in 2017 than other US semiconductor makers.

| # | Firm-specific factor |
|---|---|
| 1 | Dominance and control of technology/research |
| 2 | Control over resource supply |
| 3 | Superior brand differentiation |
| 4 | Dominance in sales distribution/marketing |
| 5 | Cost efficiency, lowest-cost producer |
| 6 | Successful focus on target niche clients |
| 7 | Established diversified core business |
| 8 | Superior leadership, management and talent retention |

Firm-specific factors are critical for making precise sales and earnings forecasts, which increases the accuracy of share-price valuation.

## 3.3 Market-Driven Factors and Asset Values

Market-driven factors are physical market conditions that affect short-term demand and supply and, consequently, the pricing of investment products.

### 3.3.1 Liquidity and Money Flow

**Excessive liquidity.** During periods of uncertainty investors sell off investments and hold more cash, and interest rates are usually low as the central bank tries to stimulate the economy. At the onset of a rally, aggressive investors and money managers begin investing, and the market keeps rising as long as there is sufficient liquidity and money flow to drive it upward.

**Liquidity shortage.** At some point the bulk of funds is almost fully invested. With insufficient funds to sustain the upward drive, the market plateaus and funds rotate to lagging sectors and assets. This is typically when parts of the market become overvalued. Lacking fresh funds to sustain a bull market, the market is poised for a trigger to cause correction and profit-taking.

Analysts track the flow of funds, the excess cash balances of mutual funds and bank deposit levels to predict the funds available for investing. When liquidity builds up and deposit rates are low, money eventually flows back into the stock market as investors seek higher returns, so there is more upside. When available or excess funds dwindle and investors are increasingly invested, there is less liquidity to push the market up — a signal to sell.

### 3.3.2 New Securities Issues

**Excessive equities issued.** A significant supply of securities can drain funds for a period. If several major IPOs come to the same market at once, funds are absorbed by them — in some cases deliberately priced at a discount to ensure demand — at the expense of similar stocks already trading. Some investors take profit on existing stocks to participate in the IPOs. The number of IPOs per year therefore affects demand for other similar securities, and when there are too many IPOs in a year, demand for some may suffer because funds are already committed to earlier issues.

**Excessive Treasury bills and bonds issued.** The same happens in fixed income. Excessive issuance of government T-bills and bonds of a particular maturity creates an oversupply in that maturity tranche, so bond prices may dip briefly to offer better yields for that tranche. The same occurs if there is a sudden reduction of interest by regular buyers of US Treasuries — if China or Japan decided to buy fewer US Treasury bonds, that would suddenly create excess supply.

### 3.3.3 Trading Influence from Derivatives and Other Asset Classes

**a. Influence from structured products.** Markets are generally efficient and difficult for any one investor or speculator to influence. But with structured products there may be a **collective incentive** for proprietary traders at financial institutions to move the price of certain stocks to reach strike-price targets for stocks or indices, because those institutions are usually the counterparty to the structured products and the profitability of their positions depends on prices moving favourably.

On thin-trading days or during specific periods of the day, certain parties may try to push prices in a specific direction. When more than one institution sells similar structured products with similar target price ranges, the collective ability to move the underlying price of securities and indices can be significant. Proving collusion and illegality is difficult, but such actions are unethical because the institution profits by working against investors to whom it sold a product. Some institutions argue that proprietary desks have no knowledge of the structured-product positions held by other investors and receive no instruction to act either way, but institutions and professionals should not tolerate practices that profit at clients' expense.

**b. Influence from futures and options.** In bearish markets, the sale of futures contracts and put options can add significant pressure on underlying assets. Futures and options are leveraged instruments traded on margin: an investor puts up only a small fraction of capital to own the full notional amount, so returns are magnified relative to the underlying. As underlying assets fall, futures and options prices fall further with magnified losses; derivative investors may be forced to reduce holdings to meet margin requirements, driving the market down further. This creates the risk of the derivative and underlying markets **feeding off each other in a downward spiral**. The reverse can occur but is less worrying, at least initially, because markets are rising.

**c. Major common expiration dates.** Many instruments mature and must be rolled over toward the end of quarterly periods, so volume and volatility can pick up in the **last two weeks of March, June, September and December**. In the US, on the **third Friday** of each of those months, the last hour of the stock market session (**3.00–4.00pm New York time**) is the **triple witching hour**: three kinds of securities mature that day — stock market index futures, stock market index options and stock options. During that hour, expirations generally increase trading volume in options, futures and the underlying stocks, and occasionally price volatility as well.

### 3.3.4 Other Trade Flows

**Hot money.** Global money from hedge and other large funds seeking temporary inefficiencies and arbitrage opportunities may flow into a market, sector or set of stocks and temporarily push up volume, interest and prices. Such hot money can be orchestrated with the dissemination of additional positive information to generate interest, only to exit once return targets are achieved. This is essentially **pump-and-dump** activity, but with more finesse and sophistication and at a global level.

**Computer-driven momentum-based trading.** Computer-driven trading follows a defined set of instructions to make high-speed decisions and transactions across many markets — commodity futures, stocks, currency, money market — generating profits at a frequency human traders cannot match. Such algorithms are predominantly influenced by technical market indicators that dictate continued buying while upticks and specific price and volume trends are in place, regardless of valuation, and continued selling on downward momentum. As a result of widespread use, prices of securities and assets have become more volatile.

### 3.3.5 Market Microstructure Influence on Price and Asset Values

In the short term, prices and values are also influenced by market structure — the trading and execution processes, rules, liquidity and strength of participants.

**a. Differences in the price discovery process.**

| Market type | How price is discovered |
|---|---|
| **Issuer-priced** | The buyer simply looks at the price quote provided by the issuer, which controls pricing. With relatively high demand there is typically no room for negotiation and the buyer is a **price taker** |
| **OTC negotiated** | Prices are negotiated over the phone with OTC dealers or market makers |
| **Continuous stock market** | A rule-based process with bid and ask quotes provided continuously by different parties. Automated platforms and exchanges prioritise matching the **highest bid to the lowest ask**, then the timing of quotes. Buyers and sellers can gauge trends and sentiment through the day and adjust or close positions |
| **Call market** (some European bourses, e.g. France) | All bids are submitted and batched for simultaneous execution once or twice a day when the market is "called". **Price, but not timing, prioritisation** is practised. Traders submit limit and/or market orders but cannot ascertain the likelihood of execution until after the call |
| **IPO** | Similar to a call market, with bids submitted, but with the added criterion of **bid size** to determine allocation |
| **Auction of a pool of stocks** | Bids are submitted and the auctioneer takes the highest bids to match the number of stocks offered. Rare, even in distress; in most distress situations creditors or parties taking over negotiate a price |

The types of players in each market structure and the specific systems and rules applied therefore affect the pricing process.

**b. Impact of computer-driven, momentum-based trading on the pricing process.** Algorithmic trading is one of the most commonly applied strategies globally. **High frequency trading (HFT)**, the primary form, is the rapid execution of extremely short-duration trades, profiting by arbitraging small price differences across exchanges. Its success comes from processing information and executing trades simultaneously without the time and emotional burden of human decisions. HFT has improved market liquidity and produced higher price efficiency through synchronised statistical arbitrage, but it also poses risks of increased volatility and potential **predatory trading** at the expense of less sophisticated participants, destabilising the market.

**c. Inventory costs and liquidity.** Research indicates the quoted bid-ask spread can be influenced by **inventory costs** and by the amount of liquidity certain traders or investors possess. A dealer or market maker holding an inventory of stocks, and incurring costs, may price differently from one that does not. Other studies suggest parties with greater cash liquidity can have greater influence over pricing.

**d. Dark pools and price efficiency.** Dark pools are networks — private financial forums or exchanges — for trading securities, derivatives and other instruments. The bulk of dark pool trades are large trades by financial institutions conducted outside public exchanges, keeping trade details confidential. Dark pools are not new, since block trades and private transactions have long existed, but the pools of entities facilitating and financing private trading have grown, away from the exchange's view, until transactions are completed and brought into the open. Although the standard price discovery process is generally assumed to keep publicly traded prices relatively fair, in dark pools the internally agreed trade prices, together with the size and lack of transparency of participant identities, put less sophisticated participants at a disadvantage.

## 3.4 Effect of Investment Behaviour and Emotions on Asset Values

### 3.4.1 Herd Instinct — Greed and Fear

A herd instinct is an emotional phenomenon characterised by a lack of independent decision-making in a crowd acting without centralised direction. It is attributed to **greed and fear** and can drive asset values well above or below the levels justified by fundamentals. Behavioural studies show the herd instinct kicks in especially when **information is scarce and threats seem imminent**.

As the market declines to a trough or turning point, participants begin accumulating when they believe the worst is over. Once the trough is confirmed, confidence gradually returns as more participants and funds re-enter. Retail investors pile back in and **greed takes over** as participants lose sight of valuations and push the market to new heights. An **investment bubble** occurs when the market is driven by investors overlooking fundamentals and overcome with optimism about asset values, perpetuated by exuberant behaviour.

The **1997–2000 US Internet Dot.com Bubble** is the example of mass euphoria with fundamentals discarded: investors bought overpriced companies, some generating no revenue at all, let alone profit. **NASDAQ rose sharply from 1,000 to 5,000**, with a market price-earnings (P/E) ratio of **200x**. Then Fed Chairman Alan Greenspan warned of overvaluation by coining the term "**irrational exuberance**" in **December 1996**, but the bubble continued until 2001.

### 3.4.2 Loss Aversion in Portfolios

Loss aversion is associated with **prospect theory**. An implication is that individuals prefer **less** uncertainty for gains and **more** uncertainty for losses. When the market is falling, there may be higher fund redemptions and securities sold. Investors and managers may be unconsciously influenced by loss aversion to sell profitable holdings to lock in positive returns (lowering uncertainty) while holding on to book losses on poorer performers in the hope of a rebound (raising uncertainty).

The profitable securities sold may actually have more upside, but because of loss aversion investors sell those first and retain the losers, which may fall deeper into the red because they are trying to avoid realising losses. Loss aversion therefore has a potential **double negative impact**.

### 3.4.3 Cognitive Bias

Cognitive bias is the tendency to make conclusions based on one's past experiences, preferences or predetermined beliefs. **Confirmation bias** is the tendency to look for views and recommendations aligned with one's own. Investors who have committed funds to an investment are more prone to listen to and agree with research calling for appreciation than research calling for a sale, and then reinforce the view by committing even more funds. An investor less influenced by this bias actively looks for research opinions different from their own and checks whether the alternative views have merit.

## 3.5 Summary

- Three primary factors affect market movements and asset values: fundamental factors, market-driven factors and investment behavioural factors.
- Fundamental analysis revolves around economic, political, industry and firm-specific factors.
- Fiscal and monetary policy, business cycles and interest rates are the main areas of economic analysis.
- Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy stimulates growth; contractionary policy controls inflation and cools the economy. Expectations and announcements of policy actions can dramatically move markets.
- Business cycles reflect the general level of economic activity. Stock market peaks and troughs have typically preceded turning points in business activity, so insight into cycle phases improves forecasting of turning points in stock prices.
- The investment community monitors key information releases to see whether actual data match estimates and whether forecasts need revision. Price volatility of affected assets tends to rise immediately before and after a release.
- Three categories of economic indicator give early warning of changes in activity: leading, coincident and lagging.
- Other economic factors include foreign currency reserve levels, the balance of payments, inflation and deflation, and changes in factor costs.
- Market participants favour countries with stable, capable and honest governments. Investors should watch elections, changes in leadership, party dissent and stated party policies to mitigate return uncertainty.
- Industry analysis matters because risks and returns vary widely across industries, and industry profitability does not remain constant. Industry-specific factors can dominate economic factors.
- The industry life cycle divides development into five stages: early development, rapid expansion, mature growth, stabilisation and decline.
- Porter's five competitive forces determine the intensity of competition and therefore industry profitability.
- Industry business cycles should be examined by comparing company earnings performance with that of the overall economy.
- Firm-specific factors uniquely distinguish one firm from another and support more accurate earnings and cash flow forecasts.
- Market-driven factors are physical market conditions affecting short-term demand and supply and therefore pricing.
- Major market-driven factors include liquidity flows, new securities issues, trading influences from derivatives and other asset classes, hot-money flows, and computer-driven momentum trading. These must not be underestimated, as they are increasingly dominant over fundamentals.
- Market microstructure influences include varying pricing processes, inventory costs and dark pool liquidity.
- Investor behaviour matters: fear, greed, loss aversion and cognitive biases significantly affect asset values and market movements.

## Exam cues

- **Three drivers of asset values:** fundamental, market-driven, investment behaviour. Fundamental and market-driven are the rational explanation; behaviour is the emotional one.
- **EMH (Fama):** prices adjust rapidly to new public information, so price equals fair value and no one consistently outperforms. The examination tests the *counter-argument* — persistent inefficiency and cognitive bias create opportunity.
- **Fiscal policy** = government expenditure and taxation. **Monetary policy** = money supply and the cost of funding. Do not mix them.
- **GDP identity:** $$GDP = C + I + G + NE$$. Match each fiscal measure to the component it raises: infrastructure → $$G$$; relocation incentives → $$I$$; income tax cuts → $$C$$; tariffs → $$NE$$.
- **Multiplier effect:** fiscal spending ripples through industries, raising wages, profits, disposable income and further consumption, then private capital expenditure.
- **Fed's three tools:** OMOs, the Fed Funds Rate, the discount window. **OMOs are the primary tool.** Liquidity changes hit **bond prices first, then stocks, then the economy**.
- **QE timeline:** rates held at **0.25% from Dec 2008 to Dec 2016**, then raised **25bp** at a time; unemployment **3.8% in May 2018** (48-year low); core inflation target **2.0%**; Fed Funds **2.25% in September 2018**.
- **Singapore's monetary policy is exchange-rate-based, since 1981.** Managed float against a **trade-weighted basket**, within a **policy band** announced **semi-annually**, reviewed about every **six months**, with an **MPS** and **Macroeconomic Review**. Singapore therefore has **no independent interest-rate or money-supply target**, and domestic rates have typically been **below US rates**.
- **Credit creation:** 10% reserve requirement → **10-fold** money supply expansion (10,000 deposit → 100,000 total).
- **Fisher's equation of exchange:** $$M \times V = P \times Q$$. Increasing $$M$$ raises $$P$$, not $$Q$$.
- **Interest rates and bonds:** lower rates → lower discount rate → higher bond value. **Highest yields near the business-cycle peak**; lowest yields at the bottom before recovery.
- **Interest rates and stocks — three scenarios:** positive ($$k$$ and $$g$$ both rise, price stable), slightly negative ($$k$$ rises, $$g$$ constant, price falls), very negative ($$k$$ rises, $$g$$ falls, price falls sharply). Banks benefit from a **gradual** rise through a widening net interest spread.
- **Business cycle phases and strategy:** early/mid bear → cash, investment-grade bonds, precious metals; late bear/early bull → cyclicals and high-beta, reduce cash; early/mid bull → growth stocks, high-yield bonds, sector rotation; late bull/early bear → defensive high-dividend stocks, investment-grade bonds.
- **A flattening yield curve is an early predictor of recession.**
- **Three indicator classes:** **leading** precede (CLEI, 10 components; PMI), **coincident** align (CEI, 4 components), **lagging** confirm (7 components). Leading indicators signal turning points but **not magnitude**.
- **PMI: 50 is the dividing line** — 50 or above = expansion, below 50 = contraction.
- **Singapore's CLI** includes total new companies formed, M2, SGX indices, business expectations, PMI, non-oil seaborne cargo, domestic liquidity indicator and non-oil retained imports. **Singapore's CCI** includes GDP at constant prices, industrial production, non-oil domestic exports, total employment and retail sales excluding motor vehicles.
- **1997 Asian Currency Crisis:** widening trade deficits made countries "debtor nations" and targets of speculative attack; currencies fell **more than 50%** in some cases. Singapore's reserves: **USD337 billion as at July 2023**, 11th globally.
- **Inflation:** US Fed targets **Core PCE at 2%**. Singapore rebased CPI to **2019 in February 2020**; 2020 Core and All-Items both **−0.2%** (2019: 1.0% and 0.6%). EIU ranked Singapore the **costliest city 2014–2018**.
- **Brexit:** **51.9%** leave vote; GBP **fell 10.5% in 4 days**, down **18%** to GBP/USD 1.21714 by October 2016. US election night: DJIA **plunged 800 points**, then recovered on fiscal-stimulus plans.
- **Industry life cycle — five stages:** early development, rapid expansion, mature growth, stabilisation, decline. Mature growth sales run **12–20%**; rapid-growth earnings compound at **30–50%**.
- **Porter's five forces:** rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power. Kodak is the substitute example; the US government and Apple are buyer-power examples.
- **Industry types:** cyclical (durable goods), defensive (food, utilities), growth, interest-sensitive (financials, real estate).
- **Firm-specific factors:** eight listed — technology dominance, resource supply, brand, distribution, cost efficiency, niche focus, diversified core, leadership.
- **Liquidity cycle:** excess liquidity drives the rally; when funds are fully invested the market plateaus and rotates; dwindling liquidity signals a sell.
- **Triple witching:** third Friday of March, June, September and December, **3.00–4.00pm New York time**; index futures, index options and stock options all expire.
- **Behavioural biases:** herd instinct (greed and fear, strongest when information is scarce), loss aversion (prospect theory — sell winners, hold losers), cognitive bias (confirmation bias).
- **Dot.com bubble:** NASDAQ **1,000 → 5,000**, P/E **200x**, "irrational exuberance" coined **December 1996**, bubble ran to 2001.
- **Trap:** a **leading** indicator predicts, a **coincident** indicator confirms the present, a **lagging** indicator confirms the past. PMI is leading; nonfarm payrolls are coincident; the average prime rate is lagging.
- **Trap:** expansionary monetary policy lowers rates and *raises* asset values; expansionary fiscal policy raises GDP components but may raise inflation and deficits.
- **Trap:** rising interest rates are not uniformly bad for equities — banks can benefit during the initial expansion.
