# Derivatives dealing practices and skills

**Learning objectives.** The candidate should understand:

- the framework of the trading strategy process and its principles — **trading strategy formulation** and **trading strategy implementation**;
- **client servicing practices** highly recommended by industry standards;
- the processes involved in carrying out the **Customer Account Review (CAR)**, the **Customer Knowledge Assessment (CKA)** and **Specified Investment Products (SIP) trading account opening**;
- the different types of risk associated at both institutional and individual level, and the appropriate risk management actions.

## 6.1 Introduction

A CMS licence holder and its representatives who **recommend any products** to a prospective client are generally required to have a **reasonable basis** for the recommendations.

There are **exceptions** where the firm and its representatives:

- merely **carry out a client's instructions** without making any recommendation; or
- are **exempt** from those standards in relation to a recommendation of a **listed excluded investment product**, subject to conditions — including **providing a rationale for the execution-related advice** given, and **giving the client notice** that the advice **does not take into account the client's objectives or needs** and that the **client needs to assess suitability** of the investment.

The chapter examines the **investment advisory process** — how it leads to an overall investment strategy constructed for the client, and how that strategy ultimately affects how the client's trades are executed.

## 6.2 Analysis of market information

At a macro level, analysis of financial markets involves **monitoring the relative performance of financial instruments in different asset classes** — equities, bonds, commodities, futures, options and swaps.

Three generic methods of financial analysis are used:

| Method | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Fundamental analysis** | Studies the **financials of an industry or company** using **financial ratios and valuation models** to ascertain **intrinsic value**. Usually applied to equities. |
| **Technical analysis** | Uses **historical price movements displayed on a chart** as guidance to gauge and make future investment decisions. |
| **Global macro analysis** | Interprets and predicts **large-scale events** in relation to macroeconomics, history and international relations — typically employing forecasts and analysis of **interest rate trends, government policies, political changes and other systemic factors**. |

Different methods suit different asset classes and strategies. Where a client wishes to invest in **equities with a long-term horizon**, **fundamental analysis** is most suitable, because it accounts for a company's **profitability over a longer period**. **Technical analysis** may be better suited to **short-term** strategies, because market fluctuations are more significant in the short term.

With the internet age and media, there has been a **massive influx of intelligence gathering** from markets — obtaining information about a product or sector from **financial news media, broker subscription or third-party research**. The firm and its representatives must still **conduct due diligence on a regular basis** to ensure information obtained is **objective and has reasonable basis**.

> **Example — market intelligence gathering.** Jonathan, a senior derivatives analyst at hedge fund Kappa Capital, is researching Theta Corporation. Kappa is interested in buying Theta shares because Jonathan's primary research shows strong fundamentals. Jonathan comes across a **conflicting report** from Alpha Research Company highlighting **questionable accounting practices**. He investigates Alpha's background and the writer: the writer is a **recently fired ex-employee of Theta**, and **Alpha has only existed for 6 months**. After careful consideration Jonathan **disregards the negative report**. He did so only after researching the **credibility of the report** — analysing the data, investigating Alpha's experience, and considering the **motivation behind publication**, suspecting the report may be biased.

## 6.3 Trading strategy formulation

### 6.3.1 Analysis of overall investment objective

After ascertaining the client investment profile and conducting market research, the firm and its representatives must **reassess the client's overall investment objective**. This includes **clearly stating the purpose for trading** — is the primary purpose to **capture excess returns** from the market, or to **hedge against market risks**?

> **Example — trading strategy formulation.** Daniel has two clients, Tom and Harry, with roughly the same annual income and healthy financial status, but **Tom has a much higher risk tolerance than Harry**. Tom's strategy is to **seek excess returns (alpha)** and he is willing to invest aggressively; Harry wants a **steady rate of return with low volatility**, achieved by **hedging against market risks**. Daniel should recommend to Tom a portfolio with **higher-yield but riskier securities** — small-cap equities or equity derivatives — with a **large portion** dedicated to them. For Harry, he should recommend **more allocation to fixed income instruments**, which offer less volatility and a steadier return.

### 6.3.2 Return and risk objectives

The firm and its representatives should **highlight details of the overall investment strategy**, including the **return and risk objectives** of the strategy used for the client's investments or portfolio.

#### 6.3.2.1 Performance target

They should consider **what returns are needed** to satisfy the overall objectives, with **careful specification of how the performance target meets the main investment objective**. They should also consider **all possible asset classes**, since different asset classes have different returns. Both client and firm can use **risk-adjustment techniques** to compare returns against **benchmarks such as stock market indices**.

#### 6.3.2.2 Spending costs and assumptions

What are the **likely costs** incurred when investing in certain asset classes? This includes **fees, taxes, inflation and anticipated spending**, useful as a guide to making **realistic assumptions**.

#### 6.3.2.3 Portfolio asset allocation

The firm and its representatives should **designate target allocations for each asset class**, including **setting an allowable range within each asset class**. They are responsible for **delineating the asset allocation strategy to the client** and for **maintaining the client's portfolio**.

#### 6.3.2.4 Risk tolerance

Does the strategy take into account **temporary negative returns and losses** that may occur over time? Representatives must ensure investors are aware of the relevant risks — **business, liquidity, geo-political and regulatory risks**, among others.

Even after risk tolerance has been outlined in the **initial client investment profiling**, it must be **continually monitored**. Gauging risk tolerance is **subjective** and may be a challenge, especially for individual clients, so **reassessments must be continual**.

### 6.3.3 Constraints of trading strategies

The firm and its representatives must address the **possible constraints** affecting a client's investment strategy and portfolio — most of which are **closely tied to the risks** associated with the portfolio.

#### 6.3.3.1 Trading strategy and time horizon

When formulating a strategy they should highlight the **time period the position will be held** — from **a few days or weeks to months and years**. This depends on the client's intention: to **capture quick profits from potential price spikes**, or to **hold the position to accumulate cash flow**.

#### 6.3.3.2 Liquidity maintenance

Clients may need to **withdraw cash to fulfil short-term needs**. The firm and its representatives should **discuss these specifications** and **prepare for situations with possible liquidity constraints**.

#### 6.3.3.3 Legal constraints

They should **conduct due diligence** to find out and comply with **any legal regulations and restrictions** that may affect the client's portfolio or trades.

#### 6.3.3.4 Restrictions on foreign investment

They must take note of the client's **portfolio exposure in foreign investments and trades**. Clients may **limit these exposures** for investment, economic or political reasons. **Administrative costs** may also be a concern when investing in foreign assets.

### 6.3.4 Risk management of the trading strategy

When formulating strategies, the firm and its representatives must **incorporate proper steps to avert or reduce risk** where necessary.

#### 6.3.4.1 Risk measurement and evaluation

They should use **consistent metrics** to assess and evaluate the risk profile of the proposed strategy, so that **meaningful comparison** can be made in gauging portfolio performance. They must **avoid inappropriate use of metrics in an attempt to disguise risks**.

#### 6.3.4.2 Process for rebalancing the portfolio and trade positions

They should plan a **rebalancing system** with the client in the event of the portfolio's exposure to volatility and risk. A rebalancing system helps **change the exposure of certain positions to reduce risk** arising from a particular event.

For example, if they suspect **oil prices will spike** due to macroeconomic events, they may **reduce weightage in oil futures or energy-sector equities**. In portfolio management this is **tactical asset allocation** — an **active management strategy** that shifts weightages to take advantage of **market mispricing or strong sectors**.

#### 6.3.4.3 Risk management framework

A simple risk management framework involves **five steps**:

- **i. Risk identification** — identifying possible risks to the trading strategy.
- **ii. Risk assessment** — making **initial qualitative assessments** and **estimating the probability and impact** of the risks if they occur.
- **iii. Risk evaluation** — making **thorough quantitative assessments and measurements** to better estimate probability and impact.
- **iv. Risk response** — planning the response to each risk addressed, **taking into account any relationship between individual risks**.
- **v. Risk reporting** — reporting on the outcome of policy execution and evaluating whether the response has **mitigated or significantly reduced** the risks; **regularly evaluating the entire framework** to provide feedback and amendments.

## 6.4 Trading strategy implementation and monitoring

### 6.4.1 Recommendations on capital markets products

The firm and its representatives are expected to have **sufficient knowledge of the client's investment objectives, financial situation and needs** before advising on sales or purchases of capital markets products.

A **dealer** is a person exempt from holding a financial adviser's licence under section 23(1)(a), (b) or (d) of the FAA who carries on a business of providing **execution-related advice**, which means advice on any **listed excluded investment product** that is **solely incidental to the execution activities** of the dealer with **no discrete fee charged** for the advice.

Where the client does not wish to receive advice, this should be followed by **documenting the client's decision** and **warning the client** that any recommendation or advice **would not take into account his investment objectives, financial situation and risk tolerance**. Ultimately it is the **client's responsibility** to ensure suitability of product recommendations.

### 6.4.2 Documentation and record keeping

It is the duty of the firm and its representatives to **maintain the client's profile** and **record the recommendation or warning** made. Recording can take the form of **file notes or tapes of a conversation**, which assists both client and firm in **resolving disputes** about advice provided based on the client's investment profile.

As **best practice**, MAS recommends records be maintained for **at least 5 years**. The nature of records is determined by the firm, taking into account the **nature of the client's business** and the **likelihood of the client disputing** that advice was given without due consideration of the client's profile and financial needs.

### 6.4.3 Disclosure of conflicts of interest

The firm and its representatives should **disclose any potential conflict of interest** to clients, **in writing or orally**. Once disclosed, they are **not required to repeat the disclosure** every time they make a recommendation or provide execution-related advice, provided:

- **i.** the previous disclosure **remains up-to-date, accurate and comprehensive**; and
- **ii.** the client **can reasonably be expected to be aware** of the previous disclosure.

They should also note whether there is a **long lapse** between the current recommendation and the previous disclosure, and consider whether the client is likely to assume the current recommendation **no longer applies** to the previous disclosure. **If ever in doubt, disclose** any conflict of interest that could potentially occur.

### 6.4.4 Disclaimers

The firm and its representatives **can choose to include formal disclaimers** in client documents stating that they are in the business only of **executing trades** for clients. If such disclaimers are made, they should ensure **procedures and systems** are set up to ensure they **do not provide any advice**.

**Disclaimers do not remove liability.** All firms and representatives remain subject to the same rules and regulations, and a disclaimer stating that it is the client's responsibility to ensure validity of a recommendation **will not remove liabilities** imposed under the SFA or the FAA. MAS will enforce those provisions to ensure clients enjoy the protection accorded to them.

### 6.4.5 Execution

After profiling the client and formulating the strategy, the firm, its representatives and the management team **create the portfolio**. Depending on how actively involved the client chooses to be, the portfolio is structured with the firm and representatives and **most likely a portfolio management team**.

Questions to consider when executing the strategy:

- What is the **size** of the client's investments or trades?
- What is the **frequency and speed** of trades being made?
- What are the **trading costs** — commissions, bid-ask spread, price impact?
- Are **derivatives used to reduce or enhance** the risks taken?

A good trading strategy considers **three factors**:

- **i.** **Small, liquidity-oriented trades** can be packaged and executed via **direct market access and algorithmic trading**. **Direct market access** refers to platforms permitting buy-side representatives to **directly access equities, fixed income, futures and FX markets** via access sponsored by the broker.
- **ii.** **Larger trades can receive custom handling.** Large, information-laden trades demand **immediate skilled attention** and experienced representatives, who must manage the **trade-off between impact and delay costs** by **releasing the minimum amount of information into the market** required to get the trade done.
- **iii.** In addition to **best execution**, representatives must be cognisant of **client trading restrictions, cash balances and brokerage allocations**.

### 6.4.6 Risk management skills in dealing

**Risk management** is the practice of **setting and maintaining acceptable loss levels** given a person's **capital, trading strategy and personal risk tolerance**. Effective risk management should be conducted **on every trade** and ideally **monitored over long-term periods**. The difference between a professional representative and the rest is the **ability to adhere to strict guidelines on risk**.

#### 6.4.6.1 Stop loss

A **stop loss** is not only the most basic risk management tool for a professional representative but also the **most important**. It is a **price at which the representative has decided a loss is too large** and the trade must be exited. Once the price hits the stop-loss level, the position is **automatically liquidated** and the loss accepted — preventing the representative from **holding on to losing positions that have already exceeded his risk limits**.

#### 6.4.6.2 Daily loss limit

A **daily loss limit** sets a **maximum loss for any given day**. When the limit is reached the representative must either **liquidate all positions** or be **prevented from entering new positions**. This protects against **further impulsive trades and excessive risk-taking to recoup losses**.

#### 6.4.6.3 Value at Risk (VaR)

**VaR** is a **statistical technique used to quantify the level of risk within a portfolio within a specific time frame**. Representatives use it to determine the **extent of possible losses** in their portfolios or positions. VaR can be applied to **specific positions, portfolios, or even as a firm-wide risk exposure measure**.

### 6.4.7 Performance monitoring and evaluation

Implementation also refers to **monitoring progress of the trading strategy** and how best to **execute changes** in light of changing market conditions and investment returns. The firm and its representatives should ensure **regular communication with the client whenever changes are made**.

They should also keep watch where the portfolio is **actively handled by a discretionary portfolio manager**:

- What kind of **risks** is the portfolio manager taking?
- What kind of **returns** is the portfolio manager making?
- Is the portfolio manager **outperforming or underperforming**?

A summarised performance monitoring system runs: **establish a performance management plan → monitor performance → record, analyse and explain variances → create a report on action for improvement → improve and refine the portfolio under market conditions** — a continuing loop.

## 6.5 Customer account and know-your-customer requirements

Where the customer is a **retail customer** wishing to trade in **Specified Investment Products (SIPs)**, the firm and its representatives shall **formally assess the customer's knowledge and experience** before allowing him to trade. Such assessment is termed a **Customer Account Review (CAR)** or **Customer Knowledge Assessment (CKA)**.

A CAR shall be conducted **before opening an account** for a customer to transact any **listed SIPs**. For the CAR, the firm and its representatives shall take into consideration the customer's **educational qualifications, investment experience and work experience**. Where a customer **does not provide adequate information**, the firm and its representatives shall **deem the customer not to possess knowledge or experience** in derivatives.

## 6.6 Specified Investment Products trading account

SIPs are **complex products**, commonly derivatives or structured products. They have **complex features and risks** which expose investors to **more factors that can cause a loss**. Some SIPs are listed on an exchange; others are not.

| Examples of SIPs **listed** on an exchange | Examples of SIPs **not listed** on an exchange |
|---|---|
| Certain **exchange-traded funds and notes** | **Structured notes** (equity-linked, credit-linked) |
| **Structured warrants** | Certain **collective investment schemes (CIS)** |
| **Futures** | Certain **investment-linked life insurance policies (ILPs)** |
| **Certificates** | |

### 6.6.1 Customer Account Review and Customer Knowledge Assessment

CAR or CKA assessments are required for any customer wishing to trade **listed or unlisted SIPs**, because not all customers have the knowledge or experience to assess a SIP's complex features and risks.

When conducting the CAR or CKA, a **retail customer is deemed to have sufficient knowledge** if he:

- **i.** holds a **diploma or higher qualification** in **accountancy, actuarial science, business / business administration / business management / business studies, capital markets, commerce, economics, finance, financial engineering, financial planning, computational finance or insurance**;
- **ii.** has **professional finance-related qualifications**, such as **Chartered Financial Analyst (CFA)** or **Association of Chartered Certified Accountants (ACCA)**;
- **iii.** **for CAR:** has **transacted in SIPs listed or quoted on an exchange at least 6 times in the preceding 3 years**;
- **iv.** **for CKA:** has **invested or transacted in unlisted CIS or ILPs at least 6 times in the preceding 3 years**, or in **unlisted SIPs (excluding CIS and ILPs) at least 6 times in the preceding 3 years**; or
- **v.** has a **minimum of 3 consecutive years of working experience in the past 10 years** in the **development, structuring, management, sale, trading, research or analysis of investment products, or the provision of training in investment products**. **Work experience in accountancy, actuarial science, treasury or financial risk management** is also considered relevant.

Where a customer is assessed **not** to possess knowledge or experience, but **subsequently demonstrates sufficient understanding** of listed SIP features and risks through:

- the **SGX Online Education Programme on SIPs**; or
- passing the **CMFAS modules CM-SIP, 8A or 9A**,

the customer **may be deemed to possess the knowledge** to transact in **listed SIPs**.

For **unlisted SIPs**, the equivalent route is a **learning module provided by the Association of Banks in Singapore and the Securities Association of Singapore** (the relevant **ABS-SAS learning module**), or passing **CMFAS modules CM-SIP, M8A or M9A**. In this regard the firm and its representatives are required to **obtain and consider**:

- **i.** the customer's **acknowledgement that he has personally undergone and passed** the assessment or examination;
- **ii.** the customer's **previous score(s)** of every assessment or examination taken; and
- **iii.** information relating to the **number of times the customer has not been allowed to transact** in that unlisted SIP by another firm.

### 6.6.2 Approval to open SIP trading accounts

The firm and its representatives **must not open a SIP trading account** unless **senior management** who is **not involved in that particular account opening process** and **not a connected person of the customer** is **satisfied, based on the outcome of the CAR or CKA**, that the customer has knowledge or experience in listed SIPs (for CAR) or unlisted SIPs (for CKA), and **has approved** the account opening.

**Regardless of the CAR outcome**, the firm and its representatives shall include in the account opening form a statement that the customer **can, at any time, request advice** concerning a SIP — and upon such request they shall **provide advice**.

**Even with a positive CKA**, the firm and its representatives should **offer to provide advice** on the unlisted SIP. If the customer **does not wish to receive advice**:

- the firm must **document the customer's decision in writing**;
- **highlight to the customer that it is the customer's responsibility** to ensure suitability of the product selected;
- **warn the customer in writing** that the customer chose not to receive advice; and
- **confirm in writing** if the customer wishes to proceed without advice.

A firm that **provides advice** to customers **must also be an exempt financial adviser**.

### 6.6.3 Requirements where a customer is assessed not to possess knowledge or experience

The firm and its representatives shall **inform the customer of the outcome of the CAR** where he is assessed not to possess knowledge or experience. If the customer intends to **proceed to open a listed SIP trading account**, they shall:

- **i.** **inform the customer in writing** of the outcome of the CAR;
- **ii.** **obtain the customer's written confirmation** that he still intends to proceed despite not possessing knowledge or experience;
- **iii.** **explain the general features and risks** of investing in derivatives and **provide a written statement of the explanation given**; and
- **iv.** **inform the customer in writing** that **it is the customer's responsibility** to ensure he understands any capital markets product he intends to transact using the account.

For an **unlisted SIP**, the firm and its representatives shall:

- **i.** **inform the customer in writing** of the outcome of the CKA and that **it is unable to proceed to transact in the unlisted SIP on the customer's behalf unless the firm is also an exempt financial adviser**; and
- **ii.** where the firm **is** also an exempt financial adviser, **provide financial advisory services in accordance with the standards in the MAS Notice on Recommendations on Investment Products (FAA-N16)** issued under the FAA.

### 6.6.4 Validity of the CAR outcome

The firm and its representatives **must not allow a customer to transact in a listed SIP** through the Listed SIP trading account **after 3 years from the date of the CAR**, unless:

- **i.** they have **checked and are satisfied** that the customer has transacted in a listed SIP through the account **more than once during the preceding 3-year period** and **more than once during each subsequent 3-year period**; or
- **ii.** they have **conducted a new CAR** for the customer.

### 6.6.5 Validity of the CKA outcome

Where a customer is assessed to have knowledge or experience to transact in an **unlisted SIP**, the firm and its representatives may allow the customer to transact for **a period of one year from the date of the CKA assessment**. **After a year has elapsed**, they shall **conduct a new CKA** before transacting on the customer's behalf in any unlisted SIP.

### 6.6.6 Documentation and record keeping

The firm and its representatives shall **document every CAR and CKA** conducted for each customer. The documentation must include:

- **i.** **information collected** on the customer's **educational qualification, work experience and investment experience**;
- **ii.** an **assessment of the customer's knowledge and experience** in derivatives or unlisted SIPs, as the case may be;
- **iii.** the **outcome** of the CAR or CKA; and
- **iv.** the **approval of senior management or designated person** to open the SIP trading account, where applicable.

Where the firm transacts in **any SIP listed or quoted on a securities or futures market** on behalf of a customer, it must **maintain records of all communication** with the customer in respect of the relevant trade — including a **file note or a tape recording of the telephone conversation**.

### 6.6.7 Requirements for dealing in overseas-listed investment products

Overseas-listed investment products carry a **different set of risks and levels of protection**. The firm and its representatives are therefore required to **warn retail customers of the possible risks before the customer's first purchase** of an overseas-listed investment product. They must:

- **provide the risk warning statement set out in Annex 4 of the MAS Notice on Sales of Investment Products** (reproduced as **Appendix C**); and
- **obtain the customer's acknowledgement** of that statement, **in written form or otherwise**, before allowing the customer to **transact for the first time**.

For this purpose, **"transact"** means **(a)** the **purchase** of any overseas-listed investment product **other than in connection with the creation of short positions**, or **(b)** the **sale** of any overseas-listed investment product **in connection with the creation of short positions**.

### 6.6.8 Risk warning statement

The statement highlights key risks customers should be aware of when trading overseas-listed products, such as:

- the **level of investor protection and safeguards** in the relevant foreign jurisdiction;
- **differences between the legal systems** in that jurisdiction and Singapore;
- **tax implications, currency risks and additional transaction costs**;
- exposure to **counterparty and correspondent broker risks**; and
- **political, economic and social developments** that may influence overseas markets.

Records of the customer's **acknowledgement must be kept for not less than 5 years**.

**EIP classification.** Where the firm offers an overseas-listed investment product, it **may implement a system** to identify and determine that the product is to be classified as an **Excluded Investment Product (EIP)**.

- If the firm **does not implement** such a system, the overseas-listed product **shall be classified as a SIP**, and the **CAR requirements apply**.
- If the firm **has classified** a product as an EIP, it must ensure the classification **remains accurate and current at all times**.
- A firm **may outsource** identification and classification to another party — but where outsourced, the firm **remains responsible for implementation of the classification system, including the accuracy of the classification**.

## 6.7 Risk management

This section delineates the types of risk and risk management associated with CMS licence holders. Effective risk management and sound internal controls rest largely on the **firm at a macro level** — including the **board's oversight** of risk management and **senior management's responsibility** to ensure practices are sound, effective and robust — and finally on **individuals** carrying out duties in risk management, compliance and audit functions.

All stakeholders should be aware of the different types of risk that can affect them. For CMS licence holders, the importance of risk management lies in ensuring a **framework is in place** should an unexpected event impact them internally.

### 6.7.1 Operational risk

**Operational risk** is the risk of loss from **internal processes** — the breakdown of internal processes, systems and people. It is **independent of other types of risk** such as financial, systematic or market-wide risk.

Operational risk management has become increasingly important as firms **modify internal processes and their products**. As processes and products develop at a rapid pace, operational risk increases, and regulators have become **more stringent about firms taking excessive risk** that may affect them financially and operationally.

> **Example — collapse of Barings Bank.** Barings Bank, a British bank based in London, **suffered massive losses and collapsed in 1995** as a result of a **rogue trader** who engaged in **unauthorised trading**, his speculative trades in futures suffering massive losses. The rogue trader was not only the **floor manager** on the trading exchange but also the unit's **head of settlement operations**, which effectively allowed him to **settle his own trades**. Barings had thus **compromised its accounting and internal controls**, which led to its downfall.

To minimise the impact of operational disruptions, firms should establish a **robust business continuity framework** with the following measures:

- **i.** **operational risk management policies and strategies** to identify, monitor and control operational risk, consistent with the firm's **capital and risk profile** and taking into account current market conditions;
- **ii.** **reporting mechanisms to notify MAS** of significant operational problems and breakdowns;
- **iii.** **management processes** focused on **effective recovery and resumption of critical business functions** following a disruption; and
- **iv.** **procedures ensuring all critical processes are properly documented** and staff adequately trained, with **controls to detect deviation from policies** as these could indicate a potential gap in the function performed.

### 6.7.2 Market risk

**Market risk** is the risk of loss resulting from **movement of market prices** — changes in **FX rates, interest rates, credit spreads, equity and commodity prices**. When trading a derivatives product, representatives make decisions and take positions based on their own investment analysis, whether fundamental or technical. Any investment may produce negative returns. One way to mitigate market risk is to **diversify and invest in different asset classes**.

Market risk is **highly related to other risks** such as credit risk or market-liquidity risk — for example a **credit rating downgrade** of a listed company leads to a drop in its share price. Firms should be aware of the market risk affecting their investments and implement a **robust risk management strategy**, always considering:

- **i.** **macroeconomic, market and liquidity conditions** that can contribute to market risk;
- **ii.** the firm's **investment portfolio** and how it would be affected if market risk increased; and
- **iii.** whether a **framework is in place to identify, measure, evaluate, monitor, report and control** market risk.

#### 6.7.2.1 Correlation risk

**Correlation risk** refers to the **correlation between different derivatives instruments**, which may affect a representative's trades. For example, a representative speculating on **Gold Futures** must be aware of **other instruments that can affect the trade, such as the US Dollar**. To manage correlation risk, the representative must **reduce positions that are correlated** with the derivatives product traded.

#### 6.7.2.2 Political and legal risk

**Political or legal risk** arises from **political or regulatory changes in a country**. Such risks exist everywhere and vary in magnitude. They may arise from **changes in government policies** — controls on exchange and interest rates, or government intervention on prices, outputs and activities. Events outside government control, such as **revolts or terrorism**, may contribute to political risk and affect prices of financial instruments associated with that country.

#### 6.7.2.3 Gap risk

**Gap risk** is the risk that **prices change from one level to another with no trading in between**. Gaps usually occur **after an event**, such as adverse news announcements, which can cause the price to plummet, because representatives buy or sell at **extreme prices** after reacting to new information. Representatives should note that **gaps can fall below stop-loss levels**, so positions must be liquidated **possibly well below the stop-loss**.

### 6.7.3 Capital risk

**Capital risk** is the potential risk that a client's account can **lose part or all of his principal invested**. It is **inherent to all types of investments and trades**; there is a possibility of losing the **entire principal** due to unforeseen circumstances if positions have **full exposure**. In trading accounts dealing with **margins**, the loss **may even exceed the principal invested**, because the representative relies on **borrowed capital** to make the trades.

### 6.7.4 Technology risk

With rapid growth in information technology, firms have expanded service offerings to clients through system operations and mobile technology. But increasing reliance on **complex IT systems** makes them **more susceptible to cyber-attacks and system shutdowns**. Firms should **continually improve cyber security and technology risk management capabilities**.

MAS has issued **technology risk management guidelines**, under which firms must ensure the **maximum unscheduled downtime for critical IT systems does not exceed a total of 4 hours within any 12-month period**. Firms must also:

- **liaise closely with MAS** on their framework and processes for monitoring critical IT systems and **system recovery testing**;
- **notify MAS as soon as possible, and not later than 1 hour**, upon discovering a relevant incident relating to a **system malfunction or IT security incident** that has a **severe and widespread impact** on operations or **materially impacts service to customers**.

In addition, firms must comply with **cyber security requirements** on:

- **i.** **securing administrative accounts**;
- **ii.** **applying security patching**;
- **iii.** **establishing baseline security standards**;
- **iv.** **deploying network security devices**; and
- **v.** **implementing anti-malware measures and strengthening user authentication**.

### 6.7.5 Credit risk

**Credit risk** is the risk of loss that an **obligor (for example a client) will fail to meet financial obligations** in accordance with agreed terms. It can arise from **both on- and off-balance-sheet transactions**. A firm can be exposed to credit risk from **FX, futures, swaps, bonds, options and other derivatives or trade finance products**.

The goal of credit risk management is to **maximise the risk-adjusted rate of return by maintaining credit risk exposure within acceptable parameters**. Firms need to manage credit risk inherent in the **entire portfolio** as well as in **individual credits or transactions**. Effective credit risk management is a **critical component of a comprehensive risk management approach** and essential to long-term success.

**Credit or counterparty risk** arises when one party to a derivatives contract, buyer or seller, **defaults**. Derivatives may be traded **OTC, which is less regulated than exchanges**. The most direct way to reduce counterparty risk is to **reduce credit exposure**; the aim is to **minimise potential losses**, and representatives should always **take risk limits into account** when trading derivatives.

### 6.7.6 Liquidity risk

**Liquidity risk** is the risk of a firm being **unable to meet short-term financial obligations** — arising from the **inability to convert financial assets into cash without incurring unacceptable losses of capital**. Liquidity risk often arises **from other risks such as credit risk** — for example, huge credit losses from loan defaults could cause major liquidity problems and affect creditworthiness.

To manage liquidity risk, firms should **establish, constantly review and monitor funding policies and strategy**. The policy should also highlight how **other risks such as credit or operational risk** can affect the funding strategy.

For **representatives**, liquidity risk may occur when they have entered a trade and plan to **close out a position**. During periods of **low trading volume**, **bid-ask spreads may be higher**, because the representative needs higher compensation for handling the transaction. If the product is **less frequently traded or less liquid**, there may be **significant cost**. To reduce liquidity risk, the representative can trade in **derivatives that are less volatile and more liquid**.

Firms can also incorporate these measures:

- **i.** **analysis of funding requirements** over different scenarios;
- **ii.** **maintenance of unencumbered, highly liquid assets** usable as a funding source in uncertain situations;
- **iii.** **diversification of funding sources** and regular efforts to establish and maintain those relationships; and
- **iv.** **regular review of the ability to liquefy assets**.

### 6.7.7 Regulatory risk

**Regulatory risk** is the risk that a **change in laws and regulations** in a country or region can impact a company, industry sector or market. Changes can **increase operating costs or reduce the attractiveness of investment**. An example is **increased stress-test requirements** imposed on firms, requiring more stringent **capital preservation** and sufficient **capital and liquidity**.

> **Example — the Dodd-Frank Act.** The **Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act** was passed by the US Government in **2010** in response to the **Global Financial Crisis of 2008**. It aims to **protect consumers from predatory mortgage lending** and ensure **financial stability of major firms** with considerable market influence. While such reforms can ensure more stability, they may also **restrict financial institutions from conducting certain types of business** — for example the **Volcker Rule** banned **proprietary trading** and restricted investment in **hedge funds and private equity firms**, which may limit investment flows from both buy-side and sell-side firms.

Firms must comply with the regulations in place, and can **minimise regulatory risk by anticipating the outcomes of regulatory impact** and **diversifying the business** to other areas of the market.

### 6.7.8 Interconnected risk

**Interconnected risk** arises from **how institutions and systems are connected** such that if one entity fails, the rest fail along with it. On a larger scale it is referred to as **systemic risk**, where one entity or event could **trigger instability or collapse of an entire industry or economy**.

For example, during the **2008 Global Financial Crisis**, several **bulge-bracket banks** had **excess exposure to mortgage-backed securities and housing-related assets**. When America's housing market tanked, these multinational banks **suffered severe losses and triggered a worldwide financial crisis**. Representatives must therefore be **familiar with the interconnected risks** they are exposed to when trading an asset class.

### 6.7.9 Provision of support to resolve complex risks

While financial institutions must have their **own risk management systems** to resolve complex risks, **regulatory authorities such as MAS also provide regulatory oversight**. MAS is the **overall integrated supervisor** of the financial services sector; one of its main roles is to **keep track of the different types of risk** associated with financial institutions and **provide ample support to resolve them**.

To achieve this, MAS has created the **Comprehensive Risk Assessment Framework and Techniques (CRAFT)**. This **risk-based supervision** seeks to **reduce the impact of systemic risks** that can greatly affect the capital markets. Through an **Impact and Risk Model**, MAS evaluates the **potential risk a financial institution can bring**, prioritising supervisory resources to firms that can potentially have a **huge impact on the financial markets**. The risks assessed are those in Section 6.7 but are **not exhaustive**.

After deriving the **overall risk rating**, MAS develops a **supervisory plan** setting out **particular areas of coverage** and the **selection of tools** to address risks and take corrective action. Areas of coverage include identifying areas for supervisory attention — significant activities, risk management processes and governance issues.

MAS also has:

- **diagnostic tools** to identify problems at financial institutions **at an early stage**; and
- **remedial tools**, involving **collaborating with the financial institution** to achieve a specific outcome.

Both types of tool **must complement each other**: diagnostic work confirms whether a particular risk is **relevant and material**, while remedial work sets **clear plans to mitigate risks**, best achieved **within a specific timeframe**.

### 6.7.10 Management of anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism

The AML/CFT regime in Singapore, and the principles of due diligence and client onboarding, are discussed in Chapter 7.

#### 6.7.10.1 Comprehension and assessment of AML/CFT risks

Sound AML/CFT risk management requires **identifying, measuring and evaluating** the AML/CFT risks present within the firm and **implementing policies commensurate with the identified risks**. A firm should develop a **thorough understanding of the AML/CFT risks present within its client base, products and services, delivery channels, and the areas in which clients conduct their businesses**.

#### 6.7.10.2 Proper governance

For AML/CFT risk management to be effective there must be **proper governance**. **Responsibility lies with the board of directors** to oversee risk management policies and compliance, ensuring all AML/CFT risks are accounted for. The board should have a **comprehensive understanding of AML/CFT risks**, and any information an employee receives should be **communicated to the board in a timely, accurate and complete manner** so it can make **informed decisions**.

#### 6.7.10.3 Transaction monitoring system

A firm should have a **monitoring system to track incoming and outgoing transactions**, **commensurate with its size, activities and the potential risks** associated with it. At any point in time the system should give the firm access to a **centralised information library**, enabling it to **risk-rate clients and monitor their transactions** with the relevant information at its disposal.

#### 6.7.10.4 Customer acceptance and risk profiling

Firms usually implement **client acceptance and risk profiling policies and procedures** to **identify clients or prospective clients likely to pose a higher risk** of ML and TF threats. In assessing AML/CFT risk, the firm should thoroughly check the client's **personal details — background, occupation, source of funding and country of origin**.

Such policies require **basic due diligence from the personnel conducting the checks**. However, the firm must ensure the policies are **not so restrictive that they hinder access to its services by the general public**.

## Exam cues

- **The two exceptions to the reasonable-basis requirement**: merely executing client instructions without recommendation, and recommending a **listed excluded investment product** under FAR Regulation 33A conditions (rationale provided, notice that advice ignores objectives and needs, client assesses suitability).
- **Fundamental analysis fits long-horizon equity investing; technical analysis fits short-horizon strategies.** The exam tests the matching.
- **Five-step risk management framework**: identification, assessment, evaluation, response, reporting. Note the fourth expressly requires **considering relationships between individual risks**.
- **Rebalancing that shifts weightages to exploit mispricing or strong sectors is tactical asset allocation** — an active strategy, not strategic allocation.
- **Three dealing risk tools**: **stop loss** (most basic and most important), **daily loss limit** (liquidate or no new positions), **VaR** (statistical, time-frame specific, applicable to positions, portfolios or firm-wide).
- **Records for recommendations should be kept at least 5 years** as best practice; **risk warning acknowledgements must be kept not less than 5 years** by statute.
- **A conflict of interest need not be re-disclosed each time** if the prior disclosure remains current and the client can reasonably be expected to be aware of it — but **if in doubt, disclose**.
- **A disclaimer does not remove SFA or FAA liability.** That is the examinable point.
- **SIP listed versus unlisted examples** are a common question: ETFs and notes, structured warrants, futures and certificates on the listed side; structured notes, certain CIS, certain ILPs on the unlisted side.
- **Sufficient-knowledge routes**: a qualifying diploma or higher in a listed discipline; CFA or ACCA; **6 transactions in the preceding 3 years** (listed SIPs for CAR; unlisted CIS/ILPs **or** other unlisted SIPs for CKA); or **3 consecutive years of relevant work experience in the past 10 years**, including accountancy, actuarial science, treasury or financial risk management.
- **Alternative routes to deemed knowledge**: SGX Online Education Programme or CMFAS **CM-SIP, 8A or 9A** for listed; ABS-SAS learning module or CMFAS **CM-SIP, M8A or M9A** for unlisted. Note the different module numbers — 8A/9A versus M8A/M9A.
- **Senior management approval** for SIP account opening must come from someone **not involved in that account opening** and **not a connected person** of the customer.
- **CAR validity is 3 years**, extended only if the customer transacted **more than once in each 3-year period**. **CKA validity is 1 year**, with a fresh CKA required after that.
- **Failure to provide adequate information means the customer is deemed not to possess knowledge or experience** — the default runs against the customer.
- **"Transact" for overseas-listed products** excludes purchases connected with creating short positions and includes sales connected with creating short positions.
- **EIP classification outsourcing does not transfer responsibility**; without a classification system the product is a SIP and CAR applies.
- **Technology risk numbers**: maximum unscheduled downtime **4 hours in any 12-month period**; MAS notification **not later than 1 hour** after discovery.
- **Capital risk in margin accounts can exceed the principal**, because borrowed capital is used — a favourite trap.
- **Gap risk can blow through a stop-loss**, so liquidation may occur well below the stop level.
- **CRAFT is MAS's risk-based supervisory framework** with an Impact and Risk Model; **diagnostic and remedial tools complement each other**.
- **Trap:** operational risk is **independent of** financial, systematic or market-wide risk — the definition is narrower than "any loss from doing business".
