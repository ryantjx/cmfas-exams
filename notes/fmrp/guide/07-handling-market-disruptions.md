# Handling Market Disruptions

**Learning objectives.** The candidate should be able to:

- identify what constitutes a market disruption;
- recognise the impact of such disruptions;
- discuss the alternative communication channels and procedures for managing trading activity during such disruptions;
- recognise the SFEMC's roles during market disruptions;
- describe the rules of the central clearing party during market disruptions.

## 7.1 Introduction

Market activities can be disrupted by unforeseen events from time to time. In such circumstances **both trading and settlement operations will be adversely affected**, impacting the **entire market or a significant portion of the trading population**.

Each disruption event **bears its own characteristics** — a natural catastrophe, government actions, civil disorders and so on. It is therefore **not possible to predict** the manner in which market participants should conduct their business. This chapter outlines key principles on handling market disruption events.

## 7.2 General description

**Market disruption events** refer to circumstances where **normal trading or settlement of financial transactions are inhibited, or have become impossible or illegal**, due to **governmental actions, natural catastrophes, civil disorders or other causes beyond the control of any individual market participant**. The exact period of disruption **may or may not be determinable**.

Market disruptions may be caused by exceptional events such as the following, noting that the list is **not exhaustive**:

- **i. Unscheduled non-business days.** Every country or city has its own schedule of public holidays and non-business days, usually **announced and gazetted well in advance**. However, unforeseen circumstances — **epidemic outbreaks, serious civil disorder, havoc caused by severe weather or natural catastrophes, terrorist attacks** — may force the government of a country or city to **declare a non-business day** where the country or city cannot function normally.
- **ii. Unforeseen market events.** An unforeseen event may trigger a **sudden wave of collapse of financial institutions or large corporations**, affecting the stability of financial markets. It may cause a **liquidity squeeze, a collapse in equity markets, or default in payment by debt issuers**. Such circumstances may lead to **panic among market participants**, who may be forced to take **unusual steps to contain risk or reduce market exposure** to prevent financial loss.
- **iii. Sudden changes of national practices or policies by the government or central bank.** This refers to a **sudden and dramatic change of policy or practice** impacting the normal functioning of financial markets. For example, during the **Asian financial crisis of 1997–1998**, the **Malaysian authorities unilaterally pegged the USD/MYR exchange rate at MYR 3.8000 per USD**, causing **significant confusion** in markets that had until then traded a freely floating USD/MYR.

## 7.3 The role of the SFEMC

The **role of the SFEMC** in a market disruption event **depends on the nature of the event and the magnitude of its impact** on the wholesale financial markets in Singapore. It is generally envisaged that the SFEMC would **work closely with market participants, related industry bodies both locally and overseas, and regulators** to **minimise uncertainties and impact** on trading and settlement operations.

In the event of **significant market disruptions**, the SFEMC will, **where appropriate, endeavour to make clear recommendations on appropriate settlement arrangements** for affected transactions. **All members of the SFEMC agree to adopt such recommendations to the fullest extent possible**, taking into consideration all relevant circumstances.

**The SFEMC's role is limited.** Notwithstanding the above, its role is limited to **offering practical recommendations to market participants to minimise the risks created by a disruption**. Market participants must still **negotiate bilaterally with their counterparties in accordance with any contractual terms agreed between them** to achieve a satisfactory resolution of affected transactions or obligations — though they are **strongly encouraged to adopt the SFEMC's recommendations**.

**Actions the SFEMC has taken in the past** to manage market disruptions:

- **i.** **convened a meeting of members** to discuss issues;
- **ii.** **developed a proposal** on appropriate measures and codes of conduct to manage market disruptions;
- **iii.** **discussed the proposal with regulators and other relevant market participants** — clearing houses, exchanges — to **get their consent to implement** it;
- **iv.** **issued statements** to get market participants to follow the announced measures and codes of conduct; and
- **v.** where a **market fixing arrangement is required**, **made clear recommendations on procedures to determine the fixing rate** and **monitored the behaviour or actions** of market participants to ensure compliance with those recommendations **to the fullest extent possible**.

## 7.4 Communication during market disruptions

Market participants should, **to the fullest extent possible, maintain effective communication with their counterparts and other related parties** — **settlement agents, clearing platforms, customers and MAS** — throughout a market disruption event. This applies **regardless of whether an operation remains in Singapore or has to be relocated elsewhere** because of the nature of the event.

**How the SFEMC disseminates information.** The SFEMC will endeavour to disseminate its recommendations or other information through the **most efficient means available**, including but not limited to:

- **announcements through its website**;
- **broadcasts through MASNET**; or
- **direct telephone contacts with, or e-mails to, senior management** of market participants.

Market participants are also **encouraged to establish contacts with the Secretary of the SFEMC** where needed.

## 7.5 Ethical standards and conduct during market disruptions

Market participants are expected to **uphold the highest standards of professionalism not only under normal market conditions but more so during a crisis**.

**All negotiations** between market participants should be carried out in **utmost good faith** and by persons **properly authorised** by their respective firms. It is **deemed bad practice for any market participant to withdraw from an agreement** on the ground that a representative **did not have sufficient authority** during the negotiation.

**Measures senior management should implement.** Senior management should ensure that:

- **i.** **dealing representatives are not using the disruption to speculate**;
- **ii.** **all deals are executed by authorised personnel**;
- **iii.** dealing representatives **honour and conclude trades at prices and rates confirmed with counterparties**, **even though he or she may suffer a loss** as markets move against the positions;
- **iv.** dealing representatives have **applied appropriate measures** — such as **telephones with voice recorders** or **using a dealing system where details of the deal are recorded** — to execute trades, since these can be used as **evidence to resolve trade disputes**; and
- **v.** **settlement of trades is done in accordance with market best practice and procedures**.

## 7.6 Settlement during market disruptions

The relevant **Master Agreements or other contractual documentation** between market participants **will typically prescribe settlement arrangements** to be applied during disruption events. In general, settlement during disruptions **should therefore always be guided by the relevant contractual obligations** laid down by the Master Agreement or other documentation between the parties.

### 7.6.1 Alternative settlement procedures

**FX transactions require settlement of two currencies on the same value date.** In the event that one currency's settlement is affected by a market disruption event, the settling parties should, **as far as possible, establish a clear understanding of each other's intention before the value date** in respect of settlement of the unaffected currency.

Alternative settlement practices and procedures that can be applied:

- **i.** the **value date, settlement date, maturity date or fixing date** of the trade falling on the specific date should be **adjusted by extending the date to the next business day**;
- **ii.** the **value dates in FX transactions may need to be dates where both parties agree**, or where **special local practice allows for split delivery**;
- **iii.** the affected parties should **agree to adjust the exchange rate according to the prevailing relevant swap mid-rate**;
- **iv.** **support staff must perform deal confirmations swiftly** to avoid any potential dispute with the dealing party; or
- **v.** the **settlement department must perform extra due diligence checks** — for example the recommended settlement procedures given by the **SFEMC or MAS** — **before making payments** on affected trades with counterparties.

### 7.6.2 Unilateral suspensions of settlement are to be avoided

**Unilateral suspension of settlements not only increases credit risk but might also trigger systemic risk**, and as such **should be avoided**.

Market participants should **not perform unilateral suspension of settlements by not fulfilling settlement obligations to counterparties**. If a counterparty fails to receive cash or security on a settlement date, it **may in turn be unable to settle another transaction with another party**. This failed settlement generates **additional credit risk exposure to trade counterparties**, and the **chain effect of a series of failed settlements might trigger systemic risk**.

The source illustration is the failure of **hedge funds to meet some settlement obligations**, which contributed to the **collapse of Lehman Brothers** and the chain effect on US and other global financial markets. Unilateral suspension should therefore be avoided.

### 7.6.3 SGD settlement disruptions

Where **settlements in Singapore dollars have been disrupted** by unforeseen events, market participants should **follow closely the instructions issued by MAS**.

## 7.7 Central counterparty rules

In an effort to **reduce pre-settlement risk**, central banks around the world have **mandated that long-dated FX forward and interest rate swap OTC transactions be cleared on a central counterparty (CCP) or clearing house**. This requires **both counterparties to place margin deposits with the clearing house, proportionate to their exposures**, which can be used to **cover unrealised losses in the event of default by either counterparty**.

Market participants should **follow the applicable rules of the relevant CCP in relation to market disruptions**, where transactions have been registered with a CCP for clearing.

## Exam cues

- **The definition of a market disruption turns on three elements**: normal trading **or** settlement is **inhibited, impossible or illegal**; the cause is **governmental action, natural catastrophe, civil disorder or other cause beyond any individual participant's control**; and the period **may or may not be determinable**.
- **The three named event types** are unscheduled non-business days, unforeseen market events, and sudden changes of national practices or policies by a government or central bank.
- **The Malaysian peg of MYR 3.8000 per USD during the 1997–1998 Asian financial crisis** is the source's example of a sudden policy change — a likely factual question.
- **SFEMC's role is recommendatory, not binding.** It convenes, proposes, consults, issues statements and recommends fixing procedures — but participants still **negotiate bilaterally under their contracts**, albeit being **strongly encouraged** to adopt the recommendations.
- **SFEMC dissemination channels**: website announcements, **MASNET** broadcasts, and direct telephone or e-mail contact with senior management; participants may also contact the **Secretary of the SFEMC**.
- **Five SFEMC past actions** are examinable in sequence: convene, propose, consult regulators and participants, issue statements, recommend and monitor fixing procedures.
- **Negotiations must be in utmost good faith and by properly authorised persons.** Withdrawing from an agreement because a representative **lacked authority** is expressly **bad practice**.
- **Honour confirmed prices and rates even at a loss** — that is the source's ethical standard during disruption, not an aspiration.
- **Voice recorders and dealing systems serve as evidence** to resolve trade disputes; that evidentiary purpose is the examinable reason for the control.
- **Five alternative settlement procedures for FX**: roll to next business day; agree value dates or split delivery where local practice allows; adjust the exchange rate to the prevailing **relevant swap mid-rate**; confirm swiftly; and apply extra due-diligence checks per SFEMC or MAS guidance before paying.
- **Unilateral suspension is prohibited in substance**, on the reasoning that failed settlements create credit exposure and can cascade into systemic risk — the **Lehman Brothers** chain effect is the illustration.
- **For SGD disruptions, follow MAS instructions** — not SFEMC recommendations, and not bilateral improvisation.
- **CCP clearing was mandated for long-dated FX forwards and interest rate swaps** to reduce **pre-settlement risk**, with margin proportionate to exposures covering unrealised losses on default.
- **Trap:** the SFEMC does **not** have power to compel. A question that offers "SFEMC may direct participants to settle" is wrong; it recommends, and participants are strongly encouraged to adopt.
- **Trap:** a disruption does **not** automatically entitle a party to suspend payment unilaterally — the applicable agreement, law or competent authority must authorise it.
