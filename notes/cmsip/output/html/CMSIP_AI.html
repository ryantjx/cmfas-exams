Chapter 1 — Overview of Derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose value is linked to the value or volatility of something else. This chapter builds the market map — the underlying asset classes, the exchange and OTC venues, the regulatory response to 2008, and Singapore's position — that every later chapter assumes.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Describe common underlying asset classes for derivatives and structured products.
  • Describe the different types of derivatives and their uses.
  • Describe the characteristics of exchange-traded and over-the-counter derivatives.
  • Discuss the development of the derivatives market in Singapore and beyond.

1.1 Introduction

A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is linked to the value or volatility of an underlying reference or asset. That reference may be a particular security, an asset, an interest rate, a combination of securities, a basket, an index, or another derivative. Derivatives trade either on centralised, regulated exchanges or bilaterally between counterparties in the less regulated over-the-counter (OTC) market.

Futures, forwards, swaps and options are the basic types. Beyond them sits a wide range of more complex or exotic derivatives, built by combining similar and different types. Barrier options, knock-in and knock-out products, swaptions, structured notes and structured funds are examples. By varying the features — strike price, tenure, currency, conditions for execution — and combining different derivatives, an issuer creates a wider range of payoff outcomes and risk profiles aimed at investors holding different views.

The economic uses are four:

  • Hedging — insuring against adverse price, rate, currency or credit movement.
  • Speculation — taking a view on the future direction of the market.
  • Arbitrage — profiting from perceived price differentials between two or more markets or assets.
  • Repackaging — combining instruments to meet specific investment needs and to create desired synthetic positions.

Investors also trade and hedge on volatility — their expectation of the degree of price movement, whether prices stay range-bound, swing wildly, or move steadily in one direction. As volatility trading on indices has grown, leveraged exchange-traded notes (ETNs) and leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have been created to amplify the impact of rising or falling percentage volatility. Many leveraged ETFs and ETNs actively use futures and options to replicate or track the direction — or the inverse direction — of the underlying index, which makes such funds and notes effectively derivative instruments.

Derivatives and structured products allow investors to:

  • a. Hedge or insure against unfavourable price movements, or potential falls in the value of assets or a portfolio already held — futures, forwards, options.
  • b. Adopt a view on the future direction of the underlying without investing directly in it.
  • c. Augment potential profits beyond what pure exposure to the underlying can deliver — options, warrants.
  • d. Change the payoff profile (cash-flow sequence, size, tenure, currency, risks) in ways the underlying asset cannot offer — swaps, futures, forwards, options.
  • e. Profit from a fall in the value of the underlying asset or index by taking short positions — options, futures.
  • f. Secure the right, without the obligation, to decide when to buy or sell the underlying, using a small outlay — long options and warrants. A short call or put is an obligation, not a right, should the buyer choose to exercise.
  • g. Arbitrage, or take advantage of price differentials between two or more markets.
  • h. Change the nature of an asset or liability to meet needs that standardised instruments cannot meet.
  • i. Create synthetic positions without incurring the cost of buying or selling the underlying.

Two footnotes matter for scope. A debt warrant resembles an equity or company warrant but attaches to a bond or other debt security, giving the holder the right to buy more bonds or debt securities from the same issuer at a stated price; only equity and company warrants are covered in Chapter 5. ETFs and ETNs are not derivatives, but they actively use derivative instruments to enhance or hedge — leveraged and volatility ETFs, for example, use futures and options to replicate or track an index.

1.2.1 Money Market and Other Short Term Interest Rate Instruments

These are short-term debt instruments issued by governments, financial institutions and corporations. They are highly liquid and less risky. They account for more than 95% of futures and options traded on global exchanges, and, according to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), consistently for more than three-quarters of OTC derivatives trades between 2015 and 2019.

Minimum denominations are relatively large and transaction sizes can be substantial. Maturities range from 1 day to 1 year, often under 90 days. The market is dominated by interbank lending and trading in Treasury bills, commercial paper, banker's acceptances, negotiable certificates of deposit and repurchase agreements. Institutional investors dominate.

Many instruments are benchmarked against interbank rates — SIBOR, LIBOR, HIBOR, TIBOR, EURIBOR — the most widely used historically being LIBOR (London Interbank Offered Rate). LIBOR is an average interbank lending rate calculated from submissions of actual borrowing rates contracted between banks, which makes it vulnerable to manipulation where submitting banks collude. Colluding to manipulate interbank rates and indices in order to engineer outcomes in the derivatives market is illegal, and traders and dealers have been prosecuted.

LIBOR was phased out and ceased to be used in 2021, replaced for dollar-denominated derivatives and loans by the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR).

1.2.2 Foreign Exchange

Cash investments in local or foreign currency are the lowest common denominator for all assets. FX derivatives form the second largest category of derivatives traded globally.

Currency forwards and futures are the most basic form, generating different payoffs depending on expected currency movement over time. Swaps and options change the underlying currency or interest payoff under conditional triggers, such as the exchange rate reaching a particular strike. Trading in currency forwards, futures, swaps and options is largely dominated by institutional investors.

In emerging markets with tightly controlled capital flows, inconvertible currency and low liquidity, hedging local-currency risk can be difficult. This is partly solved with a non-deliverable forward (NDF), created as a proxy for the emerging-market currency. NDFs trade OTC in offshore financial centres and settle in a foreign currency other than the NDF currency itself.

1.2.3 Fixed Income Securities

These are borrowings by issuers — mainly governments, government-linked entities or public institutions, financial institutions and large multinational corporations, spanning investment-grade and junk bonds. The holder provides a loan of principal to the issuer and receives periodic interest, with principal returned at maturity. Differences in size, frequency and timing of cash flow produce a return commensurate with the risk taken and the opportunity cost incurred. Fixed-income derivatives — futures, swaps and options — offer alternative cash flows, payoff profiles and modified risk exposures relative to the underlying instrument.

The industry has innovated continuously to populate the investment frontier, and investment banks in the 1980s popularised creative repackaging of debt for resale. Banks bought long-term fixed-income securities with higher yield and used them as the underlying asset base against which they repackaged and issued smaller denominations of marketable securities. Coupon payments of long-term Treasury bonds were stripped to form the asset base for issuing zero-coupon bonds. Portfolios of real-estate loans were bought and used to issue mortgage-backed securities (MBS). Consumer and student loans became the asset base for collateralised debt obligations (CDOs). Even without derivatives there was creative repackaging creating new payoff profiles; read broadly, various CDOs and stripped zero-coupon bonds are derived from other bonds and so are fixed-income derivatives.

By the late 1990s, credit derivative instruments were actively promoted. Investment banks repackaged and modified the credit risk of fixed-income securities using credit insurance and credit default swaps (CDS) issued by insurance companies. They also bought select high-yield securities — junk bonds rated CCC and below — mixed them with better-rated securities, and added CDS and credit insurance to create a new cocktail for resale.

The credit quality of the underlying asset, and the credit management process, ultimately determines the risk and value of the repackaged product. The 2008 Global Financial Crisis illustrated this dramatically: defaults on real-estate mortgage loans and CDOs triggered the collapse of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the bailout and sale of several global institutions, forced capital restructuring across the global banking community, and a subsequent massive rise in regulatory control and cost.

1.2.4 Equities

Equities let investors participate in company profitability. When earnings prospects and growth are positive the share price rises over time; when they are negative it declines.

Equities are more volatile than other traditional asset classes. The ability of equity derivatives to take short positions, or grant a right to sell, is particularly useful in hedging the downside volatility of stocks.

Equity futures let investors buy (long) or sell (short) equities for future delivery at an agreed price. Long equity call options and warrants give the choice to participate in upside with a smaller outlay, augmenting potential profit while limiting loss to that outlay. Long equity put options allow investors to take short positions.

In equity-linked structures, investors can attempt to enhance returns by selling put options to collect premium from willing counterparties when the market is flat. This secures yields much higher than typical deposit instruments, but the higher yield carries the obligation to own underperforming securities should they fall to critical trigger levels. Chapters 7 and 8 examine these instruments.

Note. Shorting put options is not a hedge against downside risk. A short put is a bullish or neutral position that loses as the underlying falls.

1.2.5 Commodities

Commodities are the original asset class that spurred the creation of derivatives. Unlike financial derivatives, a significant portion of commodity derivatives trading comes from regular hedging needs and opportunistic demand from agriculture, mining and manufacturing — real underlying business demand.

Supply and demand determine the long-run direction of each commodity, but short-term trading is influenced by other factors, notably the movement of the USD. Supply shocks make commodities volatile — the decisions of the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) on oil prices being the classic case — which makes the ability to take short positions valuable to a commodity investor.

By convention most commodity futures are denominated in USD. When the USD appreciates against other major currencies, commodity prices tend to move in the opposite direction, and vice versa. In extreme shocks to currencies or equity markets, safe-haven commodities — gold, silver and platinum — tend to rise. Futures and options on these have become increasingly popular both to hedge and to take advantage of the volatility.

1.2.6 Trading with Indices

Index-linked products give exposure to an asset class, market or security without buying its constituents. Futures, option contracts, ETFs and ETNs are pegged to equity indices, commodity indices and popular currency pairs.

Such indirect exposure lets investors:

  • a. Adopt cost-effective investment exposure, passively over the long term.
  • b. Hedge and protect profits, or minimise losses, on an existing outstanding position.
  • c. Take speculative short-term exposure to a short-term opportunity.
  • d. Augment exposure through leverage — typically 2–3× a reference index — using ETFs, ETNs, options, futures and warrants on the index of a particular underlying asset class.
  • e. Speculate on the potential volatility, or lack of volatility, of a security or an asset-class index.

Volatility itself became tradable. The Chicago Board Options Exchange Volatility Index (CBOE VIX, "VIX") measures the market's perceived volatility of the S&P 500 in either direction, derived from the price movement of call and put options. VIX options and futures trade on the CBOE daily. When volatility rises the VIX rises; when market volatility falls the VIX falls. VIX options and futures let investors trade volatility regardless of market direction.

Leveraged ETNs and ETFs on the VIX were introduced to widen access — ETNs such as XIV, ZIV, VIIX, VIIZ, TVIX, TVIZ, and ETFs such as SVXY, UVXY — allowing investors to buy or short volatility, trading the rise or fall in the percentage volatility of the S&P 500.

These structures are extremely risky. On 5 February 2018 the VIX spiked and more than doubled in a single day. The short-volatility ETF SVXY (ProShares) and XIV (Credit Suisse) each fell more than 80% in a single day, wiping out a few billion dollars overnight across just two instruments.

Trading in both was halted temporarily. There was no recovery for holders of the XIV ETN at the open, because a termination event — a price level — had been triggered and the issuing bank closed out the synthetic product to protect itself from further downside, forcing traders caught on the wrong side to realise losses. In subsequent weeks, under pressure to address the highly risky leveraged position its product created, ProShares cut its leveraging of the short and long volatility ETFs SVXY and UVXY by half.

1.3 Exchange Traded Derivatives

Exchange-traded derivatives are standardised products, mainly futures and options, traded on centralised, regulated exchanges such as Singapore Exchange Derivatives Trading (SGX-DT) or ICE Futures Singapore (IFSG) — as opposed to bilateral OTC markets. Each product's characteristics are fixed by standard convention: contract size, underlying asset, delivery or expiration dates, and delivery arrangements.

At expiry every contract is settled, either by physical delivery or by cash settlement. Once a trade is executed between a buyer and seller on the exchange, it is recorded with the exchange's clearing house. The contract is ultimately not between the original buyer and seller but between the holders at expiry and the clearing house, which guarantees settlement as originally intended using pooled initial margins from both sides of the trade.

Global open interest in exchange-traded derivatives, measured in USD trillions, is published by the BIS in its derivatives statistics; the series runs from 2007 through 2020 for futures and options.

1.4 Over-the-Counter Traded Derivatives

OTC derivatives trade directly between two parties without an exchange. Transactions are seldom terminated or assigned — transferred to a third party — before maturity unless both counterparties agree. Banks, other large financial institutions, fund managers and corporations are the main participants. Historically the two parties settle bilaterally.

Before the credit crisis that began in 2007, the OTC derivatives market was largely unregulated. In 2018, OTC derivatives trading was estimated at more than five times the value of derivatives traded on global exchanges.

Currency and interest-rate forwards and swaps make up the bulk of OTC derivatives. OTC currency forwards and swaps are driven largely by trades between international banks; corporates hedging underlying currency positions and some sophisticated investors also participate, but they are the minority.

Credit default swaps are the next largest product group. Until 2007, CDS grew in popularity as institutions used credit insurance to enhance the credit rating and pricing of bonds. This "selling off risk" became a lucrative business: insurers and financial institutions that sold credit insurance generated good revenues as long as there was no major systemic downturn. When mortgage-backed securities began to collapse, CDS — profitable for buyers — became a disaster for sellers, who were obliged to pay out on the insurance they had provided against default.

1.4.1 Regulation of OTC Derivatives Trading

Post-mortem reviews of the 2008 crisis attributed much of the blame to the proliferation of products and to the lack of monitoring and regulation of OTC financial derivatives — especially credit derivatives, sold by investment banks to institutional clients and cascading down to private and retail clients. Instruments that began as tools for risk management became instruments for speculative gain. Warren Buffett's 2002 warning is the most quoted: derivatives are "financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal."

In September 2009 the G20 leaders agreed to push for reform of the global financial system. The derivative commitments were:

  • greater transparency in derivatives trading;
  • moving more standardised OTC contracts onto exchanges or electronic trading platforms;
  • clearing through central counterparties (CCPs);
  • reporting to trade repositories; and
  • higher capital requirements for non-centrally cleared contracts.

1.4.1.1 Europe

In 2012 the European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR) set the framework for regulating OTC derivatives in Europe. In line with the G20 commitments, EMIR required European financial institutions and their counterparties to measure, report, monitor and clear obligations, and to manage operational risk and counterparty credit risk on OTC derivative transactions not cleared through a clearing house.

1.4.1.2 United Kingdom

In 2013 the UK passed the Financial Services (Banking Reform) Act, requiring major universal banks to separate and ring-fence retail and commercial banking from investment banking.

1.4.1.3 United States

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act was introduced in 2010. It enhanced the role of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) to cover regulation of swap trades.

In 2013 the Federal Reserve Board announced that banking entities covered by Section 619 of Dodd-Frank must fully conform their activities and investments to the statute and regulations by 21 July 2015. Section 619 — the "Volcker Rule" — prohibits insured depository institutions and their affiliates from engaging in short-term proprietary trading of certain securities, derivatives and other financial instruments for the firm's own account, subject to exemptions including market making and risk-mitigating hedging. It also imposes limits on banking entities' investments in hedge funds and private equity.

1.4.1.4 Singapore

Singapore aligned with the G20 recommendations through increased standardisation of derivative contracts, increased central clearing, more reporting to trade repositories, and higher capital requirements for non-centrally cleared contracts reflecting their greater counterparty risk.

In adopting a clearing regime, MAS considers three factors:

  • a. The level of systemic risk posed by the product.
  • b. The depth and liquidity of the market for the product.
  • c. The availability of OTC CCPs regulated by MAS as approved clearing houses (ACHs) or recognised clearing houses (RCHs).

From 1 October 2018, MAS required all SGD and USD fixed- and floating-rate swaps to be cleared through MAS-regulated CCPs, to make derivatives trading safer and reduce counterparty risk. These are the more widely traded interest-rate derivatives in Singapore.

MAS began with the banks holding the largest share of OTC derivatives activity, to address the largest counterparty credit risks first. As in the US, EU and Japan, MAS exempted intra-group transactions: they do not transfer risk in or out of a corporate group. Clearing obligations are more enforceable where both counterparties book and clear in Singapore. They are harder to enforce for cross-border transactions where the foreign regulator does not require counterparties to seek central clearing; the interaction of jurisdictional rules remains an ongoing challenge. The US and Japan have commenced mandatory clearing; the EU has put a regime in place that has yet to commence.

For transactions traded in Singapore but booked elsewhere — foreign subsidiaries or local branches of foreign banks — MAS maintains oversight through consolidated supervision of local banking groups, engages banks regularly on their booking policies and risk controls as part of banking supervision, and assesses risk from trade data reported to licensed trade repositories, which includes OTC contracts booked or traded in Singapore.

With the global step-up in regulatory requirements and cost, banks became noticeably more selective about derivatives business, systematically scaling down investment banking activities including proprietary trading, capital-intensive activities and higher-risk exotic and credit derivatives from 2008 to 2016. In 2019 OTC derivatives trading recovered to a degree, growing more rapidly than exchange-traded derivatives.

1.4.2 Markets in Financial Instruments Directive II

MiFID II is a set of foundational regulations and directives governing capital markets in the European Union. EU-domiciled banks and their branches are subject to it, and so are banks that deal with EU investors, individual or corporate. Singapore hosts many branch operations of EU financial institutions that must meet MiFID II guidelines.

Singapore brokerage firms and local banks are not bound by the EU directive, but they are likely to be called on to support the compliance processes of their European counterparties, which must report a wide range of transactional data. As good corporate citizens in a global financial centre, Singapore institutions and exchanges can be expected to assist EU banks in meeting those requirements.

Most of the review burden falls on senior managers and compliance teams. Much of the additional workflow and documentation should be automated in systems, which requires both operations and sales staff to ensure verification procedures and data are conducted and recorded in line with MiFID II. An investment professional should know their organisation's obligations, or its decisions on alignment, and their own role under the new rules.

1.5 Structured Products

Structured products combine one or more derivatives with an underlying investment. The underlying can be a single security, a group or basket of securities, or an index. They are popularly sold as structured deposits, structured notes or structured funds, offering investors the chance to optimise yield or participate in movement of the underlying.

Structured products are often OTC and are designed to create greater yield for securities the client already holds, or potential participation in the movement of an underlying security, index or basket. Different combinations and outcomes are designed to attract investors, each with its accompanying risks.

In exchange for returns better than the market, investors bear the risk of having to convert to, or own, securities if the underlying reaches — or as long as the price does not reach — a particular trigger level. The structure often requires the investor to have sold put options to the counterparty, which can result in accumulating more of the underlying as it falls. Investors must be comfortable bearing those inherent risks. Structures can also involve leverage, which magnifies both returns and losses.

1.6 The Singapore Derivatives Market

Singapore is a major hub for derivatives trading in Asia. SGX-DT, the derivatives arm of SGX, is a Pan-Asian derivatives exchange and the world's most liquid offshore market for the benchmark equity indices of China, India, Japan and Taiwan. It is the leading commodities exchange for contracts on iron ore and rubber, and provides Eurodollar, Euroyen, Japanese government bond and stock index futures trading for Asian investors. It also offers interest-rate futures for trading and hedging — including hedging loans and deposits and interest-rate swaps denominated in SGD, USD and JPY — plus intra-commodity, inter-commodity and inter-market trading and arbitrage services.

SGX exchange-traded derivatives volume and revenue, 2015–2019, show contracts rising from 161.2 million to 240.3 million with revenue between SGD 295.7 million and SGD 459.7 million; volume rebounded in the last two years of that period after falling in 2017.

Singapore is the leading FX hub for Asia and the third largest in the world after New York and London, benefiting from the synergy of a large FX derivatives business. It lags the major New York and London exchanges in other asset classes. The commencement of two further derivatives exchanges — ICE Futures Singapore and Asia Pacific Exchange (APEX) — added breadth to the Singapore market.

The SFEMC October 2020 semi-annual survey reported average daily turnover in OTC foreign exchange derivatives of US$91 billion in FX swaps and options, a decrease on the previous period that coincided with the global downturn caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. 1.7 Summary

  • A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is linked to the value or volatility of an underlying reference or asset. Derivatives trade on centralised regulated exchanges or bilaterally in the less regulated OTC market.
  • Derivatives and structured products let investors hedge, take a directional view without owning the asset, augment profits, change the payoff profile, profit from a fall, secure a right without obligation, arbitrage, change the nature of an asset or liability, and create synthetic positions.
  • Futures, forwards, swaps, options and warrants are the basic or "plain vanilla" derivatives. Exotic derivatives combine types or add features. The building blocks are FX, money market interest-rate instruments, fixed income, equities, commodities, and mixes or indices of these.
  • Participants use derivatives to hedge and manage risk on existing positions, to speculate, or to capitalise on perceived arbitrage between assets and markets.

Exam cues

  • Five building blocks: FX, money market, fixed income, equities, commodities. Indices, baskets and funds are extensions, not a sixth class.
  • Numbers: money market instruments are >95% of global exchange-traded futures and options and >3/4 of OTC trades 2015–2019; maturities 1 day to 1 year, typically <90 days. FX derivatives are the second largest category. OTC traded >5× exchange-traded value in 2018.
  • Regulatory dates: EMIR 2012; UK ring-fencing Act 2013; Dodd-Frank 2010, Volcker conformance 21 July 2015; MAS mandatory clearing 1 October 2018 for SGD and USD fixed/floating IRS, threshold S$20bn gross notional over the last four quarters, covering banks holding 90% of OTC contracts; IRS is >90% of SGD-booked interest-rate derivatives.
  • VIX: the CBOE VIX measures perceived S&P 500 volatility from option prices. On 5 Feb 2018 the VIX more than doubled in a day; SVXY and XIV fell >80%, and XIV hit a termination event. ProShares later halved SVXY/UVXY leverage.
  • MiFID II reaches Singapore desks: T+1 reporting, 15-minute post-trade transparency, retail/professional/elective-professional classification, target market, inducement disclosure. A foreign regime does not replace Singapore obligations.
  • Trap: ETFs and ETNs are not derivatives, even where they use futures and options internally. And shorting a put is not a hedge against downside risk.

Chapter 2 — Futures, Forwards & Swaps

Futures and forwards are the oldest derivatives, and swaps are built from repeated forwards. This chapter covers their mechanics, pricing, settlement and the four swap families the examination tests.

2.1 Introduction — Futures and Forwards

Futures are standardised contracts for the purchase and sale of financial instruments or physical commodities on regulated futures exchanges, with the price determined for future delivery. Originally these were non-homogeneous contracts made directly between buyers and sellers — forward contracts. As particular specifications of quality, size and delivery time became widely used, the more popular terms standardised. With the desire for a credit-worthy market-maker or counterparty, margin requirements were set in and the futures exchange was born.

A futures exchange is a marketplace for standardised financial and commodity derivatives where buying and selling is contracted for future delivery.

2.1.1 Futures versus Forwards

Forward contracts preceded futures. The differences decide which instrument fits a given exposure.

Futures Forwards
Standardised as to quality, quantity, delivery time and place. Usually bilaterally negotiated between buyer and seller; terms are not standardised.
The exchange is counterparty, so there is no counterparty risk between the actual buyer and seller — it is realised only if the exchange itself fails. Counterparty risk is therefore lower than for forwards. Each party is exposed to the other. Bilateral risk can be eliminated where the forward is cleared by a central counterparty.
Marked to market at the end of each trading day; participants must cover margin shortfalls under exchange rules. Typically not subject to mark-to-market adjustment and with no interim partial settlement requirement.
Usually offset (closed out) on the exchange before maturity; live contracts settle on the settlement price and procedures at maturity. Settled by physical delivery or by cash settlement on the difference between the contracted price and the market price at maturity; traded OTC with no guarantee of an active secondary market, and settlement terms vary by contract.
Globally, most trading now occurs on electronic platforms linked to the exchanges, with pit trading phased out. —

Forwards remain more useful where a player needs a customised solution — a position or hedge in an exotic currency, or a delivery period not readily available on futures. Those needs are met through a customised contract with a financial institution or another commercial counterparty.

Understanding both matters because the directional and leverage effects of futures let an investor express the degree of bullishness or bearishness they hold.

2.1.2 Economic Importance and Impact of the Futures Market

The futures market is highly active and central to the global financial marketplace. It is an important source of market information and often indicates market sentiment.

2.1.2.1 Price Discovery

The futures market reflects the current price of a contract for a product to be delivered in the future, providing a platform for ongoing price discovery — buyers and sellers contracting and influencing prices now, for future delivery, given available information and predictions. How participants absorb and react to information constantly changes prices. That information includes future economic performance, potential political developments, and forecasts of environmental and climatic change that may or may not come about.

2.1.2.2 Risk Management

The futures market is where investors manage risk. Because market prices are transparent and readily available, investors know how much they need to buy or sell. For consumers and buyers of commodities, hedging lowers the eventual cost to consumers by reducing the risk that manufacturers raise prices for key supplies because of spot-market volatility. Business managers and financial investors use suitable futures as part of planning to reach their objectives.

2.1.2.3 Augmenting Volatility

Participants use futures to leverage long or short positions. In major financial distress in equity, bond or commodity markets, the leveraging impact of futures and options can significantly augment volatility. The downward pricing pressure from shorting futures usually adds selling pressure on the spot market and underlying assets; hedge funds and traders exploit such situations, and the value of underlying assets can fall faster than normal, destroying market value over a very short time.

Computer-driven trading and electronic platforms have quickened the pace of wealth destruction during shocks and extended its ripple into other asset classes and the economy, so governments have had to intervene in both futures and spot markets in extreme situations to dampen that ripple.

2.2 General Features of Futures Contracts

  • a. Standardised. Futures are standardised contracts for the purchase and sale of financial instruments or physical commodities on a regulated futures exchange, for future delivery on the delivery date.
  • b. Price driven by the underlying. Price movement is directly influenced by the underlying asset's price and value. The futures price is set by current demand and supply, which are in turn shaped by composite information and market expectations about the future.
  • c. Finite life. A contract ends on the expiration date, so market direction and time horizon are central considerations.
  • d. Notional value. Futures carry a notional value depending on contract size or multiple and the market price at any time. At expiration the parties must fulfil their obligations — one to deliver, the other to receive. Unlike out-of-the-money options or warrants, which go to zero at expiry, a futures contract retains a notional value at expiration.
  • e. Lower counterparty risk. The counterparty is the futures exchange, backed by margin requirements.
  • f. Leverage. The investor posts only a fraction of contract value as initial margin to gain exposure to the whole position, augmenting both gains and losses.
  • g. Daily mark-to-market. The value is marked at the end of each trading session and any margin shortfall must be covered.
  • h. Close-out usual. In most executions buyers and sellers do not deliver or receive the underlying, but square off with the exchange to realise gain or loss.
  • i. Contract-specific terms. Contract size and units depend on the type of futures; they appear in the contract specification or term sheet. Traders combine futures characteristics with the underlying and other derivatives to craft strategies with different payoff and risk profiles.

2.2.2 Leverage

  • a. Initial margin. The small upfront payment that initiates a position; the investor does not pay for the whole contract at trade initiation.
  • b. Maintenance margin. The minimum the customer must keep on deposit at all times. While net equity is above it, no request for additional funds arises even after an adverse move.
  • c. Additional margin. Each account is credited or debited at the end of each trading day and checked against the appropriate margin for all open positions. If the balance falls below maintenance level, additional margin is required and the account must be restored to the initial margin level immediately or within a stipulated time.

In Singapore, SGX publishes maintenance and initial margin for every futures contract on a daily margin schedule. Margins vary when market conditions change significantly — for example, in extreme volatility.

How margins are used: SGX Nikkei 225

The contract is worth JPY 500 × the Nikkei level. At 22,500 the contract value is:

500 × 22, 500 = JPY 11, 250, 000

At an initial margin of 6.5%:

0.065 × 11, 250, 000 = JPY 731, 250

If the index rallies 7% to 24,075, the contract is worth JPY 12,037,500. The profit is:

500 × (24, 075 − 22, 500) = JPY 787, 500

That is more than 100% of the initial margin — the leverage effect, and one reason futures appeal.

If the index falls 160 points to 22,340, the contract is worth JPY 11,170,000 and the loss is:

500 × (22, 340 − 22, 500) = −JPY 80, 000

The exchange compares contract value to the account daily and adds profits or subtracts losses. The balance must stay above the maintenance margin — 5%, or:

0.05 × 11, 250, 000 = JPY 562, 500

Here the balance becomes JPY 651,250, still above the JPY 562,500 maintenance level.

2.3 Types of Futures

Growth in futures began with farmers, miners, manufacturers and businesses needing to manage risk and uncertainty: a futures contract fixes a price for a period, which makes planning easier. The categories are:

  • a. Commodity futures — agriculture, energy, metals.
  • b. Financial futures — equity, interest rates, foreign exchange.
  • c. Others — innovation has created futures in new areas such as weather.

2.3.1 Interest Rates Futures

Banks lending or borrowing over a period are exposed to rate movements and can partially or fully hedge through interest-rate futures or options on them. More broadly, these contracts let investors and traders take positions on how short-term or long-term rates may move across the yield curve, given fiscal and monetary policy and their view of economic statistics. Interest-rate futures are available on most exchanges, especially in the US, UK and Singapore.

2.3.1.1 Short-Term Interest Rates Futures

The most popular short-term contracts are US Treasury bill futures, Eurodollar futures and Euroyen futures. The underlying is typically:

  • a. A time deposit in a Eurocurrency — a currency lent or borrowed outside its country of origin — where the rate offered for a specified period to a first-class bank determines the futures price for the same period; or
  • b. A government treasury bill, where the rate at which an investor is willing to invest for the period forms the basis of the futures price.

P = 100 × (1 − R)

where R is the annualised interest rate for the period. At R = 3.50%:

P = 100 × (1 − 0.035) = 96.50

2.3.1.2 Long-Term Interest Rates Futures

Used to manage rate exposure or to take a market view. A borrower with outstanding debt may protect against rising rates; a fixed-income manager may sell interest-rate futures to protect a bond portfolio. If rates rise, futures prices fall — rates and bond prices move in opposite directions — and the profit on closing the futures offsets the portfolio decline.

$$ \left(1 + \frac{d_1 r_1}{360}\right)\left(1 + \frac{d_2 r_2}{360}\right) = \left(1 + \frac{DR}{360}\right) $$

Rearranged to solve for the second-period rate:

$$ r_2 = \frac{360}{d_2} \left( \frac{360 + DR}{360 + d_1 r_1} - 1 \right) $$

where d1 is days in the first period, d2 days in the second, D days for the entire period, r1 the first-period rate, r2 the second-period rate and R the rate for the whole period.

Worked example. Spot date to 15 September is 45 days; to 15 December is 135 days. The 45-day rate is 1.25% and the 135-day rate is 1.75%. The implied 90-day forward rate is:

$$ \left(1 + \frac{45 \times 0.0125}{360}\right)\left(1 + \frac{90 \times r_2}{360}\right) = \left(1 + \frac{135 \times 0.0175}{360}\right) = 1.006563 $$

Solving gives r2 = 2.00%.

2.3.1.3 US Treasury Futures

US Treasuries are among the most liquid financial products in the world, and Treasury futures and options support hedging, income enhancement, duration adjustment, rate speculation and spread trades. CME contracts mirror the 2, 5, 10 and 30-year tenures. On SGX, mini-Japanese Government Bond futures are the popular long-bond contract.

Each US Treasury futures contract has a face value at maturity of USD 100,000, except the 2-year and 3-year contracts at USD 200,000. Prices are quoted in points per USD 2,000 for 2- and 3-year contracts and per USD 1,000 for all others.

Profit example. A fund manager expects Treasury yields to rise. He sells 10 contracts of Jun 2017 5-year Treasury Note futures at 117.245 and buys them back at 116.000:

10 × (117.245 − 116.000) × 1, 000 = USD 12, 450 profit

The 5-year tick is ¼ of 1/32 of a point, worth:

0.25 × 0.03125 × 0.01 × 100, 000 = USD 7.8125

so the gain is (117.245 − 116.000) × 1, 000/7.8125 = 159.36 ticks, and 10 × 159.36 × 7.8125 = USD 12, 450.

Loss example. On Thursday 7 April 2017 a surprise US missile strike on Syrian air bases pushed yields down and bond prices up; the 10-year yield fell to 2.31%, the lowest since the March rate hike when it was 2.6%. A trader reads the June 2017 10-year T-Note futures as having risen only modestly, from 125 8/32 to 125 12/32, and buys 10 contracts at 125 12/32 expecting more buyers. Prices reverse and the contract ends the day at 124 8/32:

$$ 10 \times (124\,\tfrac{8}{32} - 125\,\tfrac{12}{32}) \times 1{,}000 = -\text{USD } 11{,}250 \text{ loss} $$

The 10-year tick is ½ of 1/32, worth USD 15.625, so the loss is −72 ticks, and 10 × −72 × 15.625 = −USD 11, 250.

2.3.2 Currency Futures

Currency futures are contracts to buy or sell a specified amount of one currency against another at a set price and future date. Financial institutions, investment managers, corporations and private investors use them to manage currency risk or to profit from rate movements. They were first introduced at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in 1972, after the fixed exchange-rate system and gold standard were discarded.

Currency futures volume tracks the underlying spot market. The BIS Triennial Survey reported global FX turnover of USD 5.1 trillion per day in 2016, 5.5% below USD 5.4 trillion in 2013 (the source text prints "5.1 billon"; read trillion). Part of the decline reflected lower growth in global trade and capital flows, still below pre-crisis levels.

More significant was a structural shift towards using FX for hedging rather than risk-taking, a consequence of reduced proprietary trading and tighter risk controls. Banks consolidated and cut costs over the decade. The number of dealer banks willing to warehouse risk declined while non-bank market-makers gained a stronger foothold as liquidity providers, sometimes bypassing traditional bank intermediaries. Despite the overall decline, trading in most FX derivatives — particularly FX swaps — kept growing, and emerging-market currencies, especially the renminbi, gained share. Singapore kept the top spot for FX trading, which supports FX-derivative business here.

2.3.2.1 Types of Foreign Exchange Futures Contracts Offered

Futures activity mirrors demand in the underlying currency market, so major pairs dominate. On the CME, currency futures are grouped into majors against the USD, emerging-market currencies against the USD, cross-rates not involving the USD, and E-Micro contracts trading at 1/10th the size of the regular contract. On Euronext, trading concentrates on the more active majors — EUR/USD, GBP/USD and GBP/EUR.

On SGX-DT, Asian currencies trade against the USD, among themselves, and against the non-deliverable forward for the renminbi (CNY), Indonesian rupiah (IDR), Taiwan dollar (TWD), Indian rupee (INR), Korean won (KRW), Malaysian ringgit (MYR) and Philippine peso (PHP). SGX remains one of few venues offering hedging, trading and investment exposure in these currencies, and Singapore's FX derivatives market benefits from demand for emerging Asian currencies as China, Indonesia and India expand.

2.3.2.2 Interest Rate Parity Theory

The theory holds that the forward (futures) premium or discount between two currencies should equal the difference in domestic interest rates for securities of the same maturity, ignoring small transaction costs. Where the relationship is violated, arbitrageurs can earn risk-free profit and their actions force futures and spot rates back into line.

$$ F = S \times \frac{1 + R_c \left(\dfrac{n}{360}\right)}{1 + R_b \left(\dfrac{n}{360}\right)} $$

where F is the forward (futures) rate, S the spot rate, Rb the annualised base-currency rate, Rc the annualised counter-currency rate and n the number of days.

Worked example. Base currency GBP, counter currency USD. Spot GBP/USD = 1.2422; Rb = 1-year GBP LIBOR = 0.70444%; Rc = 1-year USD LIBOR = 1.79900%.

$$ F = 1.2422 \times \frac{1 + 0.01799 \left(\dfrac{360}{360}\right)}{1 + 0.00704 \left(\dfrac{360}{360}\right)} = 1.2557 $$

So GBP 1 = USD 1.2557, a forward price of GBP/USD 1.2557.

2.3.3 Equity Index Futures

Equity index futures replicate the future performance of an underlying equity market index, letting investors capitalise on broad market movements without changing their dollar investment in stocks. An index is a representative basket or portfolio of stocks or commodities; an equity index measures the performance of a selected basket representing the whole market. Indices may be price-weighted (DJIA) or market-capitalisation weighted (MSCI).

Buying the index raises or lowers exposure to specific markets or sectors and is a cost-effective way to gain equity exposure. Most exchanges list futures on their own indices, while some position themselves regionally or globally. SGX has consolidated its position as a regional platform for futures, options and structured warrants on Asian markets, and offers index futures relevant to managers benchmarked to indices such as MSCI, so they can rebalance rapidly, hedge positions and exploit country-specific opportunities. The most successful launch has been the SGX FTSE China A50 Futures, which continues to trade heavily.

SGX equity index futures include MSCI Singapore, Straits Times Index, FTSE China A50, FTSE China H50, FTSE Malaysia, FTSE Philippines, FTSE Taiwan, FTSE Thailand, Nikkei 225, USD Nikkei 225, Mini Nikkei 225 and Nifty 50.

2.3.3.2 Pricing Equity Index Futures

$$ F = S \left[ 1 + r \left(\frac{t}{360}\right) \right] - D $$

where S is the spot index, r the short-term rate, t days to expiry and D dividends paid before expiry.

Index futures do not trade precisely at fair value all the time; the difference between futures and spot is the basis. Fair value is normally expected to be positive — futures above spot — because under normal conditions financing costs, reflected in rates such as LIBOR or SIBOR, exceed dividend yields. Loose post-2008 monetary policy has at times reversed this.

If futures rally much above fair value, an arbitrageur buys the equity portfolio and sells index futures, trading a basket or subset of constituents or using electronic systems to trade all or nearly all of them. Buying stocks and selling futures bids stocks up or pushes futures down until equilibrium returns.

2.3.4 Single Stock Futures

A Single Stock Futures (SSF) contract tracks the price of the underlying stock. On SGX the buyer agrees to pay a specified price at a predetermined date and the seller is obligated to deliver the value of the stock at that date; SGX SSFs are cash settled, with no physical delivery. Portfolio managers use them to protect against adverse moves and to diversify risk using margin financing. SSFs carry no shareholder privileges.

Advantages:

  • a. Leveraged, based on several thousand shares, so the leverage effect amplifies gains and losses.
  • b. Lower transaction cost than buying the stock, because the outlay is only the margin.
  • c. For the same reason, offshore investors bear less currency exposure — they invest only the margin, not the full underlying value.
  • d. Ease of short selling lets investors participate in and profit from market downturns.
  • e. Once an exchange admits a contract it must ensure liquidity, transparency, fairness and timeliness in market-making with reasonable bid-offer spreads, and investors must be able to close out, typically electronically. The exchange guarantees clearing-house support from designated market-makers.

The main disadvantage is that SSFs can invite short selling of the stock, reducing underlying prices and causing downward spirals in uncertainty and shocks. The market's ability to absorb increased downward volatility is an important consideration before a new contract is introduced.

2.3.5 Commodity Futures

Physical-market storage, transport, quality and delivery-location differences create basis and roll risk. Commodity contracts are grouped as agriculture, energy and metals.

2.3.5.1 Gold and Other Precious Metal Futures

In potential military conflicts involving major powers there is a tendency to flight to safety. In early April 2017, tension escalated between the US and Syria–Russia over banned chemical weapons and the US missile response, while US–North Korean nuclear-testing tension also rose. Gold futures moved up 4% to USD 1,290.70/ounce just before Easter; when tension eased the price retraced.

Calculating notional profit for gold futures

Gold rose to USD 1,288.50/ounce before Good Friday, 13 April.

Conversely, when interest rates rise, gold and other precious metals come under pressure because they yield no interest or dividend; when perceived risk is low, investors prefer risky assets and safe havens fall. Gold is also increasingly intertwined with gold ETFs: according to the World Gold Council the largest growth in gold demand in 2016 (+660%) came from ETFs.

Beyond financial markets, gold has jewellery demand: China and India are the largest sources, and more than half of physical gold trading is estimated to come from Asia. Interruption of precious-metal imports creates underlying downward pressure.

Gold futures on the CME and ICE are quoted in 100 troy ounces. In Singapore the SGX Gold contract is dormant and not traded.

2.3.5.2 Base Metal and Ferrous Metal Futures

Demand and prices depend on economic growth, with underlying demand from major traders, miners and manufacturers wanting physical delivery. Base and ferrous metals rose significantly during China's infrastructure expansion, especially 2002–2012; China's slowdown 2012–2016 amid overcapacity produced a sharp fall. Expected demand from proposed US infrastructure fiscal spending in 2017–2018 lifted prices again.

Steel pricing is not uniform and US–China trade disputes and anti-dumping allegations disrupt price discovery. The US announced steel and aluminium tariffs to protect local manufacturers, which widens the difference between US and non-US exchange prices. Steel comes in different types and grades, and SGX steel quotes from China differ from CME quotes featuring US steel.

Iron ore and steel are key SGX contracts. The London Metal Exchange is the largest global metal exchange and remains a main venue for iron ore and steel futures, while Chinese exchanges have grown strongly. Singapore is positioned as a key clearing centre for iron ore traded elsewhere, especially China.

2.3.5.3 Crude Oil Futures

Crude oil futures remain a focal point for investors because of dramatic price volatility: a high of USD 155/barrel just before the Global Financial Crisis, a plunge to USD 48 in 2009, a rebound above USD 100 for most of 2010–2013, then new lows of USD 29.60 in 2015 amid structural change and competition from fracking. Futures may have helped producers lock in higher prices on some occasions, but extended declines offer producers little protection. An OPEC agreement to limit supply eventually held crude in a USD 55–65 range in the later part of 2018, ahead of the Saudi Aramco listing. Crude remains one of the most actively traded instruments.

2.3.5.4 Energy — Natural Gas, Other Refined Products and Coal Futures

The volatility of energy futures is relatively well correlated. Like crude, other traditional energy products — LNG, jet fuel, gas oil, kerosene, coal — have been volatile over the decade. For downstream refined products there may be a lag effect before the full impact of price changes works through inventory. Coal, though environmentally unattractive, remains a viable substitute in many developing economies.

Singapore is a major petrochemical refining base, and SGX lists futures on diverse petrochemical and refined products from regional players, plus Indonesian coal futures.

2.3.5.5 Energy — Electricity Futures

Energy futures have been available in the US for many years. Multiple production sources (LNG, coal, hydroelectricity) across regions, many users (towns, cities, industries), and the need to optimise non-peak hours against fluctuating demand drove the development of electricity futures.

On SGX there are monthly and quarterly electricity futures, still relatively new and serving specific needs of Singapore industrial users. The local market is thermal, with more than 90% dependent on natural gas. Generating companies offer to sell into the spot market every half-hour, matching prevailing demand, but prices fluctuate during tight supply from plant outages or network constraints. Consumers buying directly from the spot market are exposed to those fluctuations. Electricity futures offset expensive electricity for consumers or secure higher proceeds against cheaper off-peak electricity.

2.3.5.6 Agriculture Futures

A wide range of agriculture contracts — grain, soft commodities, livestock, dairy and forest products — has existed for decades, driven mainly by the supply and business needs of farmers, producers, wholesale consumers, investors and speculators.

2.4 Contract Settlement and Clearing Process and Orders on the SGX

2.4.1 Modes of Settlement

A buyer or seller can settle a futures position in three ways.

2.4.1.1 Delivery or Hold to Expiry

Every contract has an expiry date. On that date the buyer accepts delivery of the underlying and the seller liquidates by delivering at the agreed price. Commodity contracts state physical delivery to an approved warehouse; currencies and some financial instruments such as the S&P 500 call for cash settlement. Every contract specifies the last trading day before expiry.

Investors must know the expiry dates, because as expiry approaches liquidity decreases as traders roll to the next contract month.

2.4.1.2 Offset Position

The simplest and most common route for financial futures. Buying two S&P 500 contracts is offset by selling two; selling two is offset by buying two. To limit overnight risk, many traders exit all positions and are flat at the end of each trading day.

2.4.1.3 Roll Position

Rather than closing out, the investor closes the expiring contract and simultaneously opens a position in a later month, maintaining exposure and deferring settlement.

2.4.2 Minimum Price Movement for Contracts on SGX-DT — Understanding Tick Sizes

The tick size is three things at once: the minimum movement in the quoted price, the minimum increment between bid and offer, and a dollar amount per contract. Convert between them using the contract multiplier.

Combined with the CME mutual-offset links, this positions Singapore as a high-quality global clearing centre for futures and derivatives; as technology changes how transactions are performed, the clearing process must continue to adapt.

2.5 Futures Pricing Model

2.5.1 Spot Price

The spot price is the current market price of the underlying asset or commodity.

2.5.2 Cost of Carry Model

The relationship between futures and spot prices is summarised by cost of carry. Carry includes financial costs — interest on bonds, interest on margin accounts, interest on money borrowed to buy a security — and economic costs such as the opportunity cost of taking the initial position. The net cost of carry is all costs of holding the equivalent cash position less the returns that position would have generated: financing at prevailing rates, and dividends or bonuses from shares, or the average return of an index in the case of index futures. In futures the investor deposits only a fraction of position value as margin.

The model assumes arbitrage between cash and futures eliminates all pricing imperfections. For simplicity it assumes:

  • a. the contract is held to maturity, so a fair price can be derived;
  • b. there are no transaction costs or margin requirements;
  • c. there are no restrictions on short selling; and
  • d. investors can borrow and lend at the same rate.

Once arbitrage has removed distortions, the fair futures price is spot plus the net cost of carry from today to expiry:

F0, t = S0(1 + C0, t)

2.5.3 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage

Position Cash-and-carry (futures too high) Reverse cash-and-carry (futures too low)
Debt Borrow to buy the spot asset Lend the proceeds of a short sale
Physical Buy and hold the spot asset Short or borrow the spot asset and deliver
Futures Sell futures Buy futures

Forward case. Gold spot is USD 1,275/oz, carry is 2.25% for the year, and the futures price is USD 1,305.

F0, t = 1, 275 × (1 + 0.0225) = 1, 303.69

Futures at USD 1,305 exceed fair value of USD 1,303.69, so the arbitrage is to sell futures and buy spot. Borrow to buy 100 oz:

1 × 1, 275 × 100 = USD 127, 500

Sell one 100 oz contract at USD 1,305 for USD 130,500. Repay principal plus interest:

127, 500 + (0.0225 × 127, 500) = USD 130, 368.75

Profit:

130, 500.00 − 130, 368.75 = USD 131.25

— a negligible 0.1% opportunity. To improve it, the futures price must be higher, the spot lower, and the borrowing cost materially lower.

Reverse case. Spot gold is USD 1,285.

F0, t = 1, 285 × (1 + 0.0225) = 1, 313.91

Futures at USD 1,305 are below fair value of USD 1,313.91, so buy futures and short spot. Borrow 100 oz of gold at 1.00% for a year and sell it at spot for:

100 × 1, 285 = USD 128, 500

Lend the proceeds at 2.25%, earning USD 2,891.25, so after a year:

128, 500 + 2, 891.25 = USD 131, 391.25

Buy 100 oz one-year futures at USD 1,305 for USD 130,500 to cover the borrowed gold. Surplus:

131, 391.25 − 130, 500.00 = USD 891.25

Cost of borrowing the gold:

128, 500 × 0.01 = USD 1, 285.00

Overall:

891.25 − 1, 285 = −USD 393.75  (−0.306%)

So there was no arbitrage. To create one, the gold borrowing cost must be much lower or free while the lending rate rises, or the futures price must fall or spot rise.

Arbitrage rarely exists or lasts more than milliseconds: computer-based trading has pushed efficiency past human levels and any differential disappears.

2.5.4 Expectancy Model

Futures prices are expected future spot prices. If more traders expect the futures price to rise than to fall, the current futures price is bid up. What matters is not the cash-futures relationship but the relationship between the expected spot price at expiry and the futures price. Both the cost-of-carry and expectations models explain part of futures price movement.

2.6 Basis in Futures Contracts

The difference between futures and spot is the basis.

When markets are in contango — typically stable or bullish conditions — the basis is usually positive, with futures above spot because of carrying costs such as financing and storage. In backwardation — often bearish or stressed conditions — the basis can be negative, with futures below spot.

Because a contract settles at the cash price on expiry, futures and spot converge as expiry approaches.

Convention warning. The official guide states on page 46 that contango gives a positive basis with futures above spot, and then on page 47 defines and uses basis as spot minus futures, which makes contango's basis negative. The two are the same economic relationship described with opposite signs. This guide uses basis = spot − futures throughout, so contango produces a negative basis. State your convention in any answer.

2.6.1 Factors Affecting Basis

  • a. Cost or return of carry.
  • b. Time to maturity.
  • c. Yield-curve changes.
  • d. Relative liquidity of cash and futures markets.
  • e. Market rates versus administered rates.
  • f. Expectations of market participants.
  • g. Coupon or income differences between the hedged asset and the deliverable.

2.6.2 Basis, Contango and Backwardation

Basis risk persists for three reasons: the cash and futures markets are not perfectly correlated, the exposure being hedged may not match the deliverable, and the hedge may be closed before expiry, so convergence is incomplete. A short hedger's effective price is the futures price plus the ending basis:

Vt = St + (F − Ft) = F + (St − Ft)

Roll yield arises when a position is rolled from an expiring contract to a later one; under contango the roll costs money, under backwardation it earns.

2.7 Swaps

Swaps are customised OTC agreements to exchange cash flows over several periods on a notional amount. Singapore's FX market is heavily swap-based: forex forwards and swaps were 70–75% of FX turnover in 2016–17, and currency interest-rate swaps 60–66% of FX derivatives.

2.7.1 Interest Rate Swaps

One party pays fixed and receives floating, or the reverse. The notional is normally not exchanged; the swap rate or spread and the benchmark reset dates matter.

Plain-vanilla example: USD 100 million, paying 5% fixed against six-month LIBOR. With LIBOR at 4.2% in September 2014, the fixed payer's net receipt is:

0.5 × (0.042 − 0.05) × 100m = −USD 0.4m

so Bank B receives USD 2.1m on the fixed leg and pays USD 2.5m floating. With LIBOR at 4.8% in March 2015 the floating leg is USD 2.4m and the net position reverses.

$$ \text{fixed payment} = \text{notional} \times \text{swap rate} \times \frac{\text{days}}{360} $$

The swap spread is the swap rate less the yield on the government bond of matching maturity. The LIBOR swap curve plots swap rates by tenor. A party with an existing swap can enter an offsetting swap to reduce or cancel the exposure.

2.7.2 FX Swaps

An FX swap exchanges currencies on a near leg and reverses the exchange on a far leg, managing short-term currency funding. The near-leg rate is the spot rate and the far leg is set by the swap points for the value dates. Example: a firm needing EUR 5 million for six months sells USD for EUR spot and agrees to reverse the exchange in six months at the forward rate, locking the funding cost.

2.7.3 Currency Swaps

A currency swap exchanges principal and interest in two currencies. A Singapore bank needing USD swaps SGD principal with a US bank needing SGD, each paying the other's interest, transforming both the currency and the interest exposure for the life of the deal.

2.7.4 Commodity Swaps

A fixed price is exchanged for a floating commodity price, letting a producer or user lock or transform price exposure without repeatedly rolling futures. SGX lists standardised commodity swaps.

2.7.5 Equity Swaps

One party receives the total return of an equity or index and pays a fixed or floating rate, creating synthetic equity exposure without ownership.

Four-period example on a notional of USD 200 million against the S&P 500, where the manager pays the index total return plus 2% and receives 4% fixed:

Period Index level Index return Swap cash flow (USD m) Portfolio value (USD m) Reported return
31 Jul 2017 2,470.20 — — 400.00 —
Period 1 2,569.00 +4% −2.00 414.00 +3.50%
Period 2 2,363.48 −8% +10.00 390.88 −5.58%
Period 3 2,623.47 +11% −9.00 424.88 +8.70%
Period 4 2,754.64 +5% −3.00 443.12 +4.28%

The manager's realised return each period is the portfolio return plus the swap cash flow, which is why a falling index produces a positive swap flow.

2.7.6 Credit Default Swaps

A protection buyer pays a spread, usually in basis points quarterly, to a protection seller. On a defined credit event the seller pays under cash or physical settlement. Typical notionals are USD 10–20 million.

Premium arithmetic: at 160bp on USD 20 million, quarterly:

160bp = 1.60% ⇒ 20m × 1.60% = USD 320, 000 p.a.

so each quarter is one quarter of the annual amount, USD 80,000 — equivalently 40bp of notional per quarter.

2.7.6.1 CDS Spreads

The CDS "spread" is not the same as a benchmark spread; it is the premium for credit protection on the reference entity. Spreads widen with credit deterioration — the PIIGS (Portugal, Ireland, Italy, Greece, Spain) episode is the standard example.

2.7.6.2 Credit Event

The ISDA-defined events are bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring, obligation acceleration, obligation default, repudiation/moratorium and (in some definitions) government intervention. Debt restructuring is the most negotiated of these.

2.7.6.3 Cash Settlement

The protection seller pays the difference between par and the market price of the deliverable obligation after the event.

2.7.6.4 Physical Settlement

The protection seller pays par and receives the deliverable obligation, so the buyer delivers the defaulted bond and receives face value.

Naked CDS creates large synthetic credit exposure. The AIG episode during the Global Financial Crisis showed how leverage, opacity and counterparty concentration amplify credit risk: AIG had sold protection far in excess of its capital and required a public rescue.

2.8 Summary

Futures are standardised, margined, daily-settled and cleared; forwards and swaps are usually customised OTC. Pricing is driven by carry and parity, but basis, liquidity, margin and credit constraints matter. Swaps exchange exposures rather than principal ownership and are powerful but documentation- and counterparty-dependent.

3.1 Market Participants

The main participants are:

  • a. Hedgers
  • b. Speculators
  • c. Market makers
  • d. Proprietary trading firms
  • e. Arbitrageurs
  • f. Portfolio managers
  • g. Hedge funds
  • h. Central banks, sovereign funds and regulators

As volatility rises, participants with different motivations turn to derivatives to hedge or to maximise return. The same firm can act as speculator, hedger and arbitrageur at once, and knowing which group is driving the market helps decide whether to follow or oppose the direction. Growth in hedge funds and alternative assets has driven innovative derivative strategies, and growth in wealth management has pushed institutions to offer structured products built from futures and other derivatives.

3.1.1 Hedgers

Hedgers hold a position in the underlying asset or commodity and use futures to reduce or limit the risk of an adverse price change. Producers such as farmers sell futures on the crop they grow to hedge a price fall, which makes long-term planning easier. Industrial users buy futures to fix input costs — a food processor fixing corn or wheat, an airline hedging fuel, a jeweller hedging gold and silver — so they can plan on a fixed cost for core supplies and stabilise the price passed to customers.

3.1.2 Speculators (Individual Traders)

Many speculators are individuals trading their own funds, expressing a view on a market's direction. Electronic trading has levelled the field by improving access to price and trade information; speed and ease of execution, with modern risk management, give individuals access to markets and strategies once reserved for institutions.

3.1.3 Market Makers

Market makers are trading firms contractually obliged to provide liquidity, continually quoting both bids and offers, usually in exchange for reduced trading fees. Electronic market makers now supply much of the liquidity that lets large transactions occur without moving price substantially. They profit from capturing the spread — the small difference between bid and offer across many transactions — or by trading related futures markets where they see price or profit opportunities.

3.1.4 Proprietary Trading Firms

Prop shops profit directly from their traders' activity. They supply education and capital so traders can execute many trades a day, gaining more leverage than they would on their own account and access to research and strategies developed by larger institutions.

Some large banks retain proprietary units. The most famous loss is Barings in 1995, where a single proprietary trader's excessive risk-taking on Nikkei 225 futures accumulated losses twice the bank's capital. Poor controls and supervision led several banks to close proprietary units, but losses from distressed debt, CDOs, CDS and Delta One instruments continued to be reported well after the crisis.

Delta One. Desks provide OTC derivative structures pegged one-to-one against an underlying, usually an index. The bank trades the underlying and related derivatives — sometimes swaps with other banks — seeking arbitrage profit or profit from holdings moving against the direction of the structure sold. Trades are typically very large because margins are extremely fine, and because most are OTC they are hard to monitor or regulate.

Financial institutions also hedge their sale of derivatives to institutional and private clients through futures and options, and ETF and ETN issuers manage daily offsetting futures and options positions against their long and short baskets through the proprietary desk.

3.1.5 Arbitrageurs

Arbitrageurs seek riskless profit from disequilibrium between cash and futures markets. They take no directional bet, instead exploiting price differences between related markets or markets with a fixed relationship — the gap between implied and market value. They rely on computer systems and models, because windows close very fast: computers spot the opportunity and arbitrage it away. Arbitrageurs improve liquidity and keep the pricing relationship between futures and the underlying consistent, and they usually work for market makers and proprietary firms.

3.1.6 Portfolio Managers

A portfolio or investment manager invests or hedges the assets of a mutual fund, ETF or closed-end fund, implementing the strategy and managing day-to-day trading. Futures are often used to raise or lower overall market exposure without disrupting a portfolio balance that took significant effort to build.

3.1.7 Hedge Funds

The deep, liquid futures market lets hedge funds execute large transactions and adjust exposure without changing security holdings. A hedge fund is a managed portfolio using advanced strategies to maximise return, absolutely or against a benchmark. The name is historical: early funds shorted the market to offset bear-market risk. On occasion hedge funds have exploited market weakness by shorting futures collectively, adding sharp downward pressure to both futures and spot.

3.1.8 Sovereign Funds, Central Banks and Regulators

Collective selling by speculators and hedge funds has sometimes forced governments to intervene in spot and futures markets to counterbalance speculative forces and stabilise the market. In severe cases, tightening liquidity, capital flows and trading activity has been used to break the momentum in both markets.

During the 1997 Asian Currency Crisis, several Asian currencies and equity markets were driven down under persistent heavy selling, but the speculation was broken when some governments intervened in spot and futures markets and introduced capital and currency exchange controls, locking speculators into short currency positions they could not unwind while incurring interest cost.

Day to day, many governments manage their currency within a trading band and participate in spot and futures markets to influence its direction.

3.2 Types of Orders on the SGX

Most order types are common across futures and stock exchanges.

Order type Behaviour
Limit order Carries a specified price and quantity — the price the trader will pay, or wants to receive, for one contract.
Market-to-limit (MTL) order Has a validity time and can be entered as Fill-and-Kill (FAK), Fill-or-Kill (FOK), or stored as a Day or Good-Till-Cancelled (GTC) order. A store order leaves any unfilled quantity in the book at that price. If there is no price on the opposite side, the order is stored as a limit one tick better than the best price on the same side; if no such price exists it is rejected. Entered while orders are continuously matched, it matches at the best possible price.
Market order Entered while orders are continuously matched, it matches at the best possible price. Entered when orders are not continuously matched, it takes the Equilibrium Price (EP) as its price, and its price therefore changes if the EP changes.
Stop order (limit, MTL or market) Hidden until its trigger is met, then converted to a limit, MTL or market order. Four parameters define the trigger: Stop Series (the instrument the Stop Price is compared to); Stop Price (the level the price condition must meet); Stop Price Reference Type (Bid, Ask or Last); and Stop Price Condition (≥ or ≤ versus that reference).
Market-if-touched (MIT) order An order to buy or sell a contract below or above the market, held until the trigger price is touched and then submitted as a market order. It resembles a stop, except that an MIT sell is placed above the market and a stop sell below it. Hidden until converted.
Session state order (SSO) Triggers entry of an order into the book only when the market transitions into a new session state. Participants query valid session states and select one. SSOs and their triggered orders are invisible until triggering, are deleted at end of day if the transition never occurs (for example because of a trading halt or invalid state), and may not be GTC.

3.2.1 Other Types of Orders — Packs and Bundles

Packs and bundles were introduced by CME Group for Eurodollar contracts; NYSE Euronext LIFFE offers them for STIR Euribor and Sterling futures. They buy or sell a series of futures representing a segment along the yield curve, creating or liquidating positions along the curve and gaining exposure to longer-term rates without legging risk — the risk of failing to complete one leg. The advantage is execution at a single price: no multiple orders per contract, lower overall cost, and no unfilled legs.

  • A pack buys a predefined number of contracts in four consecutive delivery months. Eurodollar packs are the simultaneous purchase of an equally weighted, consecutive series of four Eurodollar futures, executed in a single transaction so partial fills cannot occur.
  • A bundle buys a predefined number of contracts in each consecutive quarterly month for two or more years, across the standard 2, 3 or 5-year periods. There are no 1-year bundles because those are effectively packs. The first contract is typically the first quarterly contract in the strip, but a bundle can start at any quarterly contract.

Packs and bundles are quoted on the average net change in value of all constituent Eurodollar contracts since the prior settlement, in increments of ¼ of 1 basis point (0.01%).

3.3 Types of Trades and Strategies

  • a. Outright trades. Buy or sell a single futures contract, wagering the underlying rises or falls. Familiar and easy to understand.
  • b. Hedging. Sell a futures contract to offset a cash position. A holder of a large US equity portfolio who does not want to sell for tax reasons but fears a decline can sell S&P 500 index futures.
  • c. Basis trades. An arbitrage strategy taking opposing long and short positions in two securities to profit from convergence. A trader could be long 10-year US Treasury bond futures and short physical 10-year Treasury bonds, expecting undervalued futures to appreciate relative to the overpriced bond. Making the trade worthwhile requires substantial leverage to enlarge the positions.
  • d. Spread trades. See 3.4.

3.4 Spread Trades

A spread normally needs less margin than two unrelated outright positions but is not risk-free: correlation can break, liquidity can vanish, and one leg may fill without the other. Types:

  • Intra-commodity / intra-delivery — same commodity and market, different delivery months.
  • Inter-commodity — related commodities, e.g. crude against refined products.
  • Intra-market — related contracts on the same exchange.
  • Inter-market — similar contracts on different exchanges.
  • Calendar / horizontal — same underlying, different months.

For a calendar spread, long the near month and short the deferred is generally a bullish view on the near-versus-deferred relationship; short near and long deferred is bearish. Profit depends on the change in the spread, not simply on whether the underlying rises.

3.4.1 Calendar Spread

With the S&P E-mini index at 2,344.00 on 19 April 2020, June at 2,335 and December at 2,330, a long June / short December spread requires initial margin of USD 70,050 + USD 69,900 = USD 139,950 — margins are not netted across the legs.

3.4.2 Butterfly Spread

Four legs across three maturities with weights +1, −2, +1. It targets curvature or relative richness and is more market-neutral than an outright.

3.4.3 Condor Spread

A condor also combines a bear and a bull spread, but the four expiry dates are all different, with equally distributed delivery months and a ratio of +1 : −1 : −1 : +1. Buying a condor means buying the nearest month, selling the next, selling the next, and buying the farthest. It typically has a wider but flatter profit zone than a butterfly.

8.5 × 25 = USD 212.50

The tick value is derived from

$$ 0.01 \times 0.01 \times 1{,}000{,}000 \times \frac{90}{360} = \text{USD } 25 $$

3.4.4 Other Spread Trades

3.4.4.1 TED Spread

The TED spread compares Treasury bill and Eurodollar (LIBOR) pricing. It is a credit and liquidity indicator, not a pure interest-rate measure: as the spread widens, default risk is perceived to rise and investors prefer safer assets; as it narrows, perceived default risk falls. Three-month T-bill futures were delisted from the CME, so the spread is now measured between the T-bill rate and 3-month USD LIBOR.

3.4.4.2 Inter-Product or Inter-Commodity Spread

Takes advantage of the value differential between two or more related commodities or financial products influenced by similar factors. A trader may look at the spread between two financial products — US T-bills and Eurodollar — as rates move, or between two related commodities such as gasoline and heating oil as crude moves. Rather than taking exposure to one product, the trader exploits the greater sensitivity and reaction of one relative to the other.

3.5 Hedging and Portfolio Management

Forwards and futures were created to hedge the future delivery price of a commodity. The same principle hedges or partially hedges a fall in portfolio value.

3.5.1 Identifying and Measuring Risk

Hedging reduces risk: an adverse move is contained or profit protected, but a favourable move has capped profit. Hedging does not eliminate price risk — it converts price risk into basis risk between the underlying and the hedging instrument, confining the final price to a determinable range.

In portfolio management, hedging is used to:

  • a. smooth cash flows;
  • b. simplify financial planning;
  • c. reduce working capital requirements;
  • d. allow more efficient product pricing; and
  • e. manage inventory more efficiently.

Before hedging, evaluate whether the likelihood and impact justify the cost and whether the hedge is dependable. Some subjectivity remains. Six questions structure the decision:

Factor Question Interpretation
1. Probability of negative change What is the probability of the price or rate change? What is the likelihood of the events that drive greater volatility? If such events are highly likely, or the historical probability is high, the need to hedge is greater.
2. Probable size of negative change What is the likely size of the change? What was the past record? A small change may need only a small partial hedge, or none. A historically large change may justify a hedge.
3. Overall impact of negative change What is the risk value of not hedging — small or large? If likelihood is small but impact catastrophic, hedging may still be worth it. A black swan rarely happens but causes mayhem when it does.
4. Transaction costs What are the brokerage, financing, initial and variation margin costs? How does the cost compare with the risk value? If cost is too high relative to probability or size of loss, do not hedge. Small diversified global portfolios face currency risk that may not be worth fully hedging.
5. Percentage of value to be hedged What percentage of principal does the transaction cost represent? Given the probability, hedging only part of the portfolio may be more cost-efficient.
6. Assumed basis risk What is the basis risk for the hedge? Basis risk is the likelihood the hedge does not fully work, because the asset and the hedge instrument do not offset. Review how well the hedge works and how much basis risk is tolerable.

The overall assessment is subjective and not fully quantifiable, but traders estimate a risk value and a hedging cost: if risk value exceeds hedging cost, hedge.

Worked risk-value decision

A treasury exposure has a 45% probability of an adverse change of 10–11.5%, giving a risk value of 5.18% of principal (0.45 × 11.5%). The hedge costs, on a principal of USD 1 million:

Cost Calculation Amount
Brokerage Given USD 15.00
Initial margin 1,500 × 12% × 3/12 USD 45.00
Additional margin 45 ticks USD 33.75
Total hedging cost USD 93.75

As a percentage of principal:

$$ \frac{93.75}{1{,}000{,}000} = 0.009375\% $$

Adding an assumed basis risk of 3.0%, total cost is 3.009375%, which is below the 5.18% risk value, so the decision is to hedge.

3.5.2 Developing an Effective Hedge Program

  • a. Risk exposure. Evaluate exposure in assets and liabilities by group, maturity or profit centre.
  • b. Objective. Set the hedge objective as an acceptable risk level or tolerance, measured by beta, volatility, value at risk or another risk unit.
  • c. Hedgeability. Determine it by price correlation analysis, using cross hedges where necessary — hedging a security with a positively correlated instrument with similar price movement.
  • d. Hedge vehicle. Choose an instrument with a high correlation to the target security.
  • e. Target. Determine the rate or price to lock in.

3.5.3 Determining the Hedge Instrument

Find an instrument highly correlated with the target. Where the target differs from the futures contract, evaluate the yield relationship and correlation between the two, measured by regression. Examples:

  • a. A long-term government bond is better hedged with 30-year T-bond futures than 3-month Eurodollar futures, because long-bond rates correlate more highly with 30-year T-bond rates.
  • b. T-bills are better hedged with matching T-bill futures than Eurodollar futures: in distress, T-bill futures prices rise faster than Eurodollar futures and the Eurodollar hedge becomes ineffective.
  • c. US T-bond futures hedge the interest-rate risk of US corporate bond portfolios; credit quality and sector risk are not hedged.
  • d. Economic factors hit sectors differently. In distress, funds switch from corporate to Treasury bonds, widening credit spreads and pushing corporate prices down relative to governments. Basis risk on a Treasury-futures hedge of corporate bonds is therefore greater, and the hedge less effective.

Futures and exchange-traded options carry four limitations as hedges:

  • i. round numbers of contracts;
  • ii. margin maintenance;
  • iii. time horizon and expiry dates; and
  • iv. no exact hedge instruments.

3.5.4 Determining the Target Rate

An investor may want to lock in a currency rate, interest rate or asset price. A minimum variance hedge held to the futures delivery date locks in the futures rate. If the hedge is lifted before delivery, the hedger cannot be assured of a spot or futures rate, because the basis changes.

Target rate for the hedge = futures rate + target-rate basis

For a long cash exposure hedged with a short futures position:

Vt = St + (F − Ft) = F + (St − Ft) = initial futures price + ending basis

3.5.5 Current and Anticipated Positions

3.5.5.1 For a current cash position

a. Weak form cash hedge. The portfolio's time horizon is not known, so the goal is to minimise variance in the rate of return for a specified holding period.

b. Strong form cash hedge. The horizon is finite or known; the goal is to protect the portfolio by minimising variance in expected total return for a given investment period. This is immunisation — matching asset and liability durations. A cash and futures portfolio is created and maintained with the same interest-rate sensitivity as a zero-coupon bond whose initial maturity equals the investment period. The zero-coupon bond's value and rate sensitivity are tracked continuously: whenever the cash portfolio's sensitivity falls below the zero-coupon bond's, futures are bought to raise it; whenever it exceeds the zero-coupon bond's, futures are sold to reduce it.

3.5.5.2 For an anticipated cash position

a. Weak form anticipated hedge. The goal is to minimise variance in the rate of return on an asset for a specified holding period, based on cash flows to be received at an unknown date. Futures are bought with the same interest-rate sensitivity as the asset to be acquired. Uncertain timing reduces effectiveness, though futures can still narrow the range of outcomes.

b. Strong form anticipated hedge. A known amount of cash will be received on a certain date; the goal is to minimise variance of the acquisition price. The hedge requires acquiring future delivery of the anticipated dollars of bonds, and because margin calls on the long futures position must be financed, the appropriate hedge ratio is not an exact dollar match.

3.5.6 Hedge Ratio

The hedge ratio is the expected movement in the value of the cash instrument given a movement in the futures contract; formally, the ratio of futures to spot that minimises or eliminates risk. Equal dollar exposure does not create an optimum or delta-neutral hedge.

$$ h = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta F} $$

where, for the hedged position,

V = S + h × F

and V is the value of the hedged position, S the security price and F the futures price. For a complete or delta-neutral hedge, ΔV = 0:

$$ h = -\frac{\Delta S}{\Delta F} $$

Number of contracts:

$$ \text{contracts} = \frac{-h \times \text{exposure value}}{\text{contract size}} $$

Worked example. A USD 100 million, 180-day loan. A 1% rate move on a 180-day basis changes value by:

$$ 0.01 \times \frac{180}{360} \times 1{,}000{,}000 = \text{USD } 5{,}000 $$

per million, and a 90-day Eurodollar contract moves by 100bp × USD 25 = USD 2,500. So:

$$ h = \frac{5{,}000}{2{,}500} = 2 $$

and the hedge needs:

$$ 2 \times \frac{100\text{m}}{1\text{m}} = \mathbf{200} \text{ contracts} $$

Sell 200 contracts for the 180-day exposure. A 90-day exposure would need 100. Two further points: holding a stack of 90-day contracts against a 180-day exposure creates basis risk at the roll, and margin calls arise before the hedge pays off. The ratio must be recalculated when the stack is rolled.

Strips and Stacks

A strip uses sequential futures delivery months matched to the exposure periods — three-month, six-month, nine-month — so each resets against its own contract. A stack concentrates the whole exposure in one or deferred contracts and rolls them; it is cheaper and more liquid but carries roll and basis risk. Choose between them by time horizon: an interpolative hedge uses a contract expiring beyond the exposure and interpolates; an extrapolative hedge uses a contract expiring before it.

3.5.7 Strips and Stacks

See the strip and stack definitions above: a strip matches sequential delivery months to the exposure periods, a stack concentrates the exposure in one contract or a deferred month and rolls it. Strips avoid roll risk but carry more contracts and lower liquidity per month; stacks are cheaper and more liquid but expose the hedger to basis at each roll.

3.5.8 Hedging Long-Term Interest Rate Risk

Compare dollar interest-rate sensitivity, not face value:

$$ \text{hedge ratio} = \frac{\text{PVBP of the hedged security}}{\text{PVBP of the cheapest-to-deliver bond}} \times \text{conversion factor of the CTD} $$

where PVBP is the change in price for a one basis point change in yield.

Worked example. A fixed-income manager, Alice Tan, hedges a USD 20 million bond position with Treasury futures. The CTD conversion factor is 0.91, the CTD's PVBP at the settlement date is 0.6895, and the PVBP of the bond to be hedged is 0.5954. Assuming a fixed yield spread between the two bonds:

$$ \text{hedge ratio} = \frac{0.5954}{0.6895} \times 0.91 = 0.7858071 \approx 0.79 $$

$$ \text{contracts} = -0.7858071 \times \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{100{,}000} = -157.16 $$

so sell 157 contracts.

3.5.9 Hedging Equity Risks

An equity portfolio carries two risks: systemic or market risk and specific or non-market risk. Equity index futures hedge only market risk. A manager offsets market exposure by selling futures when long in the market — a short hedge — or buying futures when short — a long hedge.

Two factors decide the number of contracts:

  • i. Beta. Measures portfolio risk or volatility relative to the index. The manager must decide whether the portfolio should be more or less volatile than the index, usually through regression on the historical betas of the constituent stocks.
  • ii. Modified Portfolio Value (MPV). The value of the actual portfolio multiplied by the weighted beta — the sum of each stock's beta multiplied by its percentage share of the portfolio. This forms the base hedge.

$$ N = \frac{V_P}{F \times T} \times \beta $$

where N is the number of contracts, VP the current portfolio value, F the current futures quote, T the value per tick and β the portfolio beta. The formula assumes the futures maturity is close to the hedge maturity.

Worked example. John Lim hedges an equity portfolio over three months with 3-month futures. The S&P index is 1,000 in September, December S&P 500 futures are 1,010, the portfolio is worth USD 5,050,000, its beta is 1.50, and each contract is worth USD 250:

$$ N = \frac{5{,}050{,}000}{250 \times 1{,}010} \times 1.50 = 20.0 \times 1.50 = 30.0 $$

so sell 30 December contracts.

December leg. The index falls to 900, futures to 902, and the portfolio falls 15% to USD 4,292,500. The futures gain:

(1, 010 − 902) × 30 × 250 = USD 810, 000

Total fund value:

4, 292, 500 + 810, 000 = USD 5, 102, 500

3.5.10 Structuring the Hedge

Contract month, timing and maturity sensitivity must be chosen against the exposure before the hedge is executed. Match the futures maturity as closely as the exposure allows, keep margin headroom for variation calls, and decide the lift date in advance.

3.5.11 Managing the Hedge

Timing, contract month and maturity sensitivity must be adjusted as the hedge progresses. A hedge with 90% effectiveness leaves 10% residual standard deviation: with a 10bp standard deviation on the basis, the hedged rate lands within ±10bp about 67% of the time. Lift-date basis and cross-hedge parameter error are the main sources of that residual. Adjust the contract month as the exposure rolls, re-run the ratio when basis moves, and decide in advance at what level the hedge is lifted.

3.6 Portfolio Management Techniques

Futures implement asset allocation, tactical rebalancing, cash equitisation, duration management, synthetic equity and bond exposure, market overlays and loss control. They are cheaper, more liquid and more margin-efficient than trading every cash security, but add basis, roll, leverage, margin and tracking risk. Separate market risk from security-specific risk: an index hedge will not perfectly protect a concentrated portfolio.

3.6.1 Examples of Portfolio Management Applications

a. Asset allocation switch. A USD 50 million portfolio moving from 50/50 to 40/60 equity/bond is implemented with bond futures, avoiding the cost and disruption of selling 50% of the equity book.

b. Synthetic money market. A corporate bond portfolio is converted to synthetic short-term exposure by selling T-bond futures, without liquidating the credit positions.

c. Jessie Goh. A USD 50 million portfolio with 10% into a short-term instrument at an 8% coupon. She buys July at 101 with 2.00 accrued, so the price is 103.00. In September the futures are 108 13/32 = 108.406, conversion factor 1.0, accrued 3.33, giving proceeds of 111.736 and a gain of 8.736, or 8.48%. The hedge ratio:

$$ N = \frac{5{,}000{,}000}{108{,}406.25} = 46.12 \rightarrow \text{sell 46 contracts} $$

Loss control also uses futures to defer loss realisation across reporting periods.

3.7 Arbitrage

Arbitrage strategies include cash-and-carry, reverse cash-and-carry, bond stripping and reconstitution, futures-versus-IRS, FRA-versus-futures and option parity.

3.7.1 Arbitrage Strategies

3.7.1.1 Cash versus Futures

Fifty days at 1.00/1.0625, 91 days, and 141 days at 1.25/1.3125 give r2 = 1.351%. The theoretical futures price is 98.65 against a market 98.72:

(9, 872 − 9, 865) × 25 = USD 175

3.7.1.2 FRA versus Futures

The interbank FRA trades at a 1–3bp spread against a 1bp futures spread, and value-date mismatch leaves residual basis and fixing risk. Avoid calendar events and data releases falling between the two dates.

3.7.1.3 Futures versus Interest Rate Swaps

IMM-dated, quarterly-fixing swaps align with futures dates. A speculative variant takes only the partial leg to express a curve view.

3.7.2 Arbitrage using Options

Put-call parity violations are arbitraged with conversion and reversal strategies, which Chapter 4 covers.

Arbitrage is risk-free only in the idealised model. Value dates, fixing dates, margin, liquidity, bid-ask spread, funding, delivery options, basis and counterparty constraints turn it into relative-value trading.

3.8 Summary

Know why each participant trades — hedge, speculate, make markets or arbitrage — what each spread isolates, how to compute the hedge ratio, and how margin and mark-to-market change cash needs. A hedge reduces a targeted risk; it does not remove all risk.

  • Butterfly weights +1, −2, +1 through three maturities; condor +1, −1, −1, +1 through four. A double butterfly nets buy 1 / sell 3 / buy 3 / sell 1.

4.1 What is an Option?

An option is a contract between the option writer (seller/issuer) and the option holder (buyer) giving the holder the right to buy from, or sell to, the writer at a contracted strike price within a specific period. There are two types: calls and puts.

The exchange-listed market dates from 1973, when the Chicago Board Options Exchange listed calls on 16 stocks; before that, options were OTC only. Singapore's options market remains largely OTC, which makes counterparty scrutiny important.

Call option:

  • a. Buyer. Has the right but not the obligation to buy the underlying at the strike within the period. On exercise the holder pays the strike and takes possession.
  • b. Writer. Receives the premium and must deliver the underlying if assigned.

Put option:

  • a. Buyer. Has the right but not the obligation to sell the underlying at the strike.
  • b. Writer. Receives the premium and must take delivery at the strike if assigned.

Exotic features include compound options, and underlyings extend to real estate, weather, volatility and ETFs.

4.2 Style of Option

4.2.1 Style of Option

European options are exercisable only at expiry; American options at any time up to expiry. OTC contracts may use Bermudan or event-dependent exercise. US listed equity options conventionally expire on the third Friday of the month, moving to the Thursday if that Friday is a holiday.

4.2.2 Strike Price

The contractual price at which the underlying is bought or sold on exercise.

4.2.3 Expiration Date

The last date on which the option may be exercised. Listed series follow monthly cycles coded JAJO (January, April, July, October), FMAN (February, May, August, November) and MJSD (March, June, September, December).

4.2.4 Class and Series

A class is all options of the same type on the same underlying; a series is all options of the same class with the same strike and expiry.

4.3 Option Price

option price (premium) = intrinsic value + time value

4.3.1 Intrinsic Value

For a call, exercise has value when the market price exceeds the strike; for a put, when the strike exceeds the market price.

IVcall = max (0, S − X)   IVput = max (0, X − S)

Moneyness Call Put
In-the-money (ITM) S > X, positive intrinsic value X > S, positive intrinsic value
At-the-money (ATM) S = X, zero intrinsic value X = S, zero intrinsic value
Out-of-the-money (OTM) S < X, zero intrinsic value X < S, zero intrinsic value

Call example. Strike X = 50, premium P = 6, underlying S = 54. Intrinsic value is 4 and time value 2. If the underlying is 48 at expiry, intrinsic value is 0.

Put example. Strike X = 50, premium P = 9, underlying S = 44. Intrinsic value is 6 and time value 3. If the underlying is 53 at expiry, intrinsic value is 0.

4.3.2 Time Value of Options

Time value is generally positive and falls to zero at expiry, so at expiration the option is worth exactly its intrinsic value:

option price = intrinsic value as time value → 0

The longer the time to expiry, the greater the time value. An option that looks worthless today — ATM or OTM, zero intrinsic value — may be in the money by expiry.

4.4.1 Underlying Price

A call gains as the underlying rises; a put loses.

4.4.2 Strike Price

A higher strike makes a call cheaper and a put dearer.

4.4.3 Time to Expiration

More time means more chance of finishing in the money, so both calls and puts carry more time value.

4.4.4 Volatility of the Underlying

Higher volatility widens the distribution of outcomes and raises the value of both calls and puts, because the holder's loss is floored at the premium while the upside is open.

4.4.5 Interest Rates

The interest forgone on the cash needed to buy the underlying — the opportunity cost, measured against a risk-free alternative such as Treasury bills — is embedded in the option. The higher the rate, the greater that opportunity cost, so there is a positive relationship between interest rates and call value; puts move inversely.

4.4.6 Expected Dividends

For equity options, a dividend reduces the share price when it goes ex-dividend, which lowers call value and raises put value.

4.5 Payoff Profiles

A payoff profile shows maximum gain, maximum loss and breakeven as a function of the underlying at expiry.

4.5.1 Buying a Call

An investor bought a call a month ago with premium USD 4, strike USD 100, and the share is now USD 110.

Payoff to the call buyer:

CT = ST − X if ST > X (ITM);  CT = 0 if ST ≤ X

Item Result
i. Payoff and profit Payoff is USD 10 (110 − 100); profit after premium is USD 6
ii. Breakeven The share must rise above the strike by the premium: USD 104
iii. Maximum gain Theoretically unlimited
iv. Maximum loss The premium paid, USD 4; if ST < X exercise does not occur and the option expires worthless
v. Obligations Apart from paying the premium, none unless the buyer exercises, and then to pay the strike

4.5.2 Selling a Call

An investor sells a call with premium USD 4, strike USD 100, share at USD 90.

Payoff to the writer:

CT = −(ST − X) if ST > X;  CT = 0 if ST ≤ X

Item Result
i. Payoff and profit Below USD 100 there is no reason to exercise, so payoff is zero and profit is the USD 4 premium. Above the strike the profit erodes and becomes a loss beyond USD 104
ii. Breakeven USD 104 — identical to the buyer's
iii. Maximum gain The USD 4 premium, when the share is at or below the strike
iv. Maximum loss Theoretically unlimited
v. Obligations Must deliver if assigned

4.5.3 Buying a Put

An investor buys a put with premium USD 4, strike USD 130, share at USD 120.

Item Result
i. Payoff and profit Payoff USD 10 (130 − 120); profit USD 6
ii. Breakeven USD 126 (130 − 4)
iii. Maximum gain USD 126 at an underlying of zero, being the strike less the premium
iv. Maximum loss The premium, USD 4
v. Obligation None except on exercise

4.5.4 Selling a Put

An investor sells a put with premium USD 4, strike USD 130, underlying at USD 140 initially and USD 120 at expiry.

Payoff to the writer is the mirror image of the holder:

PT = −(X − ST) if ST < X;  PT = 0 if ST ≥ X

Item Result
i. Payoff and profit Zero above USD 130, with the USD 4 premium as maximum profit; below the strike the profit erodes and turns to loss
ii. Breakeven USD 126
iii. Maximum gain The USD 4 premium
iv. Maximum loss USD 126, when the underlying falls to zero
v. Obligation Must take delivery at the strike if assigned

4.6 Put-Call Parity

4.6.1 Definition

For European options on the same underlying, strike and expiry, with consistent units and dividends correctly treated:

C + PV(X) = P + S

The relationship assumes no arbitrage, a frictionless market, known dividends, the same exercise style and no early exercise.

4.6.2 Arbitrage

A violation is traded as a conversion or reversal. If C + PV(X) > P + S, sell the call, buy the put and buy the stock. If the left side is cheaper, reverse the trade. Funding, dividends, exercise style, transaction costs and short-sale constraints determine whether the arbitrage is real.

4.6.3 Synthetic Securities

Synthetic position Built from
Long stock Long call + short put at the same strike (plus the parity bond)
Short stock Long put + short call
Long call Long underlying + long put
Long put Short underlying + long call
Short call Short underlying + short put
Short put Long underlying + short call

Each synthetic reproduces the cash flows of the position on the left, so a trader can choose whichever route is cheaper after funding and transaction cost.

4.7 Valuation

4.7.1 Black-Scholes

The model takes six inputs — spot, strike, time to expiry, interest rate, dividends and volatility — and returns a theoretical premium. Binomial trees handle early exercise and American features. The examination does not require model calculations, but does require explanation.

4.7.2 Volatility

Volatility is the annualised standard deviation of daily price changes. Historical volatility is measured from past prices; implied volatility is backed out of the observed option price and represents the market's forward-looking estimate. The two can diverge.

4.7.3 The Option Greeks

The Greeks measure sensitivity of the premium to each input.

Greek Measures Typical sign for a long option
Delta Change in option price per unit change in underlying Positive for calls, negative for puts
Gamma Change in delta per unit change in underlying Positive
Theta Time decay Negative
Vega Sensitivity to implied volatility Positive
Rho Sensitivity to the interest rate Positive for calls, negative for puts

4.7.4 Delta

$$ \delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} $$

A delta of 0.50 means a USD 2 move in the underlying changes the option price by USD 1.00. Delta approaches 1 for deep in-the-money options near expiry and 0 for deep out-of-the-money options.

Delta hedging. A writer short 1,000 calls with delta 0.642 loses USD 642 on a one-unit rise; hedging requires buying 642 units of the underlying.

4.7.5 Gamma

$$ \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = \frac{\partial \delta}{\partial S} $$

Gamma is largest at-the-money and close to expiry, when delta changes fastest. A high-gamma book requires frequent rebalancing.

4.7.6 Theta

Time decay, normally negative for a long option. At-the-money options lose the most dollar value per day: a one-day theta of −0.0250 is −2.5 cents a day.

4.7.7 Vega

Sensitivity to a one-percentage-point change in implied volatility; a vega of 0.090 means the premium changes by 9 cents per volatility point. Vega is larger at-the-money and for longer-dated options. Implied volatility is obtained by inverting the pricing model against the observed price.

4.7.8 Rho

Sensitivity to a one-percentage-point change in the interest rate; a rho of 0.060 means the premium changes by 6 cents per point. Call rho is positive and put rho negative, because higher rates raise the opportunity cost of holding the underlying.

4.7.9 Practical Hedging Problems

Delta hedging is dynamic and imperfect. Rebalancing costs, jumps through levels, liquidity, changing volatility, discrete monitoring and model error all remain. A market maker manages the whole Greek book, not just delta.

4.10.2 Bond Options

Bond options are mostly OTC, with expiries set well before bond maturity. A bank buying a call at a strike of USD 102 with the bond at USD 101 gains protection against a rise in price — that is, a fall in yield.

4.10.3 Interest Rate Options

Caps, floors and collars are the interest-rate equivalents of calls, puts and combinations. CME lists options on US Treasuries and Eurodollar; ICE on Euribor; SGX on the 10-year Mini-JGB and Euroyen (TIBOR).

4.10.3.1 Options on Eurodollar Futures

Options on short-rate futures are quoted on 100 − yield. One tick is:

$$ \text{USD } 1{,}000{,}000 \times \frac{1}{10{,}000} \times \frac{90}{360} = \text{USD } 25 $$

A quarter tick is USD 6.25 in the spot month and a half tick USD 12.50 in other months.

Mid-curve example. A 1-year Eurodollar mid-curve option on the Dec-2019 futures at 96.93, American style, tick value USD 25, expiry June 2019, is struck on a ladder from 9,637.50 to 9,750.00. Call prices run 51.50, 42.00, 33.25, 26.25, 20.25, 15.50, 12.00, 9.25, 7.25 and 5.75; put prices 4.00, 6.75, 10.50, 15.75, 22.50, 29.75, 38.75, 48.25, 58.75 and 69.75.

Hedge arithmetic. A borrower wanting to cap the rate at 2.75% buys the strike-97.250 option (that is, 100 − 2.750) at a cost of 9.25bp, so the realised rate is:

2.7500% − 0.0925% = 2.6575%

A lender buying the put at 48.25bp achieves:

2.7500% + 0.4825% = 3.2325%

4.10.3.2 Interest Rate Caps

A cap is a series of call-like options on a reference rate, setting a maximum. A SGD 200 million 3-year cap at 2.00% on 3-month SIBOR, struck in March 2018 when SIBOR was 1.375%, pays when SIBOR fixes at 2.125% in June 2019:

$$ \frac{90}{360} \times (2.125\% - 2.00\%) \times 200{,}000{,}000 = \text{SGD } 62{,}500 $$

4.10.3.3 Interest Rate Floors

A floor is a series of put-like options setting a minimum rate, protecting a lender or depositor against falling rates.

4.10.3.4 Interest Rate Collars

A collar buys one option and sells another, reducing or eliminating premium at the cost of capping the benefit. A borrower buys a cap and sells a floor; the sold floor funds the bought cap.

4.10.4 Currency Options

A currency option involves two currencies, not one price. A USD/JPY call is economically the same exposure as a JPY/USD put. Large notionals and a 24-hour market heighten monitoring and counterparty risk.

4.10.5 Options on Futures

An option on a futures contract confers the right to enter a futures position at the strike. Such options are typically more liquid than options on the cash instrument, but they introduce a delivery problem when cross-hedging. All SGX options are futures options, American style, with in-the-money positions auto-exercised. The SGX Nifty 50 option covers 50 large, liquid NSE stocks representing about 70% of free float, is cash settled on the average weighted prices of the components, and benefits from the CME mutual-offset arrangement.

4.10.6 Swaptions

A swaption is an option to enter a swap at a fixed rate on a future date. A payer swaption gives the right to pay fixed — it protects against rising rates. A receiver swaption gives the right to receive fixed — it protects against falling rates. The terms that matter are premium, expiry, notional, the fixed rate and floating index, payment frequency and day count.

4.11 Basic Option Strategies

4.11.1 Call Buying Strategies

Calls are bought for four reasons: capital investment, taking risk exposure, extracting cash from a position, and hedging.

Hedging a short. An investor is short at USD 12.00 and buys a call at strike 12.40 for 0.80. Breakeven is 11.20; maximum loss USD 1.20; maximum gain USD 11.20 if the share falls to zero.

4.11.2 Call Writing Strategies

Covered call. Buy the stock at S0 = 12.00, write a call at X = 12.40 for C0 = 0.80.

Profit = ST − max (0, ST − X) − S0 + C0

Item Calculation Result
Breakeven S0 − C0 = 12.00 − 0.80 11.20
Maximum gain If ST > X: X − S0 + C0 = 12.40 − 12.00 + 0.80 1.20
Maximum loss If ST = 0: 0 − 12.00 + 0.80 −11.20

The call will not be exercised once the shares have fallen, so the writer keeps the premium but bears the full fall in the stock.

4.11.3 Put Buying Strategies

Protective put. Stock at USD 12.00, put at strike 11.60 for 0.80. Breakeven 12.80; maximum loss USD 1.20; upside retained without limit above the breakeven.

4.11.4 Put Writing Strategies

A put writer earns premium in exchange for a conditional obligation to buy the underlying. A bull ELN works this way: a one-month note issued at USD 9,900 against USD 10,000 face, a vanilla yield of 5% p.a., ABC spot USD 10 and a put struck at USD 9.

  • If ST ≥ 9, the note returns USD 10,000: a gain of 1.01% for the month, or 12.82% p.a.
  • If ST < 9, the note delivers 1,111 shares. At USD 8.80 those are worth USD 9,776.80, a loss of 1.24%.

The uplift over the vanilla note — 7.82% p.a. — is exactly the premium on the embedded short put.

4.12 Market Driven Strategies

4.12.1 Neutral Strategies

Used when the investor expects little movement. Premium is collected by selling options, and the risk is a break out of the range.

4.12.2 Bullish Strategies

Long call, bull call spread, bull put spread. The spread versions cap both gain and loss.

4.12.3 Bearish Strategies

Long put, bear put spread, bear call spread.

4.12.4 Creating Spreads

A spread pairs a long and a short option of the same type, differing in strike or expiry. Debit spreads pay premium — bull call, bear put. Credit spreads receive premium — bull put, bear call.

4.12.5 Straddle

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike. With ABC at USD 20 in September, buying the October 20 call and the October 20 put produces three outcomes at expiry:

Outcome Result
ST = 20 Both expire worthless; the loss is the whole cash outlay
ST > 20 The call is in the money; profit is ST − 20 − outlay, potentially unlimited
ST < 20 The put is in the money; profit is 20 − ST − outlay, with a maximum payout of USD 20 at zero

The strategy is neutral on direction and long volatility. Maximum loss is the cash outlay, occurring precisely at the strike.

4.12.6 Strangle

A strangle buys a call and a put at different out-of-the-money strikes. With ABC at USD 20, buy the October 25 call and the October 15 put. It costs less than a straddle but needs a larger move. The short strangle does the reverse: selling the call for USD 2 and the put for USD 1 collects USD 3. At an underlying of 14 the put loses USD 1 while the call gains USD 2; at 26 the call loses USD 1.

4.12.7 Bull Call Spread

ABC at USD 21. Buy the October 20 call at 1.50 and sell the October 25 call at 0.75, for a net debit of 0.75.

Item Result
Breakeven 20.75
Maximum profit 4.25 (the 5-point strike gap less the 0.75 debit)

4.12.8 Bull Put Spread

ABC at USD 24. Buy the October 20 put at 0.75 and sell the October 25 put at 1.25, for a net credit of 0.50. Maximum loss is 4.50; at an underlying of 23 the result is −1.50.

4.12.9 Bear Call Spread

ABC at USD 18. Buy the October 20 call at 1.50 and sell the October 15 call at 3.50, for a net credit of 2.00. Maximum loss is 3.00.

4.12.10 Bear Put Spread

ABC at USD 18. Buy the October 15 put at 1.00 and sell the October 20 put at 3.00, for a net debit of 2.00. Maximum gain is 3.00.

4.13 Hedging

4.13.1 Protect an Equity Portfolio

A protective put floors the downside at the strike after the premium. With ABC at USD 20, selling the USD 30 call for 1.50 is a covered call: the premium is earned but the upside above 30 is surrendered — the opportunity cost of the strategy.

4.13.2 Manage Currency Exposure

An importer with USD 2 million payable, USD/JPY at 100, buys a USD call / JPY put struck at 105. The exporter's mirror position is set out below.

Spot at expiry Unhedged proceeds Hedged with a 105 put at JPY 1 premium
100 100 99
110 110 109
90 90 94

The hedge floors the rate at 94 while retaining the upside above 105, less the JPY 1 premium.

4.13.3 Zero-Cost Options

A zero-cost collar buys a put and sells an out-of-the-money call so the premiums roughly offset.

NVDA costless collar. Spot USD 156.00. Sell 1,000 January-2020 USD 160 calls at USD 15.30; buy 1,000 January-2020 USD 155 puts at USD 15.30. Net outlay is zero.

  • Below 155, say 125: the put pays USD 30 against a stock loss of USD 31 per share, so a residual loss of USD 1,000 across the position.
  • Between 155 and 160: neither option pays.
  • Above 160: the stock is called away; upside is capped at the call strike.

The collar is not free of risk — the residual USD 1,000 is the gap between the two strikes — and it assumes no dividends and no interest over the life.

4.13.4 Summary of Options Strategies

The full strategy map crosses the market view — yield, bullish, neutral, protective, bearish — against the structure, and records whether each is a debit or credit, its maximum gain and its maximum loss. Learn the payoff of every single leg before combining them; the combination is only the sum of the legs.

4.14 Summary

Options separate rights from obligations. Price is intrinsic plus time value, and volatility, time, rates and dividends drive the premium. Learn payoff, breakeven and maximum loss for each leg before combining legs. Parity explains synthetic positions and the Greeks explain risk management.

5.1 Introduction

Besides futures and options, Specified Investment Products (SIPs) are products with structures, features and risks that may be more complex, including structured warrants, daily leveraged certificates (DLCs) and synthetic exchange-traded funds (ETFs).

Under MAS guidelines enhancing safeguards for retail investors, an investment representative must assess whether a client has the relevant knowledge and experience before the client can invest in a SIP. Investors wishing to trade SIPs complete a Customer Account Review (CAR) with their broker or representative. The qualifying criteria are educational qualifications, work experience and investment experience; the investor must satisfy one of the three.

5.2 Introduction to Structured Warrants

A structured warrant is issued by a third-party financial institution and gives exposure to the performance of an underlying for a fraction of its price. Like an option, it lets the investor profit from a bull market (call warrants) or a bear market (put warrants).

The value of a warrant is influenced by the underlying, which may be:

  • a. individual stocks;
  • b. equity indices;
  • c. investment funds; or
  • d. commodities.

Structured warrants have a short finite lifespan, usually under a year and mostly 3–6 months. A "structured" call warrant holder has the right to buy the underlying at a predetermined price on or before expiry, depending on the exercise style. Some are listed and traded on the SGX-ST platform. Different designs serve investors with different objectives and risk profiles: a zero-strike warrant — essentially a synthetic stock — has an exercise price of zero, so it is always in the money, and its cash settlement and warrant price equal the underlying's closing price.

5.2.1 European versus American Style Call and Put Warrants

European-style warrants are exercisable only at expiry; American-style warrants throughout their life. SGX-listed structured warrants are European style, and for the CM-SIP examination assume European style throughout. Warrants are less popular in the US, where American options dominate.

A European call warrant gives the right to purchase the underlying at a fixed strike at expiry. If the price at expiry is above the strike, the investor earns the difference. A call buyer holds a bullish view. If the price rises towards the strike during the warrant's life the warrant's value rises, but there is no exercise before expiry; if the price is volatile and falls back below the strike, the warrant's value falls but no loss is realised.

A European put warrant gives the right to sell at a fixed strike at expiry. If the strike exceeds the market price at expiry, the investor profits. A put buyer holds a negative view. Settlement at expiry is in cash, with typically no delivery of the underlying.

  • Call settlement: (settlement price of the underlying at expiry − exercise price of the warrant) ÷ conversion ratio.
  • Put settlement: (exercise price of the warrant − settlement price of the underlying at expiry) ÷ conversion ratio.

5.2.2 Structured Warrants versus Standard Company Warrants

Standard company warrants, often issued as a sweetener for a bond issue, are covered in the CM-EIP examination. Structured warrants differ in five ways:

# Difference
a. Structured warrants may be call or put warrants; company warrants are call only. Structured warrants therefore work in bull and bear markets.
b. Structured warrants have shorter durations, usually not more than 6 months.
c. Exercise causes no dilution of company earnings, because no new shares are issued. Structured warrants are cash settled, without delivery of shares.
d. They are issued by third parties and provide no new capital to the underlying company.
e. Designated market makers act as counterparties, so investors are exposed to issuer and market-maker counterparty risk.

5.2.3 Benefits of Structured Warrants

Warrants are created by financial institutions for profit, but in response to demand for alternative exposure to asset-price movement. The benefits are:

  • a. Call warrants give leverage to the upside at a fraction of the cost of the underlying.
  • b. Put warrants give leverage to the downside, offering portfolio managers some protection against a falling portfolio.
  • c. Losses are limited to the value of the warrants purchased.
  • d. They give access to diverse markets — equities, baskets and select indices — without a large commitment.
  • e. Because they are paid for upfront, there are no margin calls, unlike options and CFDs.

Structured warrants are popular and actively traded in Singapore, Hong Kong, London and Mumbai. They are less popular in the US, where American options are preferred.

5.3 Key Terms

5.3.1 Conversion Ratio

The conversion ratio is the number of warrants needed to buy or sell one unit of the underlying. If the ratio to buy ABC shares is 5:1, the holder needs five warrants per share.

5.3.2 Exercise or Strike Price

The conversion may also be expressed as an exercise price, in addition to or instead of a ratio: the price at which the holder can buy or sell the underlying.

5.3.3 Expiry Date

The expiration date is the last day on or before which the warrant must be exercised.

5.3.4 Issue Price

The price at which the structured warrant is initially sold or issued by the issuer.

5.3.5 Physical Delivery or Cash Settlement

Warrants may settle by physical delivery or in cash. On exercise, a call holder receives the underlying (or cash) from the issuer; a put holder delivers it. The settlement style is fixed at issue, and SGX-ST structured warrants are cash settled.

5.3.6 Gearing

Structured warrants are priced at a fraction of the share price, so the investor can trade more warrants than the underlying for the same outlay. A small gain in the underlying can produce a larger gain in the call warrant, and a fall a larger loss.

Gearing indicates how many more warrants can be bought for a given capital than shares. At 8× gearing, the investor gets eight times the underlying exposure for the same money.

5.4 Factors Affecting the Price of a Structured Warrant

The warrant price is the speculative value investors will pay. Seven factors drive it.

5.4.1 Life of the Warrant

More time means more chance an out-of-the-money warrant becomes attractive as the underlying moves. As life shortens the speculative value decays. On the day before expiry the warrant is worth only its intrinsic value; the day after expiry it is worthless.

5.4.2 Performance of the Underlying Asset

Call warrants are worth more as the underlying rises; put warrants as it falls.

5.4.3 Exercise Price Level

A higher exercise price makes a call less likely to be exercised, so it is worth less. A higher exercise price makes a put more likely to be exercised, so the put is worth more.

5.4.4 Price Volatility of the Underlying Asset

The more volatile the underlying, the more likely the warrant will appreciate within a given period, so investors pay a larger premium. The market prices the warrant on the underlying's volatility; implied volatility is that market quotation.

Warrant buyers are long volatility; writers are short volatility. Holding everything else constant, buying at 7% implied volatility and selling at 8% produces a profit, because higher volatility means a higher warrant price. Conversely a seller at 7% gains if volatility falls below 5%.

5.4.5 Dividends

Warrant holders are not entitled to dividends, but a dividend declaration has a mild effect: call warrants lose slightly and put warrants gain slightly.

5.4.6 Interest Rate

A higher interest rate raises the hedging cost for call warrants written by the issuer, who must delta hedge by buying the shares, so call warrant prices rise. Hedging put warrants becomes cheaper at higher rates, so put warrant prices fall.

5.4.7 Leverage

Warrants offering greater leverage through the conversion ratio command higher prices.

Factor Call warrant Put warrant
Life of the warrant Longer → higher Longer → higher
Exercise price Higher → lower Higher → higher
Underlying asset price Higher → higher Higher → lower
Volatility Higher → higher Higher → higher
Dividend expectations Mild negative Mild positive

5.5.4 Delta

Warrant delta measures the change in warrant price per unit change in the underlying, scaled by the ratio:

$$ \delta = n \times \frac{\partial WP}{\partial S} $$

Delta is about 0.5 at the money, approaching 1 deep in the money. The DBS call carried a delta above 90% (95%); the GS put carried −45.33%.

5.5.5 Effective Gearing

Effective gearing combines delta and gearing and approximates short-term percentage sensitivity far better than gearing alone:

effective gearing = δ × gearing

For the DBS call, 0.95 × 7 = 6.65×. For the GS put, −0.4533 × 16.92 = −7.67×; the statistics box alongside the same example prints −7.58×, so treat the two as the same magnitude with a rounding or basis difference and quote whichever figure a question supplies.

5.5.6 Implied Volatility

Implied volatility is backed out of the observed warrant price; it is the market's current pricing of the underlying's future volatility, not a forecast, and it moves independently of realised volatility.

5.6 Valuation

5.6.1 Intrinsic Value

$$ IV_{\text{call}} = \max\left[0, \frac{S - X}{n}\right] \qquad IV_{\text{put}} = \max\left[0, \frac{X - S}{n}\right] $$

5.6.2 Conversion Price

call conversion price = X + n × WP   put conversion price = X − n × WP

5.6.3 Premium

The warrant premium is the price above intrinsic value. The dollar premium is:

call = n × WP + X − S   put = n × WP − X + S

DBS: intrinsic value SGD 0.2075, conversion price SGD 19.10, premium 5.18%. GS: with the underlying at 24,981.38, intrinsic value SGD 0.2773, conversion price SGD 25,560, premium −2.32% — a negative premium, because the put was out of the money with little time left.

5.7 Roles of the Holder and Issuer

The listing document sets out the terms and the contractual rights and obligations of both parties.

  • Holder's right and obligation. The right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a stated number of shares at the exercise price within the period. The holder's only obligation is to pay the exercise price if it chooses to exercise.
  • Issuer's right and obligation. To deliver the underlying and receive the exercise price on a call exercise, or receive the underlying and pay the exercise price on a put exercise. Most, if not all, SGX-ST structured warrants settle in cash rather than by delivery.

5.8 Role of Designated Market Makers in Structured Warrant Trading

An issuer that commits to make a market in the warrants it issues is exempt from the minimum placement and holder-size requirements for listed equity securities, and the minimum issue size is SGD 2 million (previously SGD 5 million).

The listing document states whether the issuer has made that commitment. A designated market maker (DMM) appointed by the issuer must then provide competitive bid and offer prices during trading hours as prescribed by SGX, creating adequate liquidity. The maximum spread between the DMM's bid and offer, and the minimum lot size, are set out by the issuer in the listing document. DMMs may be large international banks and brokers able to react to changing conditions and generate demand.

The spread and quote size are part of the investor's liquidity risk: a DMM that is not obliged to quote, or is quoting wide, may not offer an exit at a fair price.

5.9 Settlement of Warrants

Structured warrants trade and settle like ordinary shares and company warrants listed on SGX-ST.

5.9.1 Physical Settlement

To exercise a call warrant the holder submits an exercise notice with cash payment of the exercise price to the warrant agent. The agent informs CDP and the issuer; CDP credits the holder's account with the underlying securities and debits the issuer's.

For a put warrant, CDP debits the holder's account for the securities and credits the issuer's, and the warrant agent makes a cash payment on the issuer's behalf to the holder. Whichever the type, exercise expenses are payable by the holder to the warrant agent.

5.9.2 Cash Settlement

Settlement is the in-the-money amount per warrant multiplied by the number of warrants, paid in cash rather than by delivery. This is the normal route for SGX-ST structured warrants.

5.9.3 Settlement Procedures

SGX uses Asian-style expiry. In the "Ready" and "Unit-Share" markets the last trading day is at least three business days before expiry, and the settlement price is the arithmetic average of official closing prices over the five market days before expiry. For the DBS warrant above, the last trading day was 25 September 2017 against an expiry of 2 October 2017.

The averaging makes the final payoff path dependent and prevents a single closing print from setting the settlement.

5.10 Corporate Actions

Dividends, special dividends, buybacks and cancellations, rights and bonus issues, and splits can all adjust the strike and conversion ratio so the product stays economically comparable. The adjustment factors are:

Event Factor
Dividend / special dividend $$\dfrac{P - SD - ND}{P - ND}$$
Buyback or cancellation $$\dfrac{A}{B}$$ and $$\dfrac{P - CD}{P}$$
Split $$\dfrac{A}{B}$$

The new strike = old strike × factors, and the new conversion ratio = old conversion ratio × factors. Read the issuer's adjustment announcement rather than assuming the terms.

5.11 Exotic Warrants

5.11.1 Index Warrants

Settled on exercise by cash. The cash received is the difference between the exercise level and the current or settlement index level, multiplied by a multiplier converting index points into cash.

For call warrants on MSCI Singapore Free Index futures:

$$ \text{Cash settlement} = \max\left\{0,\ \text{SGD } 1.00 \times \left[\frac{\text{Settlement Reference Level} - \text{Exercise Level}}{n}\right]\right\} $$

The settlement reference level is based on the underlying index futures contract's final settlement price as determined by the derivatives exchange. Because the index has already been adjusted for corporate actions, index warrants normally need no adjustment.

5.11.2 Currency Translated Warrants

The settlement and trading currency — the investor's home currency — differs from the underlying asset's currency. For a structured warrant on the HSI listed on SGX-ST, the underlying and reference currency is HKD while settlement is in SGD.

$$ \text{Settlement amount} = \max\left\{0,\ \text{HKD } 1 \times \left[\frac{\text{Reference Level} - \text{Exercise Level}}{n}\right] \times \text{Exchange Rate (SGD/HKD)}\right\} $$

5.11.3 Warrants with Yield Enhancement

Designed to give an attractive yield based on the current share or spot price. For marketing, capped warrants are often grouped with other yield-enhancement securities as discount certificates.

In a capped warrant the cap strike is the maximum price achievable. Upside exposure is sacrificed in return for buying the underlying at a discount to market. At maturity, if the closing price is at or above the exercise price the holder receives cash equal to the exercise price — the cap; if below, the holder receives the value of the underlying.

Worked example. Issue price of the warrant SGD 5.25, which is 89.0% of the reference spot; reference spot SGD 5.90; underlying ABC Ltd; conversion ratio n = 1. The cap is SGD 5.45, being 92.4% of spot. At or above 5.45 the holder receives 5.45; below it the holder receives the underlying value, so a fall below 5.25 produces a loss despite the discount.

5.11.4 Basket Warrants

The warrant references a basket of underlyings. Correlation, the weights and the composition of the basket matter, and a worst-of basket pays on the weakest constituent.

5.11.5 Commodity Warrants

Commodity warrants typically carry longer tenors than commodity futures or options. The terms must verify the benchmark used for settlement and the delivery-location, storage and transport arrangements, because a commodity's price is location- and grade-specific.

5.11.6 FX Warrants

FX warrants give directional exposure to a currency pair. A USD/JPY call and a JPY/USD put express the same view; the quotation convention must be checked. Commodity currencies such as AUD and CAD carry additional sensitivity to their export commodities.

5.12 Daily Leveraged Certificates

5.12.1 DLC Features

DLCs are exchange-traded, short-term, high-risk products delivering a daily multiple of an underlying's movement. Long and short certificates are commonly issued at 3× or 5×, with terms permitting leverage up to 7×; the current term sheet controls the multiple.

5.12.1.1 Daily Reset and Compounding

Daily reset means a DLC is not a simple multiple of the underlying's total return. In a volatile or mean-reverting market, repeated daily losses erode capital even if the index ends where it started.

The five-day compounding arithmetic is stark. At +2% a day for five days the index gains 10.41%, while a 3× long gains 33.82% and a 3× short loses 33.82%; a 5× long gains 61.05% and a 5× short loses 61.05%.

5.12.1.2 Designated Market Maker Obligations

A DLC DMM must quote within a maximum bid-offer spread of 10 ticks or SGD 0.20, whichever is greater, and for a minimum quantity of 10,000 DLCs. Quote obligations may stand down in six circumstances: in the five minutes before the pre-open; when the underlying is suspended or subject to price limits; when the DLC is suspended or limited; in exceptional volatility; on technical failure; or where the DMM is unable to hedge or unwind.

5.12.1.3 The Airbag

The airbag suspends the product when the underlying moves adversely by the stated threshold, then restarts it from an observed reset level after a 30-minute suspension.

Worked example. Prior close SGD 2.00, index 1,000. At 11:00 the index is 900, a 10% fall, and the 5× DLC is SGD 1.00, a 50% fall. The airbag triggers a suspension from 11:00 to 11:30. A further 5% fall in the index is 25% of SGD 1.00, so the DLC reopens at SGD 0.75. Without the airbag it would have been SGD 0.50. A further 10% fall takes it to zero. The post-trigger observation window is 15 minutes.

5.12.2 DLC Benefits

Eight benefits define the product: exchange trading and transparency; defined leverage; long and short access from one instrument; no need for a margin account; capital limited to the purchase price; no implied volatility effect, because the payoff is delta-one rather than optional; no time decay in the option sense; and a single, simple underlying.

5.12.3 DLCs at Expiry

Unlike leveraged ETFs, DLCs are not reverse-split to keep the price in a range, so they can go to zero and stay there. At 2% a day against a 5× product, the price path is roughly 2.50 → 0.01 over 50 days. The Dow Jones fell around 50% from October 2007 to February 2009; a leveraged product held through such a path loses a multiple of that.

Compared with CFDs, DLCs are exchange traded and have no OTC financing or margin account in the same form. Compared with warrants, they target a daily multiple rather than an option payoff. Compared with leveraged ETFs, their issuer, reset mechanics and the absence of reverse splits differ.

5.13 Summary

Warrants embed options issued by a bank; the price equals intrinsic value plus time value and is shaped by the conversion ratio, gearing, volatility, dividends, rates, time, liquidity and issuer credit. DLCs amplify daily returns and reset, making path dependency and compounding the central risks. SIP/CAR suitability and disclosure are part of the product analysis, not an administrative add-on.

Exam cues

  • Suitability first: warrants and DLCs are SIPs, so a CAR must be completed and the investor must meet one of the three criteria — education, work experience or investment experience.
  • Cash settlement formulas: call = (S − X)/n; put = (X − S)/n. Gearing = share price ÷ (warrant price × n). Effective gearing = delta × gearing.
  • Conversion price: call = X + nWP; put = X − nWP. The DBS example: 0.665 × 8 + 16.50 = SGD 21.82 breakeven.
  • Seven price factors: life, underlying price, exercise price, volatility, dividends (mild), interest rate and leverage. Calls rise with the underlying, volatility, rates and gearing and fall with dividends; puts invert the spot, dividend and rate signs.
  • Naming: underlying, then index exercise level, then two-letter issuer, e for European, CW/PW/DC, then YYMMDD.
  • DMM relief: a market-making issuer is exempt from placement and holder-size rules, with a SGD 2 million minimum issue size (was SGD 5 million).
  • Asian expiry: last trading day at least three business days before expiry; settlement is the average of the five closing prices before expiry.
  • DLC numbers: board lot 100; up to 7×; maximum tenure 3 years; airbag 20% at 3× and 10% at 5×; a 30-minute suspension; DMM spread 10 ticks or SGD 0.20, minimum 10,000 certificates.
  • Compounding: five days at +2% gives +10.41% on the index but +33.82% on a 3× long. A 2.49% index fall produced an 11.66% DLC fall.
  • Trap: a warrant can rise 51.2% on the screen and still lose money when held to maturity, because of decay. And a leveraged product is never a buy-and-hold instrument — the daily reset, not the total return, is what is delivered.

Chapter 6 — Barrier Options, Binary Options and Callable Contracts

Exotic features are sold as cheaper or higher-yielding alternatives to vanilla exposure. They are not cheaper — the cost is displaced into a condition, a path dependency or a termination right. This chapter is about locating that displacement.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Explain how an exotic option differs from a vanilla option.
  • Distinguish knock-out from knock-in barriers and single from double barriers.
  • Explain the effect of a barrier on the premium and on the path dependence of the payoff.
  • Explain binary and basket payoffs.
  • Explain the mandatory call event and the Category N / Category R distinction for CBBCs.
  • Compute a CBBC's theoretical price, gearing and residual value.

6.1 Introduction

Exotic derivatives extend a vanilla option by changing one or more of its features:

  • a. Exercise events — conditions under which the option lives or dies.
  • b. Path dependency — the payoff depends on the route the price took, not just the level at expiry.
  • c. Payoff shape — digital, range, or otherwise discontinuous rather than linear.
  • d. Conditional events or trigger points — if the price touches or crosses a trigger, validity is affected and the final payoff changes.
  • e. Underlying assets — extending beyond traditional classes and indices to alternative investments such as real estate, and to market indicators such as volatility.
  • f. Currency features — producing a payoff in a different currency, as in a Quanto structure.

Before recommending one, explain how the payoff is computed, what observation triggers action, whether the option can terminate early, whether the premium can be lost, and who bears counterparty risk.

6.2 Barrier Options

Barrier options are among the most popular exotic structures banks sell. Clients find them easy to understand because the concept is "game-like", following an if-then-or-else pattern.

The payoff depends not only on whether and by how much the option is in the money at exercise, but also on whether the underlying has:

  • a. touched the barrier at any time during the option's life;
  • b. been above or below the barrier at expiry, or throughout the life; or
  • c. stayed within two barriers, or outside two barriers, at expiry or throughout.

In a knock-out (or kick-out) option, touching the barrier deactivates the features and rights. In a knock-in (or kick-in) option, touching the barrier activates them.

Investment professionals must review the terms to establish exactly how the payoff is calculated, what is payable once the barrier event occurs, and what happens if it does not — because the payoffs differ depending on the price path. Features and payoffs of the options inside structured products are frequently negotiable and customisable.

A barrier is a price trigger level which, when reached, produces an event: a payment of interest, a higher interest rate, a currency conversion, a sale or purchase of stock. Depending on the underlying, the trigger may reference a stock price, an index level, an FX rate, a money-market or bond interest rate, or a weighted average of a basket.

Barrier options are path dependent, because they are activated or cancelled when a set trigger is reached. Intuitively they are cheaper than their vanilla counterparts, because the investor risks being knocked out, or never knocked in.

6.3 Knock-Out Barrier Options

A knock-out option ceases to exist when the barrier is touched. Two barrier configurations matter.

6.3.1 Single Barrier Knock-Out

Investor Position Consequence
A Call with strike 8.00 * *, knock − out * *9.00, spot $7.00 Maximum payoff 8.99 − 8.00 − price, breakeven $0.99
B Put with strike 13.50 * *, knock − out * *12.00, spot $15.00 Maximum payoff 13.50 − 12.01 − price, breakeven $1.49

The economic decomposition is a visible vanilla call plus an embedded sold call at the barrier. That is why the option is cheaper: the investor has sold the issuer the payoff beyond the barrier.

Contrast this with a bull or bear spread. In a spread the distant leg is a separately priced short option. In a knock-out, breaching the barrier extinguishes the whole option, so the investor loses the full intrinsic value accumulated to that point, not merely the capped excess.

6.3.2 Double Barrier Option

A double knock-out call carries two barriers. With strike 8.00 * *, spot * *7.80, lower knock-out 7.00 * *andupperknock − out * *9.00, there are three outcomes:

  • the underlying stays inside 7.00–9.00 and the call pays at expiry, with a maximum of 8.99 − 8.00 = $0.99;
  • the underlying touches $7.00 and the option is extinguished;
  • the underlying touches $9.00 and the option is extinguished.

Because it can be knocked out at both ends, a double barrier option is cheaper than a straight call and put combined with the same specifications. Note that launching the option in the money — spot at or above the strike — is the more popular configuration.

6.4 Knock-In Barrier Options

For knock-ins the reverse applies: the option becomes activated if the price touches the knock-in barrier. The terms become active from the moment of the touch through to expiry, even if the price falls back. A rebate is usually paid to the buyer at expiry if the knock-in barrier is never reached — compensation for the option never coming to life.

6.5 Summary and Application of Barrier Options

Unlike regular European calls and puts, barrier options are path dependent.

  • Knock-in. The option only comes into existence when the condition is met, typically when the barrier is touched at any time. Once breached, the underlying call or put becomes live and then behaves as an ordinary European option.
  • Knock-out. The underlying European option is extinguished when the barrier is touched. Once breached, the entire structure expires worthless.
Type of barrier option When the barrier is exceeded
Knock-out Deactivates the option; the underlying option is extinguished
Knock-in Activates the option; the underlying option becomes live

6.5.1 Advantages of Barrier Options

  • a. Cheaper premium than standard options, because the knock-out may terminate early, or the knock-in may never activate. A double knock-in has a higher knock-in probability than a single knock-in.
  • b. Leverage on capital for a given view.
  • c. A wider selection of option products for diverse market views.
  • d. Knock-in barriers suit large expected moves; knock-out barriers suit small sideways moves.

6.5.2 Disadvantages of Barrier Options

  • a. High risk of losing the premium, either because a knock-out occurred or because no knock-in ever occurred.
  • b. They are OTC products. The investor pays upfront and does not trade on an exchange, so bears issuer counterparty risk.

Barrier features are used heavily by banks in FX structures. A typical structured product embeds a knock-out option:

Term Value
Current spot 112.00
Strike price 112.00
Knock-out barrier 119.00
Minimum yield 0%
Knock-out rebate 2%

Maturity 3 months

The client has money-market deposits, and the interest is invested in a knock-out barrier option with a 2% rebate to make the downside more attractive. The option is active at inception. As long as spot trades below 119, the client participates in the movement from 112 to 118.99; at or above 119 the structure knocks out and the rebate applies.

6.6 Barrier Features in Structured Products

6.6.1 Barrier Capital Preservation Certificates

Often called a shark's fin, the structure combines a zero-coupon principal component with an up-and-out call. It suits an investor expecting a rise that will not breach the barrier.

Six characteristics define it:

  • a. Capital preservation is defined as a percentage of nominal, for example 100%.
  • b. Capital preservation refers to the nominal only at maturity, and not to the purchase price.
  • c. The product's value may fall below its capital-preservation level during its life.
  • d. Participation in positive underlying performance continues until knock-out.
  • e. A rebate may be paid following knock-out.
  • f. Upside is capped.

6.6.2 Barrier Reverse Convertibles

The barrier reverse convertible enhances yield when the underlying is expected to be flat or to move slightly. It offers conditional capital preservation through a kick-in barrier set a specific percentage below the share price at issue. It is commonly called a reverse convertible note and comprises a note, money-market instrument or bond, plus a put option sold by the investor to the issuer.

Investment view:

  • a. neutral on the underlying's direction;
  • b. expects flat to very slightly moving prices with falling volatility;
  • c. expects the underlying not to breach the barrier during the life; and
  • d. is looking for income or enhanced yield.

Characteristics. By selling the knock-in put the investor takes downside risk: if the underlying finishes below the strike at expiry and the kick-in level was breached, the investor loses capital. Principal is at risk once the barrier is touched.

The payout, with the strike and finish expressed as levels, is:

$$ \text{Payout} = \min\left\{100\%,\ 100\% - \frac{1}{\text{Strike}} \times \max\left[0,\ \text{Strike} - \frac{S_T}{S_0}\right]\right\} $$

The coupon is paid regardless; what changes is whether principal is returned in full or converted into a devalued underlying.

6.6.3 Bonus Certificates

A bonus certificate sets a barrier below spot and a bonus level above it. If the barrier is not breached, the investor receives at least the bonus; if it is, protection and bonus disappear.

Investment view:

  • a. neutral or generally bullish that the underlying will not trade below the barrier.

Characteristics:

  • i. Exposure similar to direct investment, with a minimum return (the bonus) if performance is flat or slightly negative — available so long as the underlying stays above the barrier.
  • ii. If the underlying is below the barrier, the investor has 1:1 downside exposure.
  • iii. The barrier is usually observed continuously throughout the term.
  • iv. Investors forgo dividends, which partly fund the issuer's purchase of the put.
  • v. If caps are introduced to limit upside, the certificate prices cheaper or the bonus can be increased.

Risks: a loss equal to the negative performance of the underlying; surrendered dividends; and issuer credit risk.

The issuer has four levers: the level of participation and any cap; the strike or bonus level; the knock-out barrier level; and maturity. The trade-offs are real. Lowering participation and capping upside allows a higher bonus or a further barrier. Lowering the bonus towards the initial price may allow a closer barrier or shorter maturity. Lengthening maturity raises investor risk, so a higher bonus may be required to sell it.

6.6.4 Barrier Capital Preservation Certificates (Straddle)

This product contains two knock-out options — a barrier call and a barrier put. The investor receives a capped return at maturity if no knock-out event occurs. There are two possible knock-out events, one for each option; if either occurs, the investor receives the agreed capital amount instead.

6.6.5 Positioning of the Barriers

Barrier placement sets the economics. A closer barrier is cheaper but easier to trigger; a wider barrier costs more but preserves more vanilla-like participation. The investor must be told which side of spot the barrier sits on, whether it is monitored continuously or on observation dates, and whether a rebate applies.

6.7 Binary Options

A binary or digital option pays a fixed amount if a condition is satisfied and zero, or another fixed amount, otherwise. One-touch pays if the barrier is touched; no-touch pays if it is never touched; double-no-touch or range structures pay if neither boundary is breached.

Binary payoffs are discontinuous and highly sensitive to barrier proximity, volatility, time and the monitoring convention.

A one-touch example: EUR/USD spot 1.08, strike 1.04, 3 months, maximum 10%, minimum 1%, money-market rate 2.00%, minimum USD 100,000.

A range currency deposit: range 1.06–1.10, 3 months, maximum 8.25%, minimum 2.00%, money-market rate 1.50%, minimum USD 100,000.

A range accrual paying on observations inside the range:

$$ \frac{60}{90} \times 8.25\% + \frac{30}{90} \times 2.00\% = 6.17\% $$

The trap is that 88 of 90 days inside the range with one day outside does not deliver close to 8.25% if the terms count day by day — read whether the condition is observed at expiry or throughout.

6.8 Basket Options

A basket option references several assets. Correlation drives the premium: imperfect correlation can make a basket cheaper than buying each single-asset option, but it creates correlation, composition, rebalancing, dividend and concentration risk. A worst-of basket pays on the weakest constituent and is especially correlation-sensitive.

6.9 Knock-Out Warrants

Knock-out warrants combine exchange-traded warrant leverage with a barrier that can terminate the product before its stated expiry. A breach may leave the warrant worth zero or a residual amount under the terms.

Six characteristics matter: exchange trading; a barrier that terminates early; delta close to one before termination; a flattening skew in pricing; issuer and DMM dependence; and settlement on the terms rather than on the expiry date. Compare the trigger, settlement, issuer, DMM, ratio and monitoring rules against a standard warrant.

6.10 Callable Bull/Bear Contracts

CBBCs are exchange-traded leveraged long or short products, usually linked to an equity or index. A bull profits from a rise; a bear from a fall. Delta is often close to 1 before the call event.

6.10.1 Price Movement

A bull CBBC moves with the underlying, up or down, until the call event. Because delta is near 1 and the conversion ratio is fixed, the percentage move in the CBBC is a multiple of the percentage move in the underlying.

6.10.2 Mandatory Call Event

The mandatory call event (MCE) is triggered when the underlying reaches the call price:

  • for a bear, spot rises to or above the call price;
  • for a bull, spot falls to or below the call price.

On an MCE the product terminates immediately. It is not a stop-loss the investor controls.

6.10.3 Gearing

$$ \text{gearing} = \frac{\text{underlying price}}{\text{CBBC price} \times \text{conversion ratio}} $$

A conversion ratio of 10:1 means 10 CBBCs per share.

6.10.4 Hedging

The issuer hedges the CBBC by trading the underlying. Because delta is close to 1, the hedge ratio is close to the conversion ratio, and the issuer's hedge must be unwound on an MCE.

6.10.5 Category N and Category R CBBCs

Category Call price relative to strike Residual value after MCE
Category N Call price equals the strike No residual value — the product is terminated
Category R Call price is above the strike for a bull (in the money) Residual value may be paid, but may be zero

Category R is not a guarantee of a positive residual. The call price, strike and conversion ratio are all specified in the term sheet.

6.10.6 Theoretical and Market Pricing

$$ \text{bull CBBC} = \frac{(\text{underlying} - \text{strike}) + \text{financial cost}}{\text{conversion ratio}} $$

$$ \text{bear CBBC} = \frac{(\text{strike} - \text{underlying}) + \text{financial cost}}{\text{conversion ratio}} $$

The financial cost comprises funding cost, the dividend adjustment and the issuer's margin.

6.10.7 Residual Value on MCE

For a Category R CBBC the residual value is direction-specific:

$$ \text{bull residual} = \frac{\text{settlement price} - \text{strike}}{\text{conversion ratio}} \qquad \text{bear residual} = \frac{\text{strike} - \text{settlement price}}{\text{conversion ratio}} $$

The settlement price follows the MCE observation rules: a bull uses the minimum and a bear the maximum traded price between the MCE and the next session, subject to the product terms.

6.10.8 Valuation at Maturity

At maturity the same direction-specific formula applies, using the closing settlement price rather than the post-MCE observation.

6.10.9 Capital Adjustments

Corporate actions adjust the strike, call price and conversion ratio to keep the product economically comparable. Regular dividends do not require adjustment, because they are already reflected in the financial cost.

6.10.10 Other SGX Issues

The issuer publishes a listing document stating the terms, including the call price and the observation method. Read it rather than inferring from the ticker.

6.11 Risks of CBBCs

  • MCE and early termination — the product can end before the investor's view plays out.
  • Residual-value uncertainty — a Category R residual may be zero.
  • Issuer credit risk.
  • Liquidity and DMM spread — quote obligations can stand down.
  • Gap risk — the underlying can jump through the call price.
  • Leverage — magnifies both directions.
  • Financing cost — accrues against the position.
  • Underlying suspension — trading halts suspend the product.
  • No margin misconception — there is no margin account, but the capital at risk is the whole purchase price.

Chapter 6 Appendix — Average Price (Rate) Options (non-examinable)

An average price or Asian option settles against the arithmetic mean of the underlying over a stated sampling period rather than a single point:

Payoffcall = max {Savg − X, 0}   Payoffput = max {X − Savg, 0}

Averaging reduces the option's sensitivity to a spike near expiry, so an average price option is cheaper than the equivalent vanilla. The longer the averaging window, the larger the discount. Sampling may be daily, monthly or quarterly.

Structured products use them to neutralise tail-end spikes: an underlying that gaps on the observation date cannot move the settlement as much when that date is one of many in the average. The trade-off is that the investor also gives up the benefit of a favourable spike.

Exam cues

  • Path dependence is the defining feature. A barrier does not change the final level; it changes whether the option is alive at that level. Two assets finishing at the same price can produce different outcomes.
  • Knock-out kills, knock-in births. Knock-out extinguishes the option and can lose the full intrinsic value already accumulated — a sharper loss than a spread's capped excess. Knock-in activates the terms from the touch through expiry even if price falls back, and pays a rebate if never activated.
  • Double barriers are cheaper than a straight call plus put, because they can be knocked out at both ends. A double knock-in has a higher probability of activation than a single.
  • Barrier capital preservation is nominal at maturity only — not the purchase price — and the product's market value may fall below that level during its life.
  • BRC payout: min(100%, 100% − (1/Strike) × max[0, Strike − S_T/S_0]). The coupon is paid regardless; only principal is at risk once the kick-in is breached.
  • Bonus certificate: bonus applies only while above the barrier; below it, 1:1 downside; dividends are forgone.
  • CBBC: call price is where the MCE fires — bull falls to it, bear rises to it. Category N has no residual; Category R may have residual but may be zero.

  • Trap: an investor can lose 83% on a CBBC when the underlying moves only 12.5%, and there is no margin call to warn them — the whole purchase price is at risk from the outset.

Chapter 7 — Structured Deposits and Other Structured Products

A structured deposit is a bank deposit whose return is produced by an embedded option. The principal may be preserved, but the yield is not, and the conversion obligation is real. This chapter teaches the decomposition and the suitability test.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Explain why structured products are treated as complex investments.
  • Apply the seven suitability criteria to a structured product.
  • Distinguish capital-preserving from yield-enhancing deposits.
  • Decompose a structured deposit into deposit plus option legs.
  • Compute a participation rate, a range-accrual payout and an accumulator's obligation.
  • Identify the worst case, not only the headline yield.

7.1 What are Structured Products?

Structured products combine a deposit, note, fund, warrant, ILP, ETF or ETN with one or more derivatives. They can customise a market view, enhance yield, preserve capital at a stated maturity, provide participation, a discount or leverage, hedge an exposure, change currency, or rebalance without directly buying the underlying.

These are complex investments. Their risks and payoff structures are less easily understood and they are usually offered to more experienced investors. Losses from OTC derivatives and structured products have historically been the subject of litigation by retail and high-net-worth individuals against banks, with clients claiming they did not understand, were not informed, or were misinformed about the risks. Verification of a client's risk profile and investment understanding, supported by documentation and acknowledgement, has therefore become standard practice.

From the institution's side, the growth of derivatives over 30 years helped offset narrowing trading commissions on traditional asset classes. OTC structured products raised transaction volume and generated higher returns than trading traditional investments would have.

7.1.4 Participation Rate

A participation rate states how much of the underlying's upside the investor receives, once the cost of the principal component and the option has been accounted for.

$$ \text{participation rate} = \frac{\text{discount sum}}{\text{call premium}} $$

Worked example. An S&P 500 call costs 24.00 * *.Twozero − couponbondsofface * *100.00, both with 5 years to maturity:

Parameter Bond A Bond B
Discount rate 5.00% 7.00%
Present value $78.35 $71.30
Discount sum (face − PV) $21.65 $28.70
Call premium $24.00 $24.00
Participation rate 21.65/24 = 90.21% 28.70/24 = 119.58%

The higher-yielding Bond B frees more cash to buy the call, so it buys more participation — but the investor has taken a weaker credit to get it.

7.1.5 Purpose and Uses of OTC Structured Products

For investors, OTC structured products offer:

  • a. Customisation. They meet needs standard investments cannot, and clients can negotiate criteria and trade-offs with the counterparty bank. They can also improve portfolio efficiency where a wide variety of instruments already exists.
  • b. Combining outcomes that cannot be bought separately. An investor wanting upside participation with some principal preservation cannot achieve it by buying securities directly, because the principal is exposed. A bond plus a call option on the securities preserves capital while retaining the upside — which is exactly the structured product.
  • c. Optimising yield and return. In low-rate periods investors seek higher yield. Nothing is free: with the promise of higher return come risks and trade-offs.
  • d. Access to markets, underlyings or structures that are otherwise unavailable or uneconomic.
  • e. A hedge with a payoff profile tailored to a specific exposure.
  • f. Currency transformation, producing a return in a different currency.
  • g. A discount entry into an underlying the investor is willing to own.
  • h. Leverage where the investor accepts amplified loss.

7.2 Structured Deposits with Capital Preservation

A structured deposit is a bank deposit whose principal is repaid in full at maturity, with or without interest. It is governed by the Banking Act and, after the Deposit Insurance Act 2005 was replaced, by the Deposit Insurance and Policy Owners' Protection Schemes Act 2011. MAS has issued the Guidelines on Structured Deposits (FAA-G09). Accredited and institutional investors may be exempt from parts of the regime.

Structured deposits are not necessarily deposit-insured, and early withdrawal may cost principal or return. The bank's credit standing matters.

From a risk perspective, structured deposits are generally less risky than direct investment in the underlying, because the bank must repay the full principal at maturity, or on early redemption where that feature exists. They are riskier than ordinary deposits, because the return depends on another instrument's performance; in some scenarios the investor gets back only the principal with no return.

7.2.2 Deposit with Standard Call and Put

A deposit plus a sold option. USD/JPY spot 112.43, 3 months, strike 113.50, minimum yield 0.00%, maximum unlimited, money-market rate 1.75%, minimum USD 250,000.

If spot finishes at 115.00, the payoff on the sold call, in JPY terms, is:

(115.00 − 113.50) × 250, 000 = JPY 375, 000

At the prevailing rate that is about USD 3,260.87, or 1.3% a quarter — 5.2% annualised. The maximum is described as unlimited because the sold call has no cap; the deposit's principal is preserved but the currency gain is not capped.

7.2.3 Standard Option with Knock-Out

Adding a knock-out at 109.50 with a minimum yield of 0.50% reduces the cost of the option, so the headline yield can rise. The trade-off is that a touch of 109.50 extinguishes the enhancement and leaves the investor with the minimum.

A step-down variant shifts the whole payoff down by a stated amount, 2.00% in the source example, once the barrier is touched. The lock-out or no-touch component is priced off the distance to the strike and the volatility.

7.2.4 Bull Call (or Put) Spread

USD/JPY spot 112.00, 3 months, spread 114.00–118.00, minimum 1.00%, maximum 14.50%, money-market rate 1.75%, 100% participation. The implied option cost is about 0.75% p.a.

The spread's theoretical maximum would be 15%; the product offers 14.5%, and the 0.5% difference is the spread capture retained by the bank.

7.2.5 One-Touch Lock-in

EUR/USD spot 1.18, strike 1.12 (about 5% below), 3 months, maximum 12.00%, minimum 0.75%, money-market rate 1.75%, minimum $200,000.

As long as 1.12 is reached at any time during the three months, the 12% return is locked in. The client is sacrificing 1% against the money-market rate of 1.75% to buy the one-touch option. The notional capital is guaranteed, and the bank can provide the 12% because it holds or hedges the position as counterparty.

7.2.6 Range Currency Deposit (Double Barrier Binary Option)

A range currency deposit suits an investor who believes the pair will stay within a range. Inside the range the investor earns a better yield than the money market; a breach at either end lowers the yield.

Example. EUR/USD spot 1.09, upper barrier 1.10, lower barrier 1.06, 3 months, maximum 7.25%. The client earns 7.25% annualised as long as the pair stays within 1.06–1.10.

The four levers that raise the yield are: narrow the range; skew it so spot sits nearer one barrier; extend maturity; or rely on higher volatility. Each makes the range more likely to be breached.

7.2.7 Range Accrual Currency Deposits

Rather than all-or-nothing, a range accrual pays the high rate on the observations inside the range and the low rate outside:

$$ \text{payout} = P_1 \frac{n}{N} + P_2 \frac{N - n}{N} $$

Example. 88 of 90 days inside, with a 7.25% inside rate and a 2.00% outside rate:

$$ \frac{88}{90} \times 7.25\% + \frac{2}{90} \times 2.00\% = 7.13\% $$

Accrual may be weekly or monthly rather than daily, which changes the payoff materially.

7.3.1 Standard Dual Currency Investments

A DCI has existed in the local market for 20–25 years. It consists of:

  • a. a short position in a call on the reference currency, or a short position in a put on another currency; and
  • b. a long position in a money-market deposit or bond denominated in the reference currency.

The option premium received lets the DCI offer a higher rate than the money market.

  • a. If the exchange rate strengthens or stays flat and does not cross the strike at expiry, principal plus interest is repaid in the reference currency.
  • b. If the exchange rate weakens through the strike, principal plus interest is repaid in the alternate currency — a conversion into a weaker currency. The risk is being forced to convert and then watching that currency fall significantly.

Worked example. The client holds USD as reference currency and believes GBP/USD will be flat or the USD will strengthen. Invests in a standard GBP/USD DCI: spot 1.37, strike 1.34, return 10.00% p.a. against a money-market rate of 1.75%.

  • If GBP/USD > 1.3400, the client receives the initial investment plus 10.00% interest in USD.
  • If GBP/USD < 1.3400, the client receives the initial investment plus interest converted at 1.3400 into GBP, and may incur an FX loss.

The headline 10% is roughly 8.25 percentage points above the money market. That gap is the option premium — the price of the conversion obligation.

7.3.2 Knock-Out DCI (Disappearing)

Strike 1.1000, knock-out 1.1500, spot 1.1200, 3 months. The knock-out DCI pays 8.50% against 9.00% for the standard version, money-market rate 1.75%.

The knock-out disappears the conversion feature if the barrier is touched, which protects the investor — so the yield is lower than the standard DCI. The further the barrier from spot, the less likely the protection is triggered, and the closer the yield moves to the standard version.

7.3.3 Knock-In DCI (Appearing)

Spot 1.1150, EUR put / USD call strike 1.1000, knock-in 1.0900, 3 months, maximum 9.00%, money-market rate 1.50%.

The conversion feature only appears once 1.0900 is touched. Until then the investor has an ordinary deposit. Because the conversion risk is conditional, the structure can pay more than a standard DCI with the same strike — the investor is being paid for accepting a risk that may not materialise.

7.4 Structured Products

Every structure should be decomposed into a deposit or bond plus long or short option legs. Step-up structures alter the coupon over time, often with a callable feature. Bull and bear spreads cap the maximum payoff. A one-touch may lock in a rate or convert after a touch.

7.4.1 Accumulators

An accumulator buys the underlying on a regular schedule at a discounted price, and is typically long in-the-money calls plus short out-of-the-money puts. Below the strike the investor must keep buying as the price falls; above the strike but below the knock-out they buy at the discount; at or above the knock-out the note terminates.

Because the ITM calls cost more than the OTM puts, an up-and-out knock-out barrier is placed to reduce the cost of the calls — which caps the gain. For a leveraged accumulator more than one OTM put is sold, which lets the knock-out barrier be pushed further from the strike or the strike reduced further. Those barriers lower the cost but can work against the client.

The risks are high because the investor is obliged to accumulate at prices above the prevailing market as it falls. Step-up features multiply the quantity — commonly 2× to 5× — once the price falls below a level, so the client accumulates more of a falling asset. Some banks offer margin financing, which magnifies gains and losses and makes the product riskier for both client and bank. Unwinding or offsetting is costly, and when credit concerns arise a bank may lower its allowable lending value, forcing a client to cover or sell at a substantial loss.

There is no capital guarantee. Gains are capped by the knock-out while losses continue below the strike.

7.4.2 Decumulators

A decumulator is the reverse of an accumulator. The investor is a writer of call options to the bank, taking on the obligation to sell a certain amount of the underlying on a regular basis at the contracted price or exchange rate.

Investors buy decumulators expecting a slightly bearish market. There is usually a knock-out level that terminates the obligation to sell once the price reaches it. For currencies, a decumulator is almost the mirror image of an accumulator, since every transaction involves a currency pair, and so decumulators are less popular than accumulators.

7.4.3 Currency Notes and Baskets

Currency notes and basket structures are covered with the other structured notes in Chapter 8.

7.5 Summary

Structured deposits may improve yield or customise currency and market exposure, but they can return less than a plain deposit, convert currency at an unfavourable level, be hard to exit, and expose the investor to bank credit risk. The client must understand the worst case, not only the headline yield.

8.1 What is a Structured Note?

A structured note is a debt or debenture obligation whose return is linked to an underlying — equity, index, currency, commodity, rate, credit reference or basket. The investor owns a claim on the issuer or an SPV, not the underlying asset.

Under Singapore's offering rules a prospectus may be required unless an exemption applies — for example an offer to accredited or institutional investors, or a minimum investment of S$200,000. The accredited-investor thresholds are:

8.4.1 Range Accrual Notes

A RAN pays a target return if a reference index stays within an agreed range, and less or nothing if it does not. The principal is not affected.

The reference can be a stock index such as the DJIA, or an interest-rate benchmark such as LIBOR, SIBOR or the Singapore Swap Offer Rate (SOR). Where the reference is 3-month LIBOR, interest accrues at the higher rate only on days the rate trades inside the range; outside it, no interest accrues.

Interest is accrued and calculated daily for days inside the range. The payout therefore depends on observed movements of the index.

$$ \text{Payout}_{\text{inside}} = P_1 \times \frac{d}{N} \qquad \text{Payout}_{\text{outside}} = P_2 \times \frac{N - d}{N} \;\; (\text{or zero}) $$

where N is the total number of observations in the period, d the number of observations inside the range, P1 the payout inside and P2 the payout outside.

The observation period may be daily, weekly or monthly, though daily is usual, and it is more common for the payout outside the range to be zero.

SOR-linked worked example. SGD 100,000 invested for one year at 5% p.a. inside a 1%–1.5% SOR range and 0% outside, semi-annual, ACT/360, fixing on 22 June and 22 December 2013.

  • First half: 110 of 182 days inside →

$$ 5\% \times \frac{110}{182} = 3.02\% $$

  • Second half: 160 of 183 days inside →

$$ 5\% \times \frac{160}{183} = 4.37\% $$

The annualised return over the year is about 3.68%, not 5%. The gap is the whole point of the structure: the headline rate is conditional on the path.

8.4.2 Callable Daily RAN (VMRAN)

A callable daily range accrual note lets the issuer call the note after a lock-out period, on designated coupon dates. Calling creates reinvestment risk: the investor loses the attractive coupon and may only be able to reinvest at lower rates.

8.4.3 Inverse Floaters

The coupon moves inversely to a reference rate, so the note performs as rates fall:

coupon = max [X% − leverage × floating-rate index, minimum coupon]

A floored version stops the coupon falling below a stated minimum, at the cost of a lower maximum elsewhere. Leverage makes the rate sensitivity nonlinear: at leverage 2, a 1% rise in the index cuts the coupon by 2%.

8.4.4 Callable Step-Up Notes

Coupons step up on a schedule, but the issuer may call. A typical issue might be an American bank, BBB+, USD 20 million, traded 30 May 2018, minimum USD 25,000 against a USD 1,000 denomination, maturing 23 May 2024 over six years, with semi-annual coupons stepping 3.00%, 4.00%, 5.00% and 6.00%.

The investor bears issuer call and reinvestment risk, rates risk, and the risk that the step-up is never received because the note is called first.

8.5 Yield Enhancement without Capital Preservation

8.5.1 Equity Linked Notes

An ELN is generally a funded bond or deposit plus a short put (bull ELN) or short call (bear ELN), and may embed a strangle or a worst-of structure. A bull ELN pays a higher yield if the equity stays above the strike; below it, the investor may receive shares or cash at the strike and bear the downside, with principal falling to zero if the underlying collapses.

Six key terms: underlying and initial reference spot; strike and conversion ratio; knock-in level where applicable; cash or physical settlement; observation dates; and the fixing date, normally two business days from maturity. The examination focuses on bull ELNs.

8.5.2 Bull ELNs

8.5.3 Worst-of ELNs

A worst-of ELN pays on the weakest underlying. Two examples show the mechanics.

Worst-of ELN #1. Three stocks A, B and C with initial spots 13.34, 12.43 and 3.12; current spots 13.26, 12.21 and 3.09; strikes 14.00, 13.05 and 3.28; knock-in levels 11.87, 10.92 and 2.76. Issue date 15 May 2018, valuations 15 June, 16 July and 14 August 2018, investment SGD 200,000, coupon 11.35% p.a.

The critical insight is that the worst performer need not be the knock-in toucher. Conversion occurs at the strike, giving:

$$ \frac{200{,}000}{13.26} = 15{,}082 \text{ shares} $$

plus a cash residual.

Worst-of ELN #2 (variable cumulative coupon). USD 200,000, two years, the call price is 100% of the trade-date close and the conversion price is 90%. Stock A is 30.00 / 27.00, Stock B 55.00 / 49.50. Calls are semi-annual, and the coupon steps 8%, 16%, 24%, 32% if the laggard is at or above its call price.

Scenario 1 at the first call date: Stock A at 29.50 (−1.67%) is the laggard and below its call price, so the note pays zero and continues. Scenario 2 at the second call date: Stock A 32.00 (+6.67%) and Stock B 56.50 (+2.73%); Stock B is the laggard but above its call price, so the note is called at 100% and the client receives USD 232,000 — the 16% return. Scenario 3 at the final date: Stock A 32.00, Stock B 65.00; Stock A is the laggard but above its call price, so principal plus the cumulative 32% coupon is paid — USD 264,000.

Scenario 4 is the loss case. The note is not called, and the laggard closes below its call price but above its conversion price. A variable coupon is calculated from the laggard's spot over its conversion price. Stock A at 29.16 against a conversion price of 27.00:

$$ \left(\frac{29.16}{27.00} - 1\right) \times 100 = 8.00\% $$

so the client receives USD 216,000, being principal plus an 8% coupon. Scenario 5 — Stock A at 26.00, below the conversion price — delivers shares instead:

$$ \frac{200{,}000}{27.00} = 7{,}407 \text{ shares} $$

worth USD 192,593 at 26.00, a loss of USD 7,407 or −3.70%, with no coupons received.

8.5.4 Risks of ELNs

The investor is short a put on the underlying, so bears the full downside of the weakest reference; receives no dividend; bears issuer and SPV credit risk; and faces liquidity risk because the note is bespoke. Worst-of structures add correlation risk — several names deteriorating together — and the coupon forgone while uncalled.

8.6 Credit Linked Notes

A CLN investor effectively sells credit protection through a CDS on a reference entity or basket, and receives an enhanced coupon in return.

On a defined credit event the payout may be reduced, securities delivered or principal lost. The events are:

  • bankruptcy;
  • failure to pay;
  • restructuring;
  • obligation acceleration;
  • obligation default;
  • repudiation or moratorium; and
  • where the definitions provide for it, government intervention.

The investor bears both the issuer or SPV credit risk and the reference-entity credit risk. A typical structure is five years at 4.00% p.a. on a reference entity, with the issuing bank rated single-A.

The 2014 Credit Derivatives Definitions reformed the event definitions and the settlement mechanics. A CLN can be cash settled — paying par less the recovery value — or physically settled by delivering a qualifying obligation.

8.6.1 First-to-Default CLN

A first-to-default basket pays out on the first reference entity to default. That amplifies both correlation and concentration risk: with highly correlated names, one systemic shock can trigger several at once, and the protection the investor has sold is triggered by the weakest link. The coupon is higher than for a single-name CLN precisely because the probability of some name defaulting is higher than the probability of any particular name defaulting.

8.6.2 Risks of CLNs

Two distinct credit risks: the issuer or collateral risk — the note is only as good as the issuer's ability to pay, and any collateral must actually be available and enforceable — and the reference-entity risk, which is the credit protection the investor has sold.

8.6.3 Bond-Linked Notes

A BLN embeds a short put on a bond. The investor receives an enhanced coupon and, if the bond's price falls below the strike, takes delivery of the bond. A BLN differs from a CLN in four ways: the trigger is a price fall rather than a credit event; the deliverable is the bond itself; there is no ISDA credit-event determination; and the credit exposure is to the bond issuer's market price rather than to a defined default.

8.6.4 Risks of BLNs

Issuer risk plus bond price risk, driven by rates, credit spread and liquidity. A BLN on a long-dated bond carries substantial duration risk — a rise in yields can move the bond price far more than the coupon the investor received.

8.7 Market Access

8.7.1 Exchange-Traded Notes

An ETN is unsecured listed debt tracking an index. The investor has no ordinary ownership, no guaranteed coupon, and central issuer credit risk — the instrument is a promise by the issuer, not a pool of assets. An ETN therefore differs from an ETF in four ways: no segregated assets; no creation and redemption against a portfolio; no custodial protection; and credit exposure to the issuer.

8.7.2 Index-Linked Notes

An index-linked note links participation, a minimum and possibly a cap to an index, normally subject to the issuer's claim.

Worked example. Issuer ABC Structured Products, rating A; STI at 3,278 on 18 May 2014; denomination SGD 1,000; investment SGD 1 million; maturity 15 May 2022, eight years. Redemption is the greater of:

  • 100% + 4%, or
  • 85% + average performance,

where the average is the arithmetic mean of the anniversary closes.

Scenario Average performance Redemption
1 +5% 85% + 5% = 90%, less than 104%, so the investor receives the 104% floor — SGD 1,040,000, or USD 42,500 of gain over eight years (4.25% in total, roughly 0.5% p.a.)
2 −5% 85% − 5% = 80%, below the floor, so the investor again receives 104% — SGD 1,040,000

The structure protects the downside at a cost: eight years of capital for a total 4% return. The investor has sold the issuer a long-dated call and received a floor in exchange.

8.7.3 Participatory Notes

A participatory note (P-note) gives exposure to a market that a foreign investor cannot access directly — Indian equities are the classic case — through a note issued by a registered intermediary rather than by acquiring the shares. The investor takes the intermediary's credit risk and has no shareholder rights.

8.7.4 Access Notes

An access note provides exposure to a market, asset or strategy through a note rather than direct ownership. The terms and the issuer's recourse control the outcome.

8.8 Investors and Suitability

8.8.1 Suitability for Retail

Assess liquidity and lock-up, risk and return, capital-protection conditions, the underlying scenario, term, fees and charges, currency, issuer or SPV, collateral, secondary market, early termination, tax and concentration. Explain that a high coupon is compensation for selling a put, a call or credit protection — not a free enhancement.

8.8.2 Sales-Process Factors

Five factors determine whether the sale is sound: liquidity (can the client exit, and at what cost); risk and return (including the worst case); terms (the payoff formula, the triggers and the settlement); fees and charges (including any embedded in the issue price); and documentation (prospectus, PHS and term sheet, with the acknowledgement retained).

8.9 Summary

Structured notes are debt plus derivatives. Ask whether principal is actually protected, when, in which currency and by whom. Callable, range, inverse-floater, ELN and CLN products shift market, rate, equity or credit risk into the investor's return; issuer or SPV credit and liquidity are as important as the reference asset.

Chapter 8 Appendix — Spread CDRANs, RAN Variations and PHS Content (non-examinable)

Spread CDRANs

A spread range accrual pays on the differential between two points on a curve — for example the 30-year against the 10-year. It pays while the curve is positive and fails on inversion. The investor is expressing a view on curve shape, not on the level of rates.

Variations of RANs

  • A daily range accrual equity-linked note observes an equity index rather than a rate. It typically carries unprotected principal, because the equity index can move far outside a range that a rate index would rarely breach.
  • A basket accrual requires all constituents to be inside their ranges, which materially lowers the probability of accrual and therefore raises the potential coupon.

Documentation

A prospectus and PHS are prepared for each issue, though the requirement is exempt for offers to institutional or accredited investors.

PHS Contents

A PHS should be at most a few pages, set in no smaller than 10-point type, and must cover seven mandatory sections: the product summary; suitability; key features; key risks — business, legal, market, credit and liquidity; a prominent warning of possible loss of all principal, in bold or italic; definitions; and contact information. The framework applies to offers of debt securities, hybrid instruments and equity securities.

Chapter 9 — Structured Funds

A structured fund is a collective investment scheme whose payoff is produced by assets, a formula, or a swap. Unlike a note, it publishes a NAV — and unlike a deposit, its capital is not a bank obligation. This chapter covers fund mechanics, CPPI, the prospectus, and ETFs.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Define a structured fund and distinguish it from a traditional fund, a tracker and a note.
  • Explain total return swap and zero-plus structures.
  • Compute the CPPI cushion, multiplier and risky allocation, and explain cash-lock.
  • Explain the roles of manager, trustee and custodian, and their reporting clocks.
  • Identify the contents of a fund prospectus and PHS.
  • Distinguish physical from synthetic ETF replication and their risks.
  • Explain tracking error, NAV premium and discount, and TER.

9.1 Structured Funds

A structured fund combines securities and/or derivatives to produce a specified risk-return profile, capital-preservation objective, participation formula, yield or cost structure. It is a collective investment scheme, governed by the applicable Code on Collective Investment Schemes, the offering documents, and licensing and custody requirements.

Capital preservation normally applies only at maturity and is subject to the fund, the issuer and the strategy performing. It is not deposit insurance and not a guaranteed NAV. A structured fund must publish its NAV, which distinguishes it from a note or a deposit.

Key governance points are a licensed manager, a fiduciary trustee or custodian, NAV calculation and reporting, valuation and pricing policies, disclosure of fees and risks, audit, and investor dealing terms. Conflicts arise where the manager, issuer, swap counterparty, custodian or distributor are affiliated.

UCITS funds are marketed globally and have succeeded in Asia and Latin America. In July 2012 the European Commission published the draft UCITS VI directive.

9.1.2 Compared with Traditional Funds

Traditional active funds select assets to outperform a benchmark, using manager judgement on securities, sectors, timing and allocation. A structured fund instead fixes the payoff mechanism in advance — a formula, a floor or a participation rate — so the outcome is determined by the structure rather than by discretionary selection.

9.1.3 Compared with Trackers

Trackers are passively managed index funds tracking a chosen benchmark. A structured fund may use an index as its underlying, but its payoff is transformed by the formula or the derivative; it does not simply replicate the index. Index investing is a form of passive investing; a structured fund is neither wholly passive nor discretionary.

9.1.4 Components

A structured fund has a principal component and a return component:

  • the principal component may be a bond, a zero-coupon bond, a deposit or a money-market instrument;
  • the return component may be a call, a put, a range, a digital or a swap.

A typical structure might place 80% of assets in a principal component designed to reach 100% at maturity, and 20% in an S&P 500 call.

9.1.5 Structured Funds Using Derivatives

Total return swap funds. A TRS fund receives the total return of a reference asset or index and pays financing to a swap counterparty. The financing leg might be SIBOR + 30bp, reset over six months, for the five-year life. It provides efficient exposure but adds counterparty, collateral, reset, liquidity and termination risk — the fund's return depends on the swap counterparty performing.

Zero-plus structures. A zero-plus fund allocates to a zero-coupon component that is projected to reach 100 at maturity, and to a call option for the upside.

Worked example. Ten-year fund. 80% is placed in a zero-coupon bond worth 80% today and projected to reach 100% in ten years — this is a projection, not a guarantee. 20% buys a ten-year STI call. If the call costs 25% of notional, the participation rate is:

$$ \frac{20}{25} = \mathbf{80\%} $$

The floor is protected only if the zero-coupon component performs and the issuer pays.

9.1.6 CPPI

Constant proportion portfolio insurance allocates dynamically between a risky and a risk-free asset. It typically offers a guarantee of 80–100% of principal; principal insurance bought from a bank costs roughly 1% p.a.

9.1.6.1 How CPPI Works

cushion = portfolio value − floor value

$$ \text{multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{crash size}} $$

risky allocation = multiplier × cushion

risk-free allocation = portfolio value − risky allocation

With a 15% floor and a 20% crash size, the multiplier is 5 and the risky allocation is 5 × 15% = 75%.

CPPI fund example. A USD 100,000 fund, five years, 100% guaranteed, guarantee fee 1%, management fee 1.5%, index allocation between 0% and 120%.

Path Year 1 Year 2 Year 3 Year 4 Year 5 Outcome
Good 100 110 105 120 125 135
Weak 100 95 90 98 100 95 → 100 guaranteed

On the weak path the fund falls to 90, the cushion approaches zero, the manager sells the risky assets and the fund finishes at the guaranteed 100 rather than at 95. That is the mechanism working — and also the reason the investor misses any recovery.

9.1.6.2 CPPI Risks

Three assumptions underpin CPPI: the risky asset appreciates, has low volatility, and has limited drawdown. Each can fail.

  • Buy high, sell low. The formula buys after rises and sells after falls, so a range-bound market erodes the fund.
  • Cash lock. A sharp fall forces the fund entirely into the risk-free asset, after which it misses any recovery.
  • Gap risk. A fall larger than the assumed crash size breaches the floor before the manager can de-risk.
  • High fees. Guarantee and management fees reduce the cushion from the outset.

9.1.7 DPPI

Dynamic proportion portfolio insurance varies the multiplier rather than holding it constant. A common configuration uses a multiplier band of 3–5×, reset to 4×. It responds to market conditions but shares CPPI's path, fee, gap, liquidity and manager risks.

9.1.8 Fund Structures

9.1.8.1 Capital-Preservation Funds

A capital-preservation fund guarantees a stated proportion of principal at maturity, typically by combining a zero-coupon bond with a call.

9.1.8.2 Formula Funds

A formula fund allocates according to a pre-determined formula based on an anticipated view, with the final payout depending on the realised market outcome. The advantage is that the investor knows from the start how the investment will be allocated. Such funds can be set up to track indices.

9.1.8.3 Capitalised and Distribution Funds

Capitalised funds — also called accumulating or reinvesting — automatically reinvest dividends into the fund. Distributing funds — also called income funds — pay dividends received to investors periodically.

9.1.8.4 Indirect Investment Policy Funds (Swap-Based)

These give a return linked to the performance of an underlying without investing directly or fully in it. The fund may invest part or all of the net proceeds in one or more derivatives under the Investment Restrictions in the prospectus. The investor's return depends on the underlying's performance and on the performance of the derivative used to link the proceeds.

The fund may instead hold a hedging asset and exchange all or part of its performance or income for a performance linked to the underlying, using derivatives within the prospectus limits. The underlying may follow a passive strategy — typically a financial index or a rules-based strategy — or an active strategy in which the real or notional basket is actively managed within the Investment Restrictions.

9.1.9 Examples of Structured Funds

Most structured funds use equities as the underlying, with others on commodities or hybrids. A common pattern is a fixed coupon for an initial period, then variable coupons or payouts linked to the underlying. A high fixed coupon forces the participation ratio lower, because the fixed payments consume the available premium.

Formula fund on a commodity index. The ABC Fund is an agriculture-related open-end USD fund tracking an agricultural index with diversified sector exposure, giving access to seven liquid commodities: corn, wheat, soybean, sugar, cotton, coffee and cocoa. Each subcomponent's weight is fixed and reset annually. The fund requires a low initial investment and offers daily liquidity at NAV.

The index uses an optimal yield rolling mechanism to generate alpha. Periodic rolling of expiring futures at a fixed term, the usual approach, can be detrimental to return and can even trigger losses. Optimal yield instead maximises roll return in backwardation and minimises roll loss in contango.

DEF Fund. An SGD fund of 25 stocks across infrastructure, utilities and real estate, paying a quarterly dividend, selling calls and buying puts as a stop-loss. It illustrates a covered-call overlay combined with a protective-put overlay inside a fund structure.

9.1.10 Risks of Structured Funds

Market, credit and counterparty (issuer, swap counterparty or custodian), liquidity, early termination, structure, correlation, currency and concentration risk all apply. A structured fund adds governance and valuation risk — NAV calculation, pricing policy and conflicts — that a direct portfolio does not carry in the same way.

9.1.11 Roles in a Structured Fund

9.1.11.1 Fund Manager

The manager implements the mandate, manages the portfolio and the day-to-day trading, and produces semi-annual and annual accounts and reports. It also handles unit creation and redemption.

9.1.11.2 Fund Trustee

The trustee holds the assets on trust and oversees the manager. The reporting clocks are strict: the semi-annual report within two months and the annual report within three months, with MAS notified within three business days of a breach.

9.1.11.3 Conflicts of Interest

Conflicts arise where the manager, trustee, custodian, swap counterparty or distributor are affiliated. They are managed through information barriers and separate reporting lines, and disclosed.

9.1.12 Managing Underlying Risk Exposure

Controls are operational, financial and legal. Risk is measured and limited through stress testing, VaR and duration, and the manager's job is to ensure risk does not exceed the level at which the fund can absorb the losses of a probable worst outcome.

9.1.13 Fund Documentation

9.1.13.1 Fund Prospectus

The prospectus must disclose, at minimum:

  • Basic information: registration and expiry date; place of constitution; the trust deed and any supplemental deeds; semi-annual reports; disclaimers; whether the scheme is constituted outside Singapore.
  • The manager: name, address and track record.
  • The representative, and the trustee.
  • Other parties: investment adviser, registrar and where the register may be inspected, and auditor.
  • Structure: standalone or umbrella; details for a fund of funds, feeder fund or sub-managed fund.
  • Investment objectives, focus and approach.
  • Facts of inclusion: fees and charges, and a fee table.
  • Risks: general and specific.
  • Subscription of units: procedure; minimum subscription; initial purchase price and offer period; dealing deadline and pricing basis; a numerical example under the pricing system adopted; confirmation of purchase; minimum fund size; return of contributions; and classes of units.
  • Regular Savings Plan: its details, and a statement that the investor may cease participation.
  • Realisation of units: procedure; minimum holding and minimum realisation amounts; dealing deadline and pricing basis; a numerical example.
  • Performance: past performance, comparison against a CIS, an index or other investments, the manager's record, future prospects, the benchmark, the expense ratio and the turnover ratio.
  • Soft dollars, conflicts, reports, specialised CIS warnings, and the complaints procedure.

9.1.13.2 Product Highlights Sheet

The PHS summarises the prospectus in a few pages: product summary, suitability, key features, key risks, fees and charges, definitions and contacts. It is a required companion, not a substitute.

9.1.13.3 Trust Deed

The trust deed establishes the fund, the trustee's powers and duties, and the investor's rights. It is the constitutional document, and the prospectus must be read with it.

9.1.14 Investor Types

A restricted scheme may be offered only to accredited or relevant persons, or at SGD 200,000 per transaction. A retail offer requires a lodged and registered prospectus. Institutional investors do not require an information memorandum.

9.1.15 After-Sales Information

9.1.15.1 Where to Get NAV

The administrative agent; newspapers; and Bloomberg, Reuters or the issuer's website via the ISIN.

9.1.15.2 Performance Information

  • Semi-annual and annual statements: the statement of net assets; the statement of changes in net assets; the statement of investments, listing the portfolio, with the same for each fund the company issues; and the changes in the number of shares for each share class, reconciling opening and closing units.
  • Investment manager report: performance of the underlying and of each share class, AUM, annualised volatility for different share classes, and a performance outlook.
  • Factsheet: launch date, manager information, key features, asset allocation, performance and applicable fees.
  • Monthly performance report: the principal terms — issuer, launch date, management company, initial NAV, management fee per annum; an overview of the investment policy and portfolio methodology; and performance — YTD, 1-month, 6-month, 1-year, 3-year and since-launch returns, with volatility, Sharpe ratio and VaR.

9.2 Structured Exchange Traded Funds

9.2.1 What are Exchange Traded Funds?

An ETF is an open-end investment fund that aims to replicate the performance of a published market index. It is listed and traded on a stock exchange, and investors buy or sell ETF units to participate in the index. Indices may be by country, region or sector, and may be emerging or developed.

Investors use ETFs to gain exposure to all constituents, diversifying without purchasing each asset. Many ETFs now do more than track stock prices: some track volatility, some track only daily price movement, and some are leveraged to 2–3× an index. For some ETFs, performance depends more on the options and futures market than on the index itself.

9.2.2 The Global ETF Market

The global ETF market reached roughly USD 7.7 trillion in AUM in 2020, with the United States accounting for about 70%.

9.2.3 Types of ETFs

Equity; fixed income; cash; currency; commodity; index; alternative; leveraged; inverse; and protective ETFs.

9.2.4 Replication Methodologies

Approach Method
Physical — full replication Hold all assets underlying the index in the exact proportion. Returns after expenses pass to investors by number of units held.
Physical — representative sampling Hold a subset of constituents, usually the more dominant, to reduce the number of securities while tracking as closely as possible. Used where some components are not liquid or easily available.
Synthetic — derivative embedded Use derivatives and/or OTC transactions to replicate the index without holding the assets. The fund relies on a third-party contractual obligation, so it takes credit exposure to the counterparty. This route provides access to markets such as China and India through P-notes.
Synthetic — swap based Exchange the fund's return for the index return through a swap, with collateral posted.

Synthetic structures are subject to a 10% net counterparty exposure limit, with collateral of at least 90% of NAV held at a third-party custodian owned by the trustee. Swap-based funds may be:

  • unfunded — the ETF buys the collateral itself;
  • funded — the counterparty deposits the collateral;
  • combined — a mix of both.

Collateral is rebalanced daily to keep it at or above 90% of NAV.

9.2.5 ETF Pricing and Valuation

  • Bid-ask spread is the immediate cost of trading.

  • Creation and redemption by authorised participants keeps the price near NAV; it can fail in stressed markets.

  • NAV premium or discount arises when the traded price diverges from NAV.

  • TER is expressed as a percentage or in basis points and accrues daily.

  • Tracking error is the divergence of fund return from index return. Six causes:

    • a. transaction costs;
    • b. portfolio holdings differing from the index;
    • c. the replication method;
    • d. futures roll, which costs in contango and earns in backwardation;
    • e. cash drag; and
    • f. hedging costs.

Tracking error is generally lower for synthetic than physical replication, and higher for emerging markets.

9.2.6 ETFs versus ETNs

An ETN is unsecured debt, with issuer credit risk and no segregated assets. Exchange-traded commodities (ETCs) are commodity-backed ETNs. A VIX ETN rolls daily futures and comes in different tenors, so it diverges from the index over time and can be shut down.

9.2.7 ETFs versus Mutual Funds

An ETF has two liquidity sources — primary creation and redemption, and secondary exchange trading. Mutual funds deal at NAV once a day. There is no difference between physical and swap-based ETFs in secondary trading; the difference is in what the fund holds and who bears the counterparty risk.

9.2.8 ETF and Fund Governance in the US and Europe

The first US ETF launched in 1993, tracking the S&P 500 physically. US funds are governed by the Investment Company Act of 1940, under SEC jurisdiction. In Europe, UCITS III from 2001 enabled European synthetic ETFs, subject to a 10% NAV per single counterparty limit, with some providers adopting voluntarily tighter limits.

9.2.9 Investor Types and Strategies

Investors range from retail to institutional. Common uses are core-satellite allocation, portfolio-imbalance management and cash-position management — using an ETF to gain immediate exposure while cash is being invested or during a transition.

9.2.10 Benefits of ETFs

  • Intraday liquidity and exchange trading.
  • Diversification and transparency of holdings.
  • Long, short, leveraged and inverse exposure.
  • Low cost and tax efficiency relative to many alternatives.
  • Core-satellite building blocks, and swap-based structures that minimise pre-fee tracking error.

9.2.11 Risks of ETFs

  • Market and liquidity risk, where liquidity is measured by the underlying, not by secondary volume.
  • Tracking error and NAV premium or discount.
  • Counterparty and collateral risk in synthetic structures.
  • Roll and cash-drag risk in commodity and futures-based funds.
  • FX and time-zone risk — an Asian-hours fund tracking a US index diverges from NAV outside overlapping hours.
  • Leverage and daily-reset risk, where the daily multiple is not a long-run multiple.
  • Closure, merger and securities-lending risk. VIX ETNs were shut down in 2018, with losses exceeding 70% even after the VIX recovered.

9.2.12 ETFs After-Sales

Monitor NAV, tracking difference, corporate actions, distributions, securities lending and factsheets. Remember that an ETF's liquidity is the underlying's liquidity: a narrow secondary market does not mean the fund is illiquid, and a deep secondary market does not mean the underlying is.

9.3 Summary

Structured funds are CIS vehicles whose payoff depends on assets, formulae and sometimes derivatives or swaps. CPPI preserves a floor only subject to path, fee, gap and issuer risk. ETFs trade intraday but carry NAV, tracking, market, liquidity, FX and counterparty exposures. Governance, custody, disclosure and suitability are part of the product.

Exam cues

  • A structured fund is a CIS, must publish NAV, and its capital preservation applies only at maturity and only if the issuer and strategy perform. It is not deposit insurance.
  • CPPI: cushion = portfolio − floor; multiplier = 1/crash size; risky = multiplier × cushion. A 15% floor with a 20% crash gives multiplier 5 and 75% risky.
  • CPPI fails by buying high and selling low, by cash-locking at the floor, and by gap risk. It assumes an appreciating, low-volatility asset with limited drawdown.
  • DPPI varies the multiplier, typically within a 3–5× band reset to 4×.
  • Zero-plus participation = available premium ÷ call cost. 20% available against a 25% call gives 80% participation.
  • Trustee clocks: semi-annual report within two months, annual within three, MAS notified within three business days of a breach.
  • Prospectus must include a fee table, a numerical pricing example, the RSP, realisation terms, performance with the expense and turnover ratios, soft dollars and conflicts.
  • Restricted scheme: accredited or relevant persons, or SGD 200,000 per transaction.
  • ETF replication: physical full, physical representative sampling, synthetic derivative-embedded, synthetic swap-based (unfunded, funded, combined). Synthetic limits: 10% net counterparty exposure, collateral at least 90% of NAV rebalanced daily.
  • Six causes of tracking error: transaction costs, holdings, replication method, futures roll, cash drag, hedging costs. Synthetic tracks tighter than physical; emerging markets track wider.
  • Trap: an ETF's liquidity is the underlying's liquidity, and a leveraged or volatility ETF's daily target is not a long-run multiple.

Chapter 10 — Contracts for Differences (CFDs)

A CFD is a leveraged, cash-settled agreement on the price difference of an underlying the investor never owns. This chapter covers the provider models, the margin mechanics, the cost arithmetic and the strategies traders run.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Define a CFD and distinguish it from ownership of the underlying.
  • Distinguish the market-maker, direct-market-access and exchange-traded provider models.
  • Explain initial margin, maintenance margin, margin calls and liquidation.
  • Compute commission, GST and financing costs and the resulting ROI on margin.
  • Explain stop-loss, stop-entry and guaranteed stops, and the gap risk they leave.
  • Explain dividend capture, pairs trading and merger arbitrage.

10.1 History and Origin

CFDs emerged in London in the early 1990s from the OTC equity-swap market, originally for institutional hedging. They reached retail markets in the late 1990s and were not available in the US retail market in the same way. That history explains their OTC, leveraged and cash-settled character.

10.2 What are CFDs?

A CFD is an agreement to exchange in cash the difference between the opening and closing value of an underlying position — or the reverse for a loss. The investor does not own the share or asset, normally has no voting rights, and does not take physical delivery.

A long equity CFD may receive a dividend adjustment; a short position may be debited. Corporate-action adjustments follow the provider's terms.

Five contract terms define a CFD: the underlying; the opening and closing price; the number of units; the direction; and the currency. Alongside those sit margin, financing rate, expiry or rollover, and corporate-action treatment.

10.3 Characteristics and Features

Leverage; flexibility; a relatively low initial outlay; a transparent reference price; simplicity of long and short exposure; no fixed expiry in some provider models; global access; cash settlement; and corporate-action adjustments.

Countervailing risks: margin calls, forced liquidation, financing, commission and GST, rollover, spread, provider credit and market liquidity.

Singapore charges no stamp duty on CFDs.

10.6 Mechanisms

10.6.1 Margin

Initial margin is a percentage of contract value. The investor controls a larger notional than the cash posted, so return on margin can be high while losses are based on the full exposure.

Example 1. At 10% margin, 10,000 shares at $2 give a contract value of 20, 000andaninitialmarginof * *2,000** — 10× leverage.

Example 2. Crude at $105 × 100 barrels is 10, 500; at * *202,100** — 5× leverage.

The Risk Disclosure Statement should explain margin, liquidation and the client's liability for any shortfall.

10.6.2 Mark-to-Market

Positions are revalued as the underlying moves, continuously during the trading day, and the account's equity is compared with the maintenance requirement.

10.6.3 Margin Call

A margin call requires additional funds. It is triggered when equity falls below the maintenance margin. The provider may then:

  • partially liquidate, selling enough positions to restore margin; or
  • fully liquidate, closing everything;

and the investor remains liable for the shortfall, loss and expenses after liquidation.

10.6.4 Liquidation

Liquidation converts the position to cash without the investor's consent. It is the mechanism that turns a temporary adverse move into a realised loss — and why a margin buffer matters more than a view.

10.6.5 Orders

All orders are day orders unless stated otherwise, and use the underlying's bid and ask.

  • Limit order. A limit buy at 10.00 * *whentheaskis * *10.02 will not execute at 10.02; it queues at 10.00 or better.
  • Market-to-limit (MTL). Executes at the market and leaves any unfilled balance at the prior execution price. A buy filling partially at $10.02 stays at 10.02; it is cancellable and valid only during market hours.
  • Market order. Matched to the lowest ask for a buy, or the highest bid for a sell.

10.6.5.2 Contingent Orders

  • Stop-loss. Long 1,000 CFDs at 10.00 * *withasellstopat * *9.50 and a limit at $9.45.
  • Stop entry. Market at 20.80 * *; place1, 000at * *21.00 with a stop sell at 20.50 * *andalimitat * *20.46. The limit must be within 10 ticks ($20.40) of the stop.
  • Guaranteed stop-loss. A distinct paid service. Where offered, the provider fills at the stop price regardless of slippage; where not, a fast market can gap straight through the stop.

10.6.6 Financing, Commission and GST

  • Financing. Long CFDs normally incur interest on the full notional; short CFDs may receive interest on deferred sale proceeds but can incur stock-borrow costs.
  • Commission. Commonly a percentage of notional, subject to a minimum.
  • GST applies to the commission.
  • Rollover depends on the provider and the asset.

10.8 CFD Trading Strategies

10.8.1 Dividend Capture

Buy before the ex-dividend date and close after, seeking to profit if the price does not fall by the full dividend. Costs, tax, timing and price behaviour can defeat the trade. Traders generally screen for shares with strong earnings and high payout potential.

10.8.2 Pairs Trading

Long an undervalued or stronger asset and short an overvalued or weaker related asset, aiming for mean reversion and market neutrality. Correlation can break and costs are doubled.

Industry-sector pairs compare companies in the same sector — CapitaLand against City Developments, for instance — leaving stock-specific and sector risk.

Statistical arbitrage uses historical correlation and quantitative signals; the relationship may not revert.

Merger arbitrage goes long the target and short the acquirer, or the reverse. Deal risk — a failed transaction — can hurt both legs.

Index pairs trade correlated indices such as the S&P 500 against the Nikkei 225 when their relationship diverges. Index liquidity is usually deeper than single-stock liquidity.

10.9 Summary

CFDs are leveraged cash-settled contracts, not ownership. Their flexibility conceals financing, margin, liquidation, counterparty, liquidity, currency, corporate-action and short-sale risk. Know the provider model, the order mechanics, the total cost and the worst-case liability.

Chapter 11 — Key Products and Investment Risks for Derivatives and Structured Products

This chapter is the risk register. It takes each product family — futures, options, warrants, CFDs — and names its specific risks, then layers the risks that structured products add, then sets out the controls a firm must operate.

Learning Objectives

The candidate should be able to:

  • Explain the specific risks inherent in futures.
  • Explain the specific risks inherent in options.
  • Explain the specific risks inherent in warrants and in CFDs.
  • Explain why structured products carry more complex, layered risks.
  • Explain the trading controls and risk-management processes a financial organisation implements.

11.1 Investment Risks for Derivatives

11.1.1 Risks in Trading Futures

11.1.1.1 Basis Risk

The basis is the difference between the futures price and the cash or spot price. Where the asset being hedged is identical to the asset underlying the contract, the basis is zero at maturity. Where the contract and the asset are liquidated before maturity, the hedger faces basis risk, because futures and cash prices do not move in perfect lockstep; gains and losses on the contract and on the asset need not exactly offset.

Basis risk arises because:

  • a. the asset to be hedged may not be exactly the same as the asset underlying the contract;
  • b. the hedger may be uncertain about the date of the purchase or sale;
  • c. the hedge may require the contract to be closed out well before expiry; and
  • d. the size and unit of measurement of the contract may not correspond to the hedged position, so it is under- or over-hedged.

11.1.1.2 Liquidity Risk

Although futures trade on exchanges, liquidity is not assured. Unless the investor is willing to take delivery, the only way to close a position is to offset it with another contract.

Liquidity can dry up near maturity, and can decline in a rapidly rising or falling market. If the price has moved by the maximum daily limit and no counterparties will transact, there are no trades — a "lock limit" market — and the investor cannot offset.

Two indicators gauge liquidity:

  • a. trading volume; and
  • b. open interest — the number of contracts not yet closed by an offsetting trade and not yet delivered.

11.1.1.3 Leverage Risk

Futures use margin financing, so a relatively small price move produces a significant profit or loss. The percentage loss on the futures position is greater than the percentage move in the underlying.

The investor posts initial margin. If the market moves against the position, or the broker raises margin levels, additional funds are needed at short notice. Failure to comply may lead to liquidation.

11.1.2 Risks in Trading Options

Holders can lose the entire premium, especially for out-of-the-money options near expiry. European options may be difficult to monetise before expiry, and OTC exercise provisions may be unusual.

Naked writers face unlimited loss on a short call and a loss down to zero on a short put, and American-style writers do not control exercise timing.

Both sides face volatility, complex-strategy, trading-halt, foreign-market timing and currency risk. OTC options add counterparty risk.

11.1.3 Equity Index Options

Index options may reference illiquid or niche indices, and OTC index options add counterparty risk. Settlement is usually cash, but the index's composition and the contract multiplier must be checked.

11.1.4 Bond Options

Bond options inherit the bond's interest-rate, credit-spread, liquidity, embedded-option and sovereign risks.

In a flight to safety, Treasury yields fall while credit spreads widen most. The "bond vigilantes" of the 2010 Eurozone crisis — Greece, Spain and Portugal — are the standard illustration: government yields rose as investors demanded a premium for credit risk, so bond prices fell.

Bond options also carry embedded call and put features and change-of-control or event risk.

11.1.5 Currency Options

Currency options involve two currencies, huge notionals and round-the-clock price movement, so monitoring is continuous. Physical delivery and OTC counterparty risk add to the exposure.

11.1.6 Warrants

Company warrants can expire worthless, carry no voting or dividend rights, and dilute shareholders if exercised. Structured warrants add issuer credit risk, underlying market risk, leverage, time decay, volatility, liquidity and currency exposure.

11.1.7 Risks in Trading CFDs

Leverage, market volatility, counterparty, liquidity, currency, financing, commission and GST, rollover, corporate actions and provider-model risk all apply. Foreign markets may have weaker protections and exchange controls. Market-maker pricing may be less transparent than DMA.

11.2 Key Risks of Structured Products

Structured products combine risks that a headline yield does not reveal.

11.2.1 Market Risk

Macro rates, inflation, employment, recession, earnings and political change all move the underlying and the payoff.

The crucial framing is that a short option sells protection:

  • selling a call sells protection against a rise;
  • selling a put sells protection against a fall;
  • a short receiver or payer swaption sells protection against a rate move.

Four payoff profiles illustrate it: the short call, the short put, the short receive-fixed (call) swaption, and the short commodity call. In each, the investor receives premium and takes the loss if the protection is called upon.

11.2.2 CPPI Risk

CPPI buys more risky assets as they rise and sells as they fall, and a sharp fall can lock the fund at its floor, after which it misses any recovery.

11.2.3 Credit and Counterparty Risks

The issuer, the SPV, a swap counterparty, the reference obligor or the collateral may fail. An SPV may be not guaranteed by its parent, so the claim is limited to the SPV's own assets and the documents' terms.

11.2.4 Types of Credit Default

The ISDA credit events are bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring, obligation acceleration, obligation default and repudiation/moratorium. The 2014 Credit Derivatives Definitions reformed these definitions and the settlement mechanics.

In a CLN the structure runs: the investor buys notes issued by an SPV; the SPV uses the proceeds to buy AAA-rated securities; the investor receives enhanced interest at LIBOR + x bp; the investor is selling credit protection to the SPV; and the SPV in turn sells protection to a bank through a CDS, receiving a protection payment that subsidises the enhanced interest.

If the reference entity defaults, the SPV — protection seller to the bank — liquidates the AAA securities and pays the bank. The investor, protection seller to the SPV, pays the contingent payment and recovers the remainder, par minus losses.

11.2.5 Liquidity Risks and Secondary Market Trading

Structured products are usually designed for investors willing to hold to maturity. Being customised to a specific risk-reward profile, they have a limited secondary market, so an early exit is hard.

A lock-up prevents withdrawal until a stated date, from a few months to a few years. A further reason for illiquidity is that the embedded derivatives — exotic options, CDS — may not be actively traded.

Liquidating before maturity may cost part or a substantial part of principal.

The issuer or its affiliates usually provide a secondary market even where not obliged. Investors should assess the issuer's quality, commitment and track record in providing liquidity, while recognising that the price ultimately depends on prevailing market conditions.

11.2.6 Early Termination Risk

Unwinding the bond and the derivative before maturity incurs discount and break costs. Full principal preservation may exist only at maturity.

11.2.7 Structure Risk

Combinations vary, and the loss may be unlimited or the principal fully exposed despite a familiar wrapper.

A receive-fixed swaption has its loss capped at the fixed rate; a pay-fixed swaption's loss is unlimited. Negative interest rates are a scenario the structure may not have contemplated.

11.2.8 Reinvestment Risk

The risk that coupons or principal must be reinvested below the yield to maturity. If rates fall, proceeds can only be reinvested at the prevailing lower rates, so the realised return is below the YTM.

It is greatest for bonds with large coupons, higher payment frequency, long maturities and those selling at a premium. Zero-coupon bonds have none; callable bonds have the most, because the issuer chooses when to return the money.

11.2.9 Volatility Risk

Applies to bonds and instruments with embedded options — callable, putable and convertible bonds. Their prices respond to volatility as well as to rates. A call option rises in value when rate volatility rises, which reduces the value of the callable bond because the holder has sold that call component to the issuer.

11.2.10 Correlation Risk

The likelihood that an event affecting one underlying directly affects another. A structured product is exposed whenever its underlyings are correlated — baskets, worst-of structures and credit pools, where banking or sector relationships make simultaneous deterioration likely.

11.2.11 FX Risk

The underlying and the note may be in different currencies, so the investor bears the currency move between them as well as the underlying's performance.

11.2.12 Legal Risk

The investor owns a note or a claim, not necessarily the underlying assets. Creditor priority and recourse depend on the documents, and an SPV's position in a group structure matters.

11.2.13 Transactional Risk

Pricing and contract commencement, and settlement timing across currencies, can create exposure between the trade and the settlement.

11.2.14 Mis-selling Risk

The product may not meet the client's objective. The PHS, prospectus or private-placement documents, and the advice given, must explain the real payoff — not the headline.

11.3 Trading Risk Control and Management

The front office, middle office and back office manage market, credit and settlement risk.

11.3.1 Futures Controls

Fixed-income measures support these:

  • 1 basis point = 0.01%.
  • DV01 / PVBP is the dollar price change per basis point.
  • Duration is a weighted cash-flow time measure; modified duration measures price sensitivity:

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -\text{modified duration} \times \Delta YTM $$

A modified duration of 2 and a 100bp rise implies about a 2% fall. A bond priced at 980 * *withPVBPof * *1 falls to $975 on a 5bp rise.

A 1-day 95% VaR of SGD 1m means a 5% probability that the loss exceeds SGD 1m over one day, under the model's assumptions — about one day in twenty. VaR has three components: confidence, horizon and the loss estimate. It is neither a guarantee nor a substitute for stress testing.

11.3.3 Credit and Settlement Controls

OTC counterparty limits depend on credit rating, settlement amount and tenor. A Credit Support Annex (CSA) specifies collateral.

Clearing-broker risk arises because margin is held with the broker; exchange and CCP risk remains despite default resources.

The exchange itself is a risk to be assessed, not assumed away.

11.3.4 Settlement Risk

Back-office controls require timely, accurate confirmations stating the option style; the expiry date and time; the premium payment date; the settlement date; the strike; and both currency amounts.

11.4 Summary

Risk management must match the product: basis, liquidity and leverage for futures; premium, exercise, volatility and Greek risk for options; issuer and gearing for warrants; margin, financing and provider risk for CFDs; and layered market, credit, liquidity, legal, structure, correlation, FX and reinvestment risk for structured products.

Exam cues

  • Futures basis risk has four causes — a different asset, an uncertain date, an early close-out, and a size mismatch causing under- or over-hedging. Basis is zero at maturity only if the assets are identical.
  • "Lock limit" means the price has hit its daily limit and no counterparty will trade, so the position cannot be offset. Volume and open interest are the liquidity indicators.
  • Option losses: a holder's maximum is the premium; a naked short call is unlimited; a short put loses down to zero.
  • Structured products sell protection: a short call sells upside protection, a short put sells downside protection, a short swaption sells rate protection.
  • Reinvestment risk ranking: zero-coupon has none, then low coupons, then high coupons, then callable bonds have the most. Large coupons, frequent payments, long maturities and premium prices all increase it.
  • Volatility and callables: rising rate volatility raises the call's value and therefore lowers the callable bond's value.
  • Duration and PVBP: ΔP/P ≈ −D × ΔYTM; D = 2 with a 100bp rise is −2%; $980 with PVBP 1anda5bpriseis * *975**.
  • VaR: 1-day 95% VaR of SGD 1m is a 5% chance of losing more than SGD 1m in a day, roughly one day in twenty — not a maximum loss.
  • Controls: open-contract, maximum-loss, maturity and stress-test limits; Greek limits for delta, gamma, vega, theta and rho; CSA collateral; and a confirmation checklist covering style, expiry date and time, premium date, settlement date, strike and both currency amounts.
  • Trap: receive-fixed swaption loss is capped at the fixed rate, but pay-fixed loss is unlimited. And a 1-day VaR says nothing about what happens beyond the horizon or outside the model.

12.5 Range accrual note — Robert

AUD/USD range = 0.7600–0.8200, maturity three months, maximum yield 6%, minimum 1%, MMR 2.75%. If inside for two months and outside for one, annualised payout is $$6\% \times \tfrac{2}{3} + 1\% \times \tfrac{1}{3} = 4.33\%$$ (the answer text displays approximately 4.32% due to rounding).

To make the digital spread cheaper or raise maximum yield: narrow the range, skew it so spot is nearer a barrier, extend maturity, or lower the minimum yield. Widening the range makes inside outcomes more likely and the option more expensive; shortening maturity can increase cost. A double-no-touch version observes the path: touching either barrier terminates the enhanced payout and leaves only the minimum return.

12.6 Equity accumulator — Harry

ABC spot SGD1.10; strike SGD1.00; knock-out SGD1.30; one year/250 business days; 10,000 shares per observation; monthly settlement. An accumulator combines long call and short put economics and can be funded or margin/unfunded. Below strike under a 1×2 arrangement, the client may have to buy 2×; above strike but below the knock-out, 1×; at/above the barrier, the note terminates.

  • If the barrier is touched on day 13, days 1–12 accumulate: 120,000 shares.
  • Twenty days in month 1 plus 12 days in month 2 before day-13 knock-out = 320,000 shares; cost = SGD320,000.
  • If marked at SGD1.30, value = SGD416,000 and gain = SGD96,000.

Worst case: price zero for 250 days, 2.5m shares purchased at SGD1 = SGD2.5m loss. There is no capital preservation; the bank may margin-call/liquidate; gains are capped by the knock-out while losses continue below strike; early-break costs can be large.

12.7 Gold, silver and USD volatility — Brandon

Rising US rates may strengthen USD and weaken gold/silver, while political/equity uncertainty may support safe-haven gold. Gold’s widening premium to silver and different USD sensitivities create relative-value ideas. If the direction is uncertain but a large move is expected, buy a gold straddle: long call and long put at the same strike. A strangle uses different OTM strikes and is cheaper but needs a larger move. Combine a bull call spread with a bear put spread, or a bull call with a bear call, to express two-sided volatility; do not use two bearish or two bullish structures if the view is genuinely two-sided.

12.8 Yield-curve movement — Tracy

Tracy expects the US yield curve to flatten/invert: short rates rise faster than long rates. A constant maturity swap (CMS) resets a floating leg against a long-term constant-maturity rate; unlike a vanilla IRS, the reference maturity/duration remains constant. It can express steepening/flattening views. To benefit from short rates rising faster, swap the long end for the short end — pay the long-end rate and receive the short-end rate — subject to the exact swap convention and market quotation.

Appendix A — CPPI strategy worked examples

Parameters: initial $100; crash size 20%; multiplier 1/20% = 5; risky asset S&P index fund; five-year tenor.

  • Day 1 floor 85%: cushion 15; risky = 5×15 = $75; risk-free = $25.
  • New floor 86% with total still $100: cushion 14; risky = $70; risk-free = $30.
  • Portfolio at $86 after risky asset falls 20%: risky falls $70→$56; plus $30 risk-free = $86; liquidate all risky into risk-free, so later equity appreciation is missed.
  • Portfolio at $96: risky value $66, required risky allocation 5 × (96 − 86)% = $50; sell $16 risky and hold $46 risk-free.
  • Portfolio at $110: required allocation 5 × (110 − 86)% = $120; if capped, put all $110 in risky; if leverage permitted, borrow $10 and hold $120 risky.
  • If leveraged risky value falls 20% from $120 to $96, repay $10 and move remaining $86 to risk-free. The crash has again locked the floor.

The reallocation repeats periodically. CPPI assumes an appreciating, relatively low-volatility asset with limited drawdown; a range-bound or gap-down market can cause buy-high/sell-low behaviour.

What these questions test

The set is a format and scope orientation, not a syllabus. Read against the chapters, it reveals where the examination puts its weight.

Calculation discipline. Six questions require a number, and each uses a single formula from Appendix D: interest rate parity (Q4), the contract-count hedge (Q8, Q9), intrinsic value (Q12, Q20), conversion price (Q18), gearing (Q19), warrant premium with a non-unit conversion ratio (Q21) and participation rate (Q31). Q21 is the trap — a 2:1 conversion ratio doubles the effective conversion cost before the premium is computed.

Sign and direction conventions. Q11 excludes the dividend rate because a higher dividend reduces call value; Q22 requires the barrier of an up-and-out call to sit above spot; Q16 identifies volatility as the one factor that raises both call and put value.

Product boundaries. Q1, Q25, Q42 and Q45 turn on distinguishing products that look alike: futures versus forwards (counterparty risk and interim settlement), CBBC versus structured warrants (the call price), CFDs versus equity futures (OTC versus exchange, implicit versus explicit financing, dividend entitlement), and the set of instruments that let an investor profit from a falling price.

Structural facts worth memorising. Q8 and Q9 both use the same hedge formula — $$\frac{\text{notional}}{\text{contract size}} \times \frac{\text{exposure tenor}}{\text{contract tenor}}$$ — applied to a strip and a stack respectively. Q26 separates the disadvantages of barrier options from their advantages: the premium-loss risk on breach and issuer counterparty risk on OTC products.
