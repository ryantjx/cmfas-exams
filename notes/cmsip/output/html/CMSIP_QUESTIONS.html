Chapter 1 — Overview of Derivatives

1.1 Introduction and 1.2 Underlying asset classes

1. [Single choice] Which statement best defines a derivative?

a. A security that always gives ownership of a company. b. A contract whose value is linked to an underlying asset, rate, index, event or outcome. c. A deposit whose principal is always insured. d. A bond with no maturity.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. A derivative derives value from an underlying; ownership does not automatically pass to the investor.

2. [Multiple response] Which are common uses of derivatives?

a. Hedging an existing exposure. b. Speculating on direction or volatility. c. Arbitrage between mispriced markets. d. Repackaging cash flows or creating a synthetic position.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. All four are core uses.

3. [Single choice] A Singapore importer payable in USD in three months is most directly exposed to:

a. USD/SGD currency risk. b. Equity dilution risk only. c. The risk that a call option will always be exercised. d. No risk until payment day.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. A currency forward, future or option may hedge the USD payable.

4. [Multiple response] Which asset classes are discussed as derivative underlyings?

a. FX. b. Money-market/short-term rates. c. Fixed income and credit. d. Equities and commodities.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. The guide also extends these to indices, baskets, funds and other references.

5. [Single choice] Why can an NDF be useful for a restricted currency?

a. It guarantees physical delivery of the restricted currency. b. It is normally cash-settled without exchanging the notional currency. c. It eliminates all FX risk. d. It is always exchange traded.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. An NDF settles the difference between the contracted rate and the fixing/spot rate.

6. [Single choice] Which statement about a CDS is correct?

a. It always transfers legal ownership of a bond. b. It transfers or references credit protection on a debt obligation or index. c. It is a form of equity dividend. d. It is a physical commodity storage contract.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. The protection buyer pays a spread and receives a contingent payment after a defined credit event.

7. [Multiple response] Commodity-derivative pricing can be affected by:

a. Storage and insurance cost. b. Transport and delivery location. c. Seasonality, weather and supply/demand. d. The USD and interest rates.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. These factors influence carry, convenience yield and spot/futures relationships.

1.3 Exchange-traded derivatives and 1.4 OTC derivatives

8. [Multiple response] Typical exchange-traded derivative features include:

a. Standardised contract terms. b. Central clearing. c. Daily mark-to-market/margining. d. Bespoke bilateral terms for every customer.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b and c. Bespoke terms are primarily an OTC feature.

9. [Single choice] Central clearing mainly reduces:

a. All market losses. b. Bilateral counterparty exposure through the clearing mechanism. c. The need to post margin. d. Basis risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. A CCP becomes the buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, but market, margin, liquidity and clearing risks remain.

10. [Multiple response] OTC customisation can increase exposure to:

a. Counterparty risk. b. Liquidity and close-out risk. c. Model, valuation and documentation risk. d. Legal and operational risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. Customisation is valuable but less standardised and often less liquid.

11. [Single choice] Which post-crisis reform is consistent with the guide?

a. Ban all derivatives. b. Move suitable standardised OTC derivatives toward electronic/exchange trading, CCP clearing and trade reporting. c. Remove capital requirements for uncleared derivatives. d. Replace all OTC products with physical shares.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. These are examples of the G20 reform direction described in the source.

Chapter 2 — Futures, Forwards and Swaps

2.1 Introduction and 2.2 General features

16. [Single choice] Which is the clearest difference between a future and a forward?

a. Futures are usually standardised/exchange cleared; forwards are usually customised/OTC. b. Futures have no maturity; forwards always expire daily. c. Forwards are always safer because they are bilateral. d. Futures cannot be closed before maturity.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Futures also use daily MTM/margin, while forwards generally have bilateral credit exposure and less secondary liquidity.

17. [Multiple response] Futures contracts commonly specify:

a. Contract size/multiplier. b. Tick size and price limits. c. Trading hours and last trading day. d. Settlement and delivery terms.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. These define the tradable contract.

18. [Calculation] An E-mini contract is quoted at 5,000 and has a multiplier of 50. What is the notional value?

a. USD5,050. b. USD25,000. c. USD250,000. d. USD500,000.

Answer and explanation

Answer: c. 5,000 × 50 = USD250,000 per contract.

19. [Calculation] A Nikkei contract is at 22,500 with a JPY500 multiplier. Initial margin is 6.5%. What margin is required?

a. JPY73,125. b. JPY731,250. c. JPY1,462,500. d. JPY11,250,000.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Notional = JPY11,250,000; 11,250,000 × 6.5% = JPY731,250.

20. [Single choice] Daily mark-to-market means:

a. Gains/losses are settled periodically based on the contract’s changing market value. b. The investor pays the entire notional on day one. c. The underlying is always physically delivered daily. d. Margin is never adjusted.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Daily settlement creates cash-flow and margin-call risk.

a. Rises. b. Falls. c. Is unchanged. d. Becomes the spot equity index.

Answer: b. The quote is inversely related to the implied rate.

a. 0.175%. b. 1.75%. c. 98.25%. d. 101.75%.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. R = 100 − 98.25 = 1.75%.

a. 0.9989. b. 1.2487. c. 1.2557. d. 3.1743.

Answer: c. F = 1.2422 × (1.0070444)/(1.01799) ≈ 1.2557 under the guide’s quotation convention.

24. [Multiple response] A futures position can be resolved by:

a. Offset/close-out. b. Physical delivery where specified. c. Cash settlement where specified. d. Rolling into a later contract.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. The appropriate route depends on the contract and investor’s objective.

25. [Single choice] What does an SGX clearing house do?

a. Sets all market prices by itself. b. Clears/margins contracts and manages default processes as the central counterparty. c. Guarantees profitable trades. d. Removes the need to understand contract specifications.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Clearing reduces bilateral credit exposure but does not guarantee profit or eliminate market risk.

2.5 Pricing and 2.6 basis

26. [Calculation] Spot gold is 1,300, carry is 0.28% for the period and there is no income. Approximate fair futures value under F=S(1+C) is:

a. 1,296.36. b. 1,300.00. c. 1,303.64. d. 1,328.00.

Answer and explanation

Answer: c. 1,300 × 1.0028 = 1,303.64.

27. [Single choice] In a cash-and-carry arbitrage when futures is too high, the trader normally:

a. Buys/finances the spot asset and sells futures. b. Sells spot and sells futures. c. Buys both spot and futures. d. Does nothing because price differences are always risk-free.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The spot position carries the asset into delivery while the short futures locks the sale price.

28. [Multiple response] Basis can be affected by:

a. Carry, storage, insurance and convenience yield. b. Time to expiry and yield-curve changes. c. Liquidity and contract/asset mismatch. d. Administered versus market rates and expectations.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. Basis is not simply “the forecast price.”

29. [Single choice] Contango usually means:

a. Futures price is above spot. b. Futures price is below spot. c. Spot and futures are identical at every date. d. There is no carrying cost.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Backwardation is the opposite relationship, though the exact level also reflects income and convenience yield.

30. [Scenario] A hedge is closed two months before expiry and the asset being hedged is similar but not identical to the futures underlying. The main residual risk is:

a. Basis risk. b. Voting-rights risk only. c. No risk because futures are cleared. d. Deposit-insurance risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Mismatch and early close-out prevent perfect convergence.

2.7 Swaps and 2.8 summary

31. [Single choice] In a vanilla interest-rate swap, the notional is normally:

a. Exchanged physically at each reset. b. Used to calculate interest cash flows but not exchanged. c. Always converted into shares. d. The option premium only.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Fixed and floating interest amounts are netted/paid on the notional.

32. [Multiple response] Which swap descriptions are correct?

a. An FX swap has near and forward legs. b. A currency swap exchanges debt-service obligations in two currencies. c. An equity swap exchanges equity/index return for fixed or floating payments. d. A commodity swap can exchange fixed and floating commodity prices.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. These are distinct applications of OTC cash-flow exchange.

33. [Single choice] A CDS protection buyer generally:

a. Pays a spread and receives protection after a defined credit event. b. Receives a fixed dividend and gives voting rights. c. Buys physical gold. d. Has no exposure to the protection seller.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The protection seller’s ability to pay is itself a counterparty risk.

34. [Scenario] A borrower pays floating interest but wants fixed cash flows. The most direct swap action is to:

a. Pay fixed and receive floating. b. Pay floating and receive floating. c. Buy a put warrant. d. Sell a commodity future.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The received floating leg offsets the borrower’s floating liability.

35. [Multiple response] Why can a swap still be risky after it achieves the desired hedge?

a. Counterparty/default risk. b. Basis, reset and mismatch risk. c. Collateral, legal and documentation risk. d. Early termination/valuation and liquidity risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. Hedging reduces a targeted risk, not every risk.

Chapter 3 — Futures Strategies

3.1 Participants and 3.2 SGX orders

36. [Single choice] A portfolio manager sells index futures while continuing to hold the cash portfolio. The manager is acting primarily as a:

a. Hedger. b. Market maker. c. Arbitrageur with no cash exposure. d. Depositor.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The futures position offsets the portfolio’s market exposure.

37. [Multiple response] Which participants can be active in futures markets?

a. Hedgers. b. Speculators and hedge funds. c. Market makers and proprietary firms. d. Arbitrageurs, portfolio managers and official institutions.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. Different participants supply liquidity and transfer/accept risk.

38. [Single choice] A buy limit order at $10.00 when the ask is $10.02 will generally:

a. Execute immediately at $10.02. b. Rest in the queue at $10.00 or better until filled, amended or cancelled. c. Become a sell order. d. Guarantee execution at the close.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. A limit order controls price but not execution.

39. [Single choice] In an MTL buy order, any unfilled quantity normally:

a. Is cancelled immediately in all cases. b. Remains at the price where the filled portion executed, subject to the rules. c. Converts into an option. d. Is physically delivered.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. MTL provides market execution for the first fill and a limit-like resting balance.

40. [Multiple response] A stop order may be used to:

a. Cut a losing long position. b. Protect profit using a trailing stop. c. Enter after an upside breakout. d. Enter short after a downside breakdown.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. The trigger direction determines the resulting buy/sell order.

3.3 Outright trades and 3.4 spreads

41. [Single choice] Which is an outright futures trade?

a. Buy one contract and sell a related contract simultaneously. b. Buy one contract to express a directional view. c. Buy near month and sell deferred month. d. Buy one call and sell another call.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. The other choices are spread or options structures.

42. [Multiple response] Spread-trade risks include:

a. Correlation breakdown. b. One leg filling before the other. c. Liquidity and margin changes. d. The spread moving in the wrong direction.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. Lower spread margin does not mean no risk.

43. [Single choice] A calendar spread usually involves:

a. Same underlying with different delivery months. b. Different underlyings and same maturity only. c. A cash share and a bank deposit. d. Two unrelated currencies only.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. It is also called a horizontal/time spread.

44. [Scenario] An investor expects the near-month price to strengthen relative to the deferred-month price. The natural calendar-spread expression is:

a. Long near and short deferred. b. Short near and long deferred. c. Long both months. d. Short both months.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Profit depends on the near-minus-deferred spread widening.

45. [Single choice] A butterfly futures spread commonly has which weights across three maturities?

a. +1, −2, +1. b. +1, +1, +1. c. +1, −1 only. d. +2, +2, −1.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The structure targets curvature rather than a simple outright direction.

46. [Single choice] Compared with a butterfly, a condor normally has:

a. Four different maturities and a wider but lower peak profit zone. b. Two legs and unlimited loss. c. No sensitivity to volatility. d. Only one maturity.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. A condor uses four different delivery points.

47. [Single choice] The TED spread is associated with:

a. T-bill/Treasury versus Eurodollar/LIBOR pricing and credit/liquidity conditions. b. Gold versus silver storage only. c. Two equity dividends. d. A company’s ordinary share split.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. It is not a pure directionless interest-rate measure.

3.5 Hedging and portfolio management

48. [Single choice] The purpose of hedging is best described as:

a. Eliminate every possible loss. b. Exchange uncertain exposure for a more predictable outcome, usually leaving basis risk. c. Guarantee a higher return than speculation. d. Avoid all margin.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. A hedge targets a defined risk and may introduce other risks/costs.

49. [Multiple response] A hedge programme should specify:

a. Exposure and objective. b. Correlation/hedgeability and vehicle. c. Horizon, target ratio and roll/close-out. d. Monitoring, limits and residual risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. A hedge is a managed programme, not a single trade.

a. 10. b. 50. c. 100. d. 1,000.

Answer and explanation

Answer: c. 100m/1m = 100; a short position would normally hedge a long cash exposure.

51. [Calculation] A portfolio is SGD5.1025m, the futures quote is 4,100, value per point is SGD50 and beta is 1.2. Approximate contracts using N=VP/(F×T)×β.

a. 15. b. 30. c. 60. d. 120.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. 5,102,500/(4,100×50)×1.2 ≈ 29.77, rounded to about 30.

52. [Single choice] A long-term bond hedge should primarily compare:

a. Face value only. b. PVBP/DV01 and the conversion factor of the deliverable futures bond. c. The issuer’s dividend yield. d. Equity beta only.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Dollar interest-rate sensitivity is the relevant exposure.

53. [Multiple response] Futures can support portfolio techniques such as:

a. Asset allocation and rebalancing. b. Synthetic exposure and cash equitisation. c. Duration or equity overlay hedging. d. Temporary market exposure while cash is invested.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. The efficiencies come with basis, roll, margin and tracking risks.

3.6 Portfolio techniques and 3.7 arbitrage

54. [Single choice] Cash-and-futures arbitrage is most credible when:

a. The apparent spread exceeds funding, trading, storage, margin and delivery costs. b. The spread is smaller than all transaction costs. c. The asset cannot be bought or borrowed. d. There is no way to close either leg.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The theoretical arbitrage must survive real-world costs and constraints.

55. [Multiple response] Why might an apparent futures arbitrage not be risk-free?

a. Funding/borrow constraints. b. Basis and value-date mismatch. c. Liquidity, bid–ask and margin. d. Delivery options or counterparty issues.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. The textbook cash-and-carry assumptions are often violated.

56. [Scenario] A trader buys the cash asset, sells an overpriced future and plans to deliver at maturity. The position is a:

a. Cash-and-carry arbitrage. b. Reverse cash-and-carry. c. Bull put spread. d. Covered call.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The physical/cash asset is carried against a futures sale.

57. [Single choice] A strip differs from a stack because a strip generally:

a. Uses sequential futures delivery months corresponding to the exposure periods. b. Uses only the nearest contract and never rolls. c. Is always an option. d. Has no basis risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. A stack concentrates in one/deferred contract and may be rolled to match a loan’s reset dates.

58. [Scenario] A company has a floating-rate loan and expects rates to rise. A short Eurodollar/short-rate futures hedge aims to:

a. Gain as futures prices fall when rates rise, offsetting higher loan interest. b. Increase its floating-rate exposure. c. Receive dividends. d. Remove all credit risk from the borrower.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The hedge offsets rate sensitivity, subject to correlation and basis.

59. [Multiple response] A 130/30 equity strategy means:

a. Long 130% of portfolio value. b. Short 30%. c. Net 100% equity exposure before other effects. d. No leverage or short-sale risk.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b and c. It still carries leverage, borrow, stock-selection and market risks.

60. [Single choice] A player who sells contracts while holding the underlying stock is most likely:

a. A hedger. b. A market maker with no risk. c. A depositor. d. A call-option buyer.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The futures sale reduces cash-market exposure.

Chapter 4 — Options

4.1–4.2 Basics and definitions

61. [Single choice] The buyer of a call option has:

a. The obligation to buy. b. The right, but not the obligation, to buy at the strike. c. The right to sell only. d. No premium obligation.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. The buyer pays premium for optionality; the writer has the contingent obligation.

62. [Single choice] An American option may be exercised:

a. Only at expiry. b. Any time up to expiry, subject to its terms. c. Only if it is OTM. d. Only by the writer.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. European exercise is expiry-only.

63. [Calculation] A call has strike $50, underlying $54 and premium $6. Its intrinsic value and time value are:

a. $6 and $0. b. $4 and $2. c. $50 and $4. d. $54 and $6.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Call intrinsic = 54−50=$4; time value = $6−$4=$2.

64. [Multiple response] For a long call, which changes generally increase value, all else equal?

a. Higher underlying price. b. Higher volatility. c. More time to expiry. d. Higher dividends.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b and c. Higher dividends generally reduce call value.

4.3–4.6 Payoff, factors and parity

65. [Calculation] A long call has strike $100 and premium $7. What is breakeven at expiry?

a. $93. b. $100. c. $107. d. Unlimited.

Answer and explanation

Answer: c. Breakeven = X + premium = $107.

66. [Single choice] Maximum loss for a long put is normally:

a. Unlimited. b. The premium paid. c. The strike plus premium. d. Zero only if the stock rises.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. The put may expire worthless, but the holder’s loss is limited to premium.

67. [Single choice] A naked short call has:

a. Unlimited theoretical loss if the underlying rises sharply. b. No obligation. c. A guaranteed maximum loss equal to premium. d. A right to buy the underlying.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The writer must deliver/satisfy the call if exercised.

68. [Single choice] Put-call parity for European options is:

a. C + PV(X) = P + S. b. C + P = S − PV(X). c. C − PV(X) = P + S. d. C + S = P − PV(X).

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Same underlying, strike, expiry, quantity and consistent dividend assumptions are required.

69. [Multiple response] Under parity, a synthetic long stock can be created by:

a. Long call and short put, with the same strike/expiry. b. Long put and short call only. c. Two long calls with different strikes. d. A bond alone.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Long call − short put plus the parity bond/financing relationship reproduces the stock cash flows.

70. [Scenario] A trader buys a call and a put at the same strike and expiry, expecting a large move but no direction. This is a:

a. Long straddle. b. Short strangle. c. Bull put spread. d. Covered call.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. A straddle is long call and long put at the same strike.

4.7–4.10 Valuation, Greeks and other underlyings

72. [Single choice] Gamma is often largest when an option is:

a. Deep ITM and years from expiry. b. Near ATM and close to expiry. c. Certain to expire worthless immediately. d. A zero-coupon bond.

Answer and explanation

Answer: b. Delta changes rapidly near the strike as expiry approaches.

73. [Single choice] Vega is generally positive for:

a. Long calls and long puts. b. Short calls only. c. Long stock only. d. Short straddles only.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. Higher implied volatility generally increases the value of long options.

74. [Multiple response] Equity-index option analysis should consider:

a. Cash settlement and multiplier. b. Index composition and liquidity. c. Basis/correlation with a portfolio. d. OTC counterparty risk where applicable.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b, c and d. An index is not automatically a perfect hedge.

75. [Single choice] A payer swaption generally benefits its buyer when:

a. Rates rise above the fixed strike. b. Rates fall to zero only. c. Equity dividends rise. d. A commodity is delivered.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The buyer gains the right to enter a pay-fixed/receive-floating swap at the strike.

4.11–4.14 Strategies and hedging

76. [Multiple response] A bull call spread normally consists of:

a. Long lower-strike call. b. Short higher-strike call. c. Same expiry/underlying. d. Unlimited loss in all cases.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b and c. The short call caps gain and helps fund the debit; loss is generally limited to net premium.

77. [Single choice] A bear put spread profits primarily when:

a. The underlying falls. b. Volatility is always zero. c. The underlying rises without limit. d. Interest rates fall only.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. It is a debit bearish structure with defined risk and capped profit.

78. [Single choice] A long strangle is generally cheaper than a long straddle because:

a. It uses OTM options at different strikes and needs a larger move. b. It has no premium. c. It is always risk-free. d. It contains only one option.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. The trade-off is lower initial cost for wider breakevens.

79. [Scenario] An investor owns shares and wants to floor downside but retain upside above the strike. The most direct option hedge is:

a. Buy a protective put. b. Sell a naked put. c. Buy a short call only. d. Sell the shares and buy a bond.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a. A protective put is long stock plus long put.

80. [Multiple response] A zero-cost collar normally involves:

a. Buying a put. b. Selling an OTM call. c. Premiums approximately offsetting. d. Unlimited upside after the call strike.

Answer and explanation

Answer: a, b and c. The sold call limits upside; the structure is not free of risk.

88. [Multiple response] Corporate actions may cause a warrant issuer to adjust:
