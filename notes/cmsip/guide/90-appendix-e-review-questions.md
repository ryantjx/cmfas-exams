# Appendix E — Review Questions

Candidates should note that the **sole purpose of the Review Questions is to familiarise candidates with the scope and general nature of the examinations, and the format of the examination questions**. They are **not intended as preparatory study material**, and **do not cover all the material tested**. The correct answer for each question is in **bold** in the source booklet and is reproduced as **Answer** below.

## Chapter 2 — Futures

**1.** There are certain risks involved in hedging in the forward or futures market. Which of the following is TRUE?

- **a.** **Futures contracts have lower counterparty risk, while forward contracts carry the credit risk of the counterparty.**
- **b.** Futures contracts have basis risk, whereas forward contracts do not.
- **c.** Forward contracts have the risk of the exchange.
- **d.** Forward contracts have interim partial settlements.

**Answer: a.**

**2.** Advantages of futures contracts over OTC instruments include:

- **a.** More transparency in pricing.
- **b.** Market liquidity.
- **c.** Reduced credit risk.
- **d.** Increased credit risk.

Options: (a) and (c) · (b) and (c) · **(a), (b) and (c)** · (a), (b) and (d)

**Answer: (a), (b) and (c).**

**3.** What is the economic importance and impact of the futures market?

- **a.** Futures are not standardised contracts and therefore not readily available.
- **b.** The futures market is both a less active and less important source of vital market information.
- **c.** **Participants can use contracts to leverage on long or short positions.**
- **d.** Participants cannot use contracts to leverage on long positions.

**Answer: c.**

**4.** Spot GBP/USD is trading at **1.2422**. Given annualised US interest rates at **1.799%** and annualised British interest rates at **0.70444%**, what should be the theoretical price for a 1-year forward contract?

- **a.** **1.2557.**
- **b.** 1.2487.
- **c.** 3.1743.
- **d.** 0.9989.

**Answer: a.** — By interest rate parity, $$F = S \times \frac{1 + r_{USD}}{1 + r_{GBP}} = 1.2422 \times \frac{1.01799}{1.0070444} \approx 1.2557$$.

**5.** What are the factors affecting basis in the market of a futures contract?

- **b.** Non-difference in coupons between fixed income instruments.
- **c.** Market rates versus administrated rates.
- **d.** Yield curve changes.

Options: (a) and (d) · (a), (c) and (d) · (a), (b) and (c) · (b) and (c)

**Answer: (a), (c) and (d).**

**8.** A local company wishes to take a **6-month USD 50 million** loan; the prevailing 6-month USD lending rate is **2.15%**. The September Eurodollar futures contract is trading at **98.15**; at the time of entering the hedge it was **98.65**. The Eurodollar futures contract size is **$1,000,000**, each representing a **3-month LIBOR period (0.25 years)**. Assuming the loan rate is correlated one-to-one with the Eurodollar rate and the company intends to fully hedge using September futures, calculate the number of contracts needed.

- **a.** 25
- **b.** 50
- **c.** **100**
- **d.** 150

**Answer: c.** — Contracts $$= \frac{\text{loan}}{\text{contract size}} \times \frac{\text{exposure period}}{\text{contract period}} = \frac{50{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} \times \frac{0.5}{0.25} = 100$$.

**9.** Calculate the hedge ratio required to hedge the interest rate on a **6-month loan for USD 100 million**. Assume the loan rate is correlated one-to-one with the Eurodollar rate of the nearest 90-day contract, and that a **stack hedge** is employed. Contract size **$1,000,000**, each representing a **3-month LIBOR period (0.25 years)**.

- **a.** 200
- **b.** 20
- **c.** **2**
- **d.** 0.2

**Answer: c.** — In a **stack** hedge the whole exposure is covered by contracts of the nearest expiry, so the contract count is $$\frac{100{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} \times \frac{0.5}{0.25} = 200$$ contracts — but expressed against the **notional of the stack** the hedge ratio is **2**, the ratio of exposure tenor to contract tenor.

**10.** A player in the financial markets who sells contracts to manage his cash market exposure while holding the underlying stock is a/an:

- **a.** Arbitrageur.
- **b.** **Hedger.**
- **c.** Market maker.
- **d.** Speculator.

**Answer: b.**

## Chapter 4 — Options

**11.** There are positive relationships between the value of a call option and ______.

- **a.** Interest rates.
- **b.** Time to expiration of the call option.
- **c.** Dividend rate of the underlying stock.
- **d.** Volatility of the underlying stock.

Options: (a) and (c) · (a) and (b) · (a), (c) and (d) · **(a), (b) and (d)**

**Answer: (a), (b) and (d).** — A higher **dividend rate reduces** call value, so (c) is excluded.

**12.** The exercise price of a call option is **$50**. The price of the call is **$6**, and it expires in 3 months. The current share price is **$54**. What is the intrinsic value?

- **a.** $50.
- **b.** **$4.**
- **c.** $54.
- **d.** $6.

**Answer: b.** — Intrinsic value $$= \max(S - K, 0) = 54 - 50 = 4$$. The remaining **$2** of the **$6** premium is time value.

**13.** Which of the following statements is TRUE about Theta and Vega?

- **b.** Vega measures the change in asset price with respect to time, while Theta measures the volatility of the option's portfolio.
- **c.** Vega is positive for long calls and short puts.
- **d.** **Vega is positive for a long volatility investment position with call or put options.**

**Answer: d.**

**17.** The warrant premium is affected by:

- **a.** The length of time before expiration date.
- **b.** The size of the cash dividends paid on the underlying share.
- **c.** The intrinsic value of the warrant.
- **d.** The credibility of the individual buyer.

Options: (a), (b) and (d) · **(a), (b) and (c)** · (b), (c) and (d) · (a), (b), (c) and (d)

**Answer: (a), (b) and (c).**

**18.** A call warrant with an exercise price of **$1.00** is trading at **$1.20**. One warrant converts to one share. What is its conversion price?

- **a.** $1.00.
- **b.** $1.20.
- **c.** **$2.20.**
- **d.** $0.20.

**Answer: c.** — Conversion price $$= \text{exercise price} + \text{warrant price} = 1.00 + 1.20 = 2.20$$ for a **1:1** conversion ratio.

**19.** The warrant is trading at **$0.40**, the exercise price is **$0.80**, and the share price is **$2.40**. What is its gearing? (Assume a company-issued warrant with a conversion ratio of 1.)

- **a.** 2X.
- **b.** **6X.**
- **c.** 3X.
- **d.** 5X.

**Answer: b.** — Gearing $$= \frac{\text{share price}}{\text{warrant price}} = \frac{2.40}{0.40} = 6$$X (with a 1:1 ratio).

**20.** Company A's warrant is trading at **$0.35**, its exercise price is **$0.50**, and the share price is **$0.65**. What is its intrinsic value?

- **a.** $0.80.
- **b.** **$0.15.**
- **c.** $0.30.
- **d.** $1.00.

**Answer: b.** — Intrinsic value $$= \max(S - K, 0) = 0.65 - 0.50 = 0.15$$.

**21.** The market price of Company B's common share is **$5** and the exercise price of the warrant is **$3.50**. **Two warrants** convert into **one** common share. The warrant price is currently **$2**. What is the warrant premium?

- **a.** 25%.
- **b.** 30%.
- **c.** 50%.
- **d.** 100%.

**Answer: 50%.** — Because **two warrants** are needed per share, the warrant outlay per share is $$2 \times 2 = 4$$. Premium $$= \frac{\text{warrant outlay} + \text{exercise price} - \text{share price}}{\text{share price}} = \frac{4 + 3.50 - 5}{5} = \frac{2.50}{5} = 50\%$$. The examinable point is that a **non-unit conversion ratio must be applied to the warrant price** before computing the premium.

## Chapter 6 — Barrier Options, Binary Options and Callable Contracts

**22.** For a single "knock-out", up-and-out call barrier option, the barrier level is:

- **a.** Below the current spot price of the asset.
- **b.** Equal to the current spot price of the asset.
- **c.** Equal to the strike price.
- **d.** **Above the current spot price of the asset.**

**Answer: d.** — An **up-and-out** call is knocked out when the price rises to the barrier, so the barrier must sit **above** spot.

**23.** Which of the following statements are CORRECT?

- **a.** For a Category-N callable bull/bear contract (CBBC), there is no residual value when a mandatory call event occurs.
- **b.** For a Category-R CBBC, there is always a positive residual value when a mandatory call event occurs.
- **c.** The Category-N CBBC call price is always equal to the strike price.
- **d.** The Category-R CBBC call price is always different from the strike price.

Options: **(a), (c) and (d)** · (b), (c) and (d) · (a), (b) and (c) · (a), (b) and (d)

**Answer: (a), (c) and (d).** — A Category-N CBBC has **no residual value** on a mandatory call event, and its call price **equals** the strike; a Category-R CBBC has a call price **different** from the strike so that a residual value remains.

**24.** In a double "knock-out" barrier option, the barrier levels ______.

- **d.** **Will vary, as there will be one barrier level above the strike price and another barrier level below the strike price.**

**Answer: d.**

## What these questions test

The set is a **format and scope orientation**, not a syllabus. Read against the chapters, it reveals where the examination puts its weight.

**Calculation discipline.** Six questions require a number, and each uses a single formula from Appendix D: **interest rate parity** (Q4), the **contract-count hedge** (Q8, Q9), **intrinsic value** (Q12, Q20), **conversion price** (Q18), **gearing** (Q19), **warrant premium with a non-unit conversion ratio** (Q21) and **participation rate** (Q31). Q21 is the trap — a **2:1** conversion ratio doubles the effective conversion cost before the premium is computed.

**Sign and direction conventions.** Q11 excludes the dividend rate because a higher dividend **reduces** call value; Q22 requires the barrier of an **up-and-out** call to sit **above** spot; Q16 identifies **volatility** as the one factor that raises both call and put value.

**Product boundaries.** Q1, Q25, Q42 and Q45 turn on distinguishing products that look alike: futures versus forwards (counterparty risk and interim settlement), CBBC versus structured warrants (the **call price**), CFDs versus equity futures (OTC versus exchange, implicit versus explicit financing, dividend entitlement), and the set of instruments that let an investor profit from a falling price.

**Structural facts worth memorising.** Q8 and Q9 both use the same hedge formula — $$\frac{\text{notional}}{\text{contract size}} \times \frac{\text{exposure tenor}}{\text{contract tenor}}$$ — applied to a **strip** and a **stack** respectively. Q26 separates the **disadvantages** of barrier options from their advantages: the premium-loss risk on breach and **issuer counterparty risk** on OTC products.

The expanded question bank in `notes/cmsip/CMSIP_QUESTIONS.md` covers these themes and adds application questions beyond the orientation set.
